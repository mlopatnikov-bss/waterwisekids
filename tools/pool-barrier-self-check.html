Pool Barrier Self-Check

Walk your fence, your gate and your back doors through the federal barrier guidelines in about four minutes. You will get a printable punch list of what to fix, ordered by how much each item matters.

How it works: Fourteen questions, each drawn from the CPSC Safety Barrier Guidelines for Residential Pools. Nothing you enter leaves your device — the result is generated in your browser. Bring a tape measure; most of these are measurements, not judgment calls.

Read this first. CPSC states plainly that the barrier guidelines are not a CPSC standard, nor are they mandatory requirements. Many states and localities have adopted them into their building codes, and many have amended them. This tool produces a safety punch list, not a compliance certificate. Before you build or alter anything, confirm the enforceable requirement with your building department — our guide to looking up your local pool fence code shows how. And no barrier replaces supervision.
1. The barrier itself

Does the barrier completely surround the pool on all four sides, separating it from the house as well as from the yard?

A fence that uses the house as its fourth side leaves every door a way in. Four-sided isolation fencing is the configuration associated with roughly an 83% reduction in a young child's drowning risk.

Measured from the side facing away from the pool, is the top of the barrier at least 48 inches above the ground everywhere along its run?

Measure at the lowest point of ground, not the highest. A slope, a raised planter or a retaining wall can quietly cost you six inches.

What is the largest gap between the ground and the bottom of the barrier?

Measured on the side facing away from the pool. The guideline is 4 inches over a solid surface, reduced to 2 inches over grass or gravel because a child can dig, and 1 inch for removable mesh.

Is every opening in the barrier small enough that a 4-inch-diameter sphere cannot pass through it?

The 4 inches comes from the head breadth and chest depth of a young child. A rigid 4-inch ball, or a 4-inch cardboard disc, tests this faster than a tape measure — try it at the widest-looking gap, and at the gap beside the gate post.

What is the barrier made of?

The spacing rule that governs your fence depends on how it is built. Pick the closest match and the result will name the figure for it.

On a fence with horizontal rails: is the distance between the top edges of the rails 45 inches or more?

This single measurement decides your spacing rule. At 45 inches or more the rails are too far apart to climb, and vertical members may be up to 4 inches apart. Under 45 inches, vertical spacing drops to 1¾ inches and the rails must move to the pool side.

Is the barrier clear of handholds, footholds and climbable objects — including anything on the outside of it?

Decorative scrollwork, protruding bolts, a lattice panel, an air-conditioning unit, a woodpile, a planter, a wheelie bin, a trampoline. The guidelines say barriers should be located so that permanent structures and equipment cannot be used to climb them.

2. The gate

Does the gate self-close and self-latch from any starting position, every single time?

Test it three ways: released from wide open, from halfway, and from an inch ajar. The third one is where tired hinges fail. A gate that closes but does not latch is an open gate.

Does the gate open outward, away from the pool?

An outward-swinging gate means a child pushing to get in is pushing the gate closed. It also means a crowd leaving the pool cannot force it open against the latch.

Measuring from the bottom of the gate, how high is the latch release?

From the bottom of the gate, not from the ground — that distinction is what most homeowners get wrong. At 54 inches or above, the height is the protection.

If the release is lower than 54 inches: is it mounted on the pool side, at least 3 inches below the top of the gate, with no opening larger than ½ inch anywhere within 18 inches of it?

Both conditions have to hold. The 3-inch recess stops a child reaching over the top; the ½-inch rule stops a hand or a stick coming through to flip it.

3. The house and other access

If a wall of the house forms part of the barrier, is every door with direct pool access alarmed, or is the pool fitted with a power safety cover meeting ASTM F1346-91?

The guidelines ask for one of these when the house is part of the barrier. Door alarms have a specification of their own: sound within 7 seconds, lasting 30 seconds or more, at least 85 decibels measured 10 feet away, distinct from the doorbell and the smoke alarm, with an adult bypass at least 54 inches up that silences it for no more than 15 seconds.

Is there a pet door on any wall or door that leads toward the pool?

Children have drowned after going through pet doors that adults never thought of as doors. Locking one is not considered sufficient, and some municipalities prohibit them outright in homes with pools.

For an above-ground pool: can the ladder or steps be secured, locked or removed — and if a barrier is mounted on top of the pool wall, is the gap under it 4 inches or less?

On an above-ground pool the ladder is the gate. Once it is removed, any opening left behind should still not pass a 4-inch sphere.

How should I read the result?

The list comes back in three bands. Fix first holds the failures that leave a child a direct route to the water — a missing side of the barrier, a gate that does not latch, an unprotected house door. Fix soon holds the ones that shorten how long a barrier delays a determined child: a climbable rail, an oversized opening, a gap at the bottom. Verify holds anything you answered "not measured," because an unmeasured barrier is an unknown barrier, and the guidelines are written in inches for a reason.

Almost every item on the list is cheaper than people expect. A self-closing hinge and a magnetic latch cost less than a single swim lesson. Slats for a chain link fence are sold by the roll. The expensive item on the list is usually the missing fourth side, and that is also the one that does the most work.

Why does the barrier carry so much weight?

Because it is the only layer that works when nobody is watching. Supervision is the strongest protection when it is switched on; a barrier is the protection that stays on during the gap between "he was right here" and "where is he." CPSC's own framing is that barriers are not childproof — they buy a parent time to notice. Four-sided isolation fencing, the kind that separates the pool from the house rather than the yard from the street, is associated with roughly an 83% reduction in a young child's pool drowning risk.

The corollary is that the weakest point sets the standard. A 60-inch fence with a gate that sticks open is a 0-inch fence. That is why the gate questions above are weighted the way they are, and why the guidelines devote more text to the latch than to the fence.

What should I do after the checklist?

Three things, in order. Fix whatever landed in the first band this week. Then confirm your local requirement — the guidelines are national and advisory, your building code is local and enforceable, and where they differ the local code wins. Then put the layer that barriers cannot provide back in place: a child who can swim, and an adult who is watching.

Frequently asked questions

How tall does a pool fence have to be?

The CPSC Safety Barrier Guidelines put the top of the barrier at least 48 inches above grade, measured on the side facing away from the pool. Fences of 60 inches are preferable and some states, counties and municipalities require them. Height alone is not enough: the same guidelines limit the bottom clearance to 4 inches, and no opening anywhere in the barrier should pass a 4-inch-diameter sphere.

How high does a pool gate latch need to be?

CPSC measures the latch release from the bottom of the gate. At 54 inches or higher, the height alone is the protection. Where the release sits lower than 54 inches, two conditions apply instead: it must be mounted on the pool side of the gate at least 3 inches below the top, and the gate and barrier must have no opening larger than half an inch within 18 inches of the release, so a child cannot reach over or through it.

Does this tool tell me whether my pool fence is legal?

No. It checks your barrier against the CPSC Safety Barrier Guidelines for Residential Pools, which CPSC states are guidelines rather than a mandatory federal standard. Many states and localities have adopted them into building codes, but the rule enforced at your address is your local code, and local amendments are common. Use the result as a punch list, then confirm the enforceable requirement with your building department.

Is a pool cover or alarm a substitute for a fence?

No. Barriers, covers and alarms are layers, and four-sided isolation fencing that separates the pool from the house is the layer associated with roughly an 83 percent reduction in a young child's drowning risk. Door alarms and a power safety cover meeting ASTM F1346-91 are what the guidelines ask for when a wall of the house forms part of the barrier, not a way to skip the barrier.

Do above-ground pools need a fence?

The guidelines apply to above-ground pools too. The pool structure itself can serve as the barrier, or a barrier can be mounted on top of it, in which case the vertical clearance between the top of the pool structure and the bottom of the barrier should not exceed 4 inches. Where a ladder or steps are the means of access, they must be securable, lockable or removable, and any opening left once they are removed should not pass a 4-inch sphere.

Sources

  • CDC — Drowning Facts: four-sided isolation fencing and the reduction in a young child's pool drowning risk.
  • American Academy of Pediatrics: fencing as the primary barrier, with covers, alarms and supervision as supplemental layers.

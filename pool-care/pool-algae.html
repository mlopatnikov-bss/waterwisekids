Pool Algae: Recognizing It, Clearing It and Keeping It Away

Recognize green, mustard and black algae, brush and treat by the label, use your filter, and keep algae from coming back in a home pool.

  • Guides
  • Pool stores
  • Service companies

Applies to: In-ground and above-ground home pools with vinyl, fiberglass or plaster surfaces, using chlorine (including salt systems). Follow your product labels and equipment manuals.

Quick answer

Algae is tiny plant growth that can turn water green and cloudy and leave dark green or black spots on the pool shell. Guidance written for public pools and spas lists remedies including superchlorinating, brushing the spots and using an approved algaecide. Algaecides are pesticides, and their labels are legally enforceable, so follow the label exactly.[1][2][3]

Pool upkeep is not drowning prevention.Keep the fence, self-latching gate, alarms and close adult supervision in place, and keep children away from chemicals and equipment while you work. See backyard pool safety layers.

How to recognize algae

Algae is tiny plant growth that may feel slimy and can look green, brown or black. Signs include green, cloudy water and dark green or black spots on the pool surface.[1]

Three kinds are common:[4]

  • Green algae: the most widespread and the easiest to eliminate.[4]
  • Yellow or mustard algae: forms spots on the walls and floor.[4]
  • Black algae: the most stubborn and difficult to get rid of.[4]

Check corners and fixtures too. Algae can gather on walls and on ladders and slides.[5]

Why algae grows

  • Not enough chlorine, and skipped routine maintenance such as testing and shock treatment.[1]
  • Disinfectant at the wrong level, or not enough filtration.[4]
  • High pH or incorrect water hardness, which reduces how well treatment products work.[4]
  • Warm water. Algae thrive in it.[4]
  • Plant debris and biofilm left in a poorly cleaned pool.[4]
  • Plaster that is etched or cracked.[1]

Act early. The longer you wait, the more algae spreads and the longer treatment takes.[4]

Safety first

SafetyKeep everyone out of green or cloudy water. Before getting in, make sure you can see the drain at the bottom of the deep end.[6]

SafetyTreating algae means handling pool chemicals, and mishandling them can cause serious injuries. Read and follow all label directions, wear goggles and gloves, and keep chemicals away from children and pets. Never mix chlorine products with acid, which could create toxic gases, or mix different pool chemicals together. If a label says to pre-dissolve, add the chemical to water, never water to the chemical.[7][8][9]

SafetyIf someone is exposed to a pool chemical, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. It is available 24/7 in over 100 languages.[10]

Clearing algae: the general sequence

Guidance written for public pools lists superchlorinating, brushing spots with an algae brush and using an approved algaecide among its remedies for algae. The steps below combine those with manufacturer guidance. How much of any product to use, and when, comes from its label and your pool's volume, not from this guide.[1]

  1. Test chlorine and pH. A pH that is too high or too low makes chlorine less effective. Aim for pH 7.0 to 7.8 and at least 1 ppm chlorine in pools, or at least 2 ppm if you use cyanuric acid (stabilizer) or stabilized chlorine products.[7][11]
  2. Brush. Scrub spots with an algae brush; brushing helps detach algae spores. Hayward TigerShark owners get the same advice for algae on pool walls: brush the walls and chemically treat for algae.[1][4][12]
  3. Superchlorinate (shock) by the label, following the manufacturer's directions for shock treatments.[1][5]
  4. Use an approved algaecide only if its label covers your situation.[1]
  5. Vacuum up the brushed-off algae and sediment, and clean and maintain your filter according to its manufacturer.[5]
  6. Retest, and don't swim until the drain at the deep end is clearly visible.[6][11]

Hold off on automatic cleaners during the worst of it. Do not use a Polaris 280 cleaner during shock chlorination. With a Hayward TigerShark, when pool condition is unacceptable for the cleaner, sweep walls, vacuum and balance the chemicals first.[12][13]

Mind your surface. Some patterned vinyl liners can be scratched or worn by a pool brush. Check your liner or pool builder's care instructions before scrubbing hard.[13]

What algaecide labels mean

Algaecides are a type of pesticide, used to kill or slow the growth of algae. With limited exceptions, a pesticide must be registered with EPA before it can be legally sold or distributed in the United States.[2][14]

Pesticide labels are legally enforceable: "the label is the law." Every label carries a statement that using the product in a way inconsistent with its labeling violates federal law.[3][15]

Labels tell you how to use a product safely, give first aid instructions, and list phone numbers for help.[16]

Preventing algae

  • Keep sanitizer up. Pool sanitizers kill bacteria and inhibit the growth of algae, and pool chemicals can protect against algal growth.[17][18]
  • Test regularly: check chlorine and pH at least twice a day.[11]
  • Brush walls and fixtures weekly and skim often.[5]
  • Keep the filter clean and maintained according to its manufacturer.[5]

A neglected green pool is also a mosquito problem. Mosquitoes that can carry West Nile virus and other diseases breed in green backyard pools, in as little as a quarter inch of standing water. In San Diego County, the vector control program asks residents to report green pools they notice; check with your own county.[17]

When to call a professional

When to call a professionalEtched or cracked plaster can lead to algae; guidance written for public pools lists refinishing the pool surface as a remedy. Polaris 280 owners: if a vinyl liner is damaged, do not install the cleaner before the necessary repairs are made or the liner is replaced by a qualified professional. If the Polaris 280 malfunctions, do not repair it yourself; contact a qualified technician.[1][13]

Common questions

Is it safe to swim in a green pool?
No. Keep everyone out until the water is clear and you can see the drain at the bottom of the deep end.[6]
Do I have to use an algaecide?
Guidance written for public pools lists an approved algaecide as one remedy, alongside superchlorinating and brushing. If you use one, the label is the law.[1][3]
Which algae is hardest to remove?
Black algae is the most stubborn and difficult to eradicate. Green algae is the easiest to eliminate.[4]

Pool Chlorine and Stabilizer, Explained for Families

How chlorine keeps a home pool clean, free vs combined chlorine, the stabilizer trade-off, shocking by the label, and safe chemical storage.

  • Guides
  • Pool stores
  • Service companies

Applies to: In-ground and above-ground home pools sanitized with chlorine, including tablet, granular, liquid and salt-generated chlorine. Indoor pools and hot tubs are noted where sources treat them differently.

Quick answer

Chlorine is the main germ-killer in most home pools. At recommended levels it kills most germs within minutes, and pH affects how well it works. Stabilizer (cyanuric acid) protects chlorine from sunlight but also slows it down. The product label tells you how much to use and how to handle it safely.[1][2][3]

Pool upkeep is not drowning prevention.Keep the fence, self-latching gate, alarms and close adult supervision in place, and keep children away from chemicals and equipment while you work. See backyard pool safety layers.

What chlorine does in your pool

Disinfection with chlorine or bromine, together with pH, is the first defense against germs that cause swimming-related illness. At the recommended levels, chlorine can kill most germs within minutes.[1]

Check chlorine and pH at least twice a day, and more often when the pool is busy. Keep free chlorine at least 1 ppm in pools, or at least 2 ppm if you use cyanuric acid (stabilizer) or chlorine products that contain it, with pH at 7.0–7.8. Follow your product's instructions; the typical range is 1–4 ppm.[1][4]

pH matters as much as chlorine. When pH is too high or too low, chlorine works less well, and the water can irritate skin and eyes and damage pool equipment.[1]

Common forms of chlorine

Chlorine is sold as pucks, tablets or liquid, or made on site by a device that creates chlorine from salt.[3]

  • Liquid: sodium hypochlorite (bleach). Spilled liquid bleach can leak into other containers or seep into cracks in the floor, and liquids can create hazards not seen with solid or granular products.[5][6]
  • Tablets and sticks: trichlor is often sold as tablets or sticks for an erosion feeder.[2]
  • Granular: dichlor usually comes as granules marketed for home pools. Calcium hypochlorite also comes in solid and granular forms.[2][5]
  • Salt: a salt chlorine generator still makes chlorine. See our salt water pools guide.[3]

Trichlor and dichlor contain stabilizer as well as chlorine, so pools using them do not need stabilizer added separately.[2]

Trichlor is acidic and slow to dissolve. On a vinyl liner, it can bleach the spot where it rests.[7]

Free chlorine and combined chlorine

Free chlorine is the sanitizer that is available to work, also called "free available chlorine".[3]

When chlorine mixes with sweat, dirt, pee and poop, less chlorine is left to kill germs, and irritants called chloramines form. Chloramines are a type of combined chlorine. A strong "chlorine" smell usually means chloramines.[1][8][9]

Guidance for public pool operators tracks chloramines, a type of combined chlorine, by measuring both free chlorine and total chlorine.[9]

Prevention is simple. Keeping sweat, dirt and pee out of the water helps stop chloramines forming, and a one-minute rinse removes most dirt.[8]

Stabilizer (cyanuric acid): the trade-off

Cyanuric acid is a chlorine stabilizer. It shields chlorine from the sun's ultraviolet rays, so less chlorine is lost.[2]

The downside: it reduces chlorine's overall effectiveness, and bacteria take longer to kill as the stabilizer level rises. Disinfection is slower whenever cyanuric acid is present.[2][10]

Stabilizer is never used up. The best way to lower it is to partially drain and add fresh water. Some pool types should not be drained, so check your pool maker's guidance first; see our pool surfaces guide.[2][11][12]

Stabilizer targets vary by source. With Pentair's IntelliChlor system, keep stabilizer at 30–50 ppm. Montana's rules for public pools set 50 ppm as the maximum. Follow your own equipment manual and local rules.[2][13]

Indoor pools and hot tubs are different. Indoors, stabilizer brings no benefit, and Montana's state guidance does not recommend it in hot tubs or spas. With Pentair's IntelliChlor system, do not use it in indoor pools.[2][13]

Shocking (superchlorination): follow the label

Guidance for public pool operators uses superchlorination to clear chloramines, with swimmers out of the pool and good ventilation. In backyard pools, raising chlorine very high for hours increases the risk of chemical injuries.[9][10]

Some equipment has its own shock mode. Hayward AquaRite units, for example, have a Super Chlorinate setting for heavy use or heavy rain. Use your own manual.[14]

  1. Read the whole label before you start. It states how much to use for your pool and how to handle the product.[1][3]
  2. Put on goggles and gloves.[1][15]
  3. Keep children and pets away from the chemicals while you work.[1]
  4. Add the product the way the label says. Pre-dissolve only if the label tells you to, and then add the chemical to water, never water to the chemical.[15]
  5. Keep water circulating and brush afterward. On vinyl liners, bleached spots are linked to dead spots, a pump turned off for too long, and too little brushing after shocking.[7]
  6. Test before anyone swims. Wait until chlorine is back within your product's instructions and the recommended levels: at least 1 ppm, or at least 2 ppm with stabilizer, and typically 1–4 ppm. Checking regularly is the only true way to know your chlorine level, and it is common practice to stay out of the water after shocking.[1][4][7]

SafetyNever mix pool chemicals. Chlorine products mixed with acid can create toxic gases, and different types of chlorine products should never be mixed. Explosions have occurred when a pool user switched from one type of chlorine tablet in a chlorinator to a different type without thoroughly cleaning it.[5][15][16]

Storing chemicals safely at home

  • Lock chemicals up, away from children and pets.[1][16]
  • Keep them dry, cool, ventilated and out of direct sunlight. Guidance for pool operators sets storage below 95°F.[16][17]
  • Keep each product closed in its original, labeled container.[16]
  • Keep acid and chlorine apart, and store liquids low, below powders or solids.[3][16]
  • Store them away from gasoline, fertilizers, paints and other flammable materials.[3]
  • Do not mix old product with fresh, even if it is the same type. Even a small amount of water splashed on a pool chemical may in some cases trigger a strong reaction.[6]

SafetyFor accidental poisoning or injury from a pool chemical, get medical help or call poison control right away. In the U.S., Poison Help is 1-800-222-1222, available 24/7 in over 100 languages.[3][18]

When to call a professionalFor pool chemicals in unlabeled containers, contact your local or state hazardous materials agency about proper disposal.[16]

Common questions

Does a strong chlorine smell mean there is too much chlorine?
Usually not. A "chlorine" smell usually means chloramines, which form when chlorine combines with sweat, dirt and pee.[1][8]
Can I switch tablet brands in my feeder?
Check your feeder's manual first. Explosions have occurred when a pool user switched from one type of chlorine tablet in a chlorinator to a different type without thoroughly cleaning it. Do not mix different types of chlorine products.[5][16]
Do I need stabilizer?
It depends on your pool. Stabilizer helps outdoor pools keep chlorine in sunlight, but it slows disinfection, and the guidance above does not recommend it indoors or in hot tubs. Follow your equipment manual.[2][10][13]

Pool Water Balance, Explained for Parents

What pH, total alkalinity, calcium hardness and cyanuric acid do in a home pool, the target ranges, and how pool surfaces differ.

  • Guides
  • Pool stores
  • Service companies

Applies to: Home in-ground and above-ground pools with plaster, vinyl or fiberglass surfaces; chlorine and salt-chlorine systems.

Quick answer

Balanced water helps chlorine work and protects your pool's surface and equipment. Keep pH at 7.0–7.8. For alkalinity, calcium hardness and stabilizer, test-kit maker Taylor lists ideal ranges. Product labels, your pool maker and local codes come first.[1][2][3][4][5][6]

Pool upkeep is not drowning prevention.Keep the fence, self-latching gate, alarms and close adult supervision in place, and keep children away from chemicals and equipment while you work. See backyard pool safety layers.

What “balanced” means

Proper water balance is the single most important factor in how long a pool lasts and how it looks.[2]

This guide looks at four readings: pH, total alkalinity, calcium hardness and cyanuric acid.

You may also see the Langelier Saturation Index (LSI), a measurement of the water's overall balance.[4]

Check with your local pool professional to confirm the right ranges for your pool. Commonly accepted ranges do not override product label directions or local and state codes. Products with EPA-accepted labels that show other ranges must be used as their label says.[4][5]

pH

Keep pool pH at 7.0–7.8. This range best balances killing germs, the lifespan of the pipes, and swimmers' comfort.[3]

  • Too high: chlorine kills germs less well as pH rises, especially above 8.0, and scale can build up.[3][4]
  • Too low: as pH drops, especially below 7.0, pipes are more likely to corrode or break down. Low pH also irritates skin and corrodes equipment.[3][4]
  • Either way: pH out of range can cause skin and eye irritation and damage pool equipment.[1]

Taylor's ideal pH, 7.4–7.6, sits inside CDC's range. Rain, dust, organic matter, covering the pool and pool additives can all shift pH.[4][7]

Total alkalinity

Total alkalinity is your water's buffer: its ability to resist a pH change. It guards against sudden pH swings.[7][8]

  • Too low: there may not be enough buffering, and pH can quickly drift out of range.[8]
  • Too high: pH tends to drift upward.[8]

Taylor's ideal range for total alkalinity is 80–120 ppm.[4]

Correct alkalinity first, before adjusting pH or sanitizer. If you use stabilizer, cyanuric acid above 30 ppm shows up in the alkalinity test.[5][8][9]

Calcium hardness

  • Too low: the water becomes aggressive and can leach minerals out of pool walls and grout.[4]
  • Too high: it leads to crusty, grayish-white scale in pipes and the heater. It can also clog filters and plumbing and cloud the water.[4][7]

Sources differ here. Taylor gives an ideal calcium hardness of 200–400 ppm for pools. AquaChek gives 250–500 ppm, but for total hardness, which also counts magnesium. Check which reading your test gives before comparing.[4][7]

Cyanuric acid (stabilizer)

Cyanuric acid is mainly used in outdoor pools, where it works like sunblock for chlorine. Too much makes chlorine less efficient.[4][7]

Taylor's ideal range is 30–50 ppm. Germ-kill times are longer when cyanuric acid is present. In stabilized pools, keep chlorine at least 2 ppm.[3][4]

Cyanuric acid does not stabilize bromine. Our chlorine and stabilizer guide covers this in more depth.[10]

How your pool surface and equipment change the picture

  • Plaster (gunite): water that is too soft slowly dissolves plaster walls, and low pH can etch plaster.[7][11]
  • Vinyl liners: liners are sensitive to pH changes, which can make them wrinkle, stretch and turn brittle. Below pH 7.0 a liner can develop wrinkles, and high pH speeds up aging.[2][11]
  • Fiberglass: low pH can stain fiberglass. Ask your pool's maker for its own targets.[11]
  • Metal parts and heaters: soft water can corrode metal fixtures, and acidic water can corrode equipment. Dissolved metals can stain surfaces.[2][7][11]
  • Salt systems: scale forms inside the generator cell even in balanced water, so follow the maker's advice on preventing and removing it.[12]

Check with your local pool professional to confirm the right ranges for your pool.[4]

Adjusting safely

This guide gives no amounts on purpose. Read and follow every product label for your pool's volume, and retest afterward. Testing again 24–48 hours after adding chemicals helps you rebalance accurately.[1][7][8]

SafetyNever mix chlorine products with acid; this could create toxic gases. Never mix different pool chemicals, such as different types of chlorine products, either. If a label says to pre-dissolve, add the chemical to water, never water to the chemical, because a violent reaction can occur.[13][14]

SafetyWear safety equipment such as gloves and goggles, follow the label, and keep chemicals secure and away from children and pets.[1]

When to call a professionalIf your readings won't stay in range, or you see etching, scale, staining or liner wrinkles, talk to a pool professional or dealer and bring your test log. For any chemical exposure, call the Poison Help Line at 1-800-222-1222.[15][16]

Common questions

My test kit lists an ideal pH of 7.4–7.6, but CDC's range is 7.0–7.8. Which is right?
CDC's range, 7.0–7.8, best balances killing germs, the lifespan of the pipes and swimmers' comfort. Taylor's 7.4–7.6 "ideal" sits inside it.[3][4]
Do I need stabilizer?
Cyanuric acid is mainly used in outdoor pools to protect chlorine from sunlight. If you use it, keep chlorine at least 2 ppm.[3][4]
Why does my alkalinity read high when I use stabilized chlorine?
Cyanuric acid above 30 ppm shows up in the alkalinity test. Check your kit's instructions.[5]

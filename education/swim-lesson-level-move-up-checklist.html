Moving Up a Swim Level: A Parent’s Checklist for the Next Class (Free Printable)

Your child has been moved up to the next swim level. That is worth celebrating, and it also brings changes: harder skills, maybe a new instructor, a new group or a new time slot. A little planning keeps the first few weeks calm. Here is a simple checklist and a free one-page printable.

Quick Answer: Confirm what the new level works on and what changes (instructor, day, time, class length, water depth), tell your child what stays the same, plan a calm first class and expect a short dip while new skills settle. Praise effort, check in with the instructor after a few weeks and keep watching closely, because a new level never replaces adult supervision. Our free printable puts the plan on one page.

⬆️ Why a Move-Up Feels Different

Moving up is good news, but it can still feel like starting over. The skills get harder, the instructor may expect more independence, and your child may be the newest swimmer in a group that already knows the routine. Some children love the challenge, while others feel unsure for a while. Our guide to swim lesson levels and swim level translator explain how levels usually build on each other, and this checklist helps you plan the step itself.

📋 Confirm the New Class Details

Before the first class, ask the school a few practical questions: which level your child is moving to, what skills it works on, and whether the instructor, day, time, class length or pool area will change. Ask whether the move needs a new registration or a change to your schedule or billing, and when it starts. If you are not sure why your child moved now, ask which skills the instructor saw; our level placement guide, swim level assessment guide and guide to mastery-based and session-based advancement explain the different ways schools decide. If the time slot changes, our swim schedule planner helps you fit it into the week.

💬 Talk With Your Child

Celebrate the hard work that led to the move, not just the new level name. Then talk about what will stay the same (the pool, the swim bag, you watching) and what may be different (a new teacher, new friends, harder skills). Let your child ask questions, and keep your words calm and positive; our what-to-say encouragement checklist has phrases that help. If your child is nervous or says they do not want to go, our swim lesson reluctance plan has gentle next steps. A reward chart or a new goal from our goal-setting worksheet can give the new level a fresh focus.

🏊 Plan the First Class in the New Level

Arrive a little early so your child can find the new class spot and meet the instructor. If the instructor is new, share one or two helpful things about your child, such as what helps when they feel unsure. Keep the rest of the routine the same, from the night-before prep to the goodbye. Follow the school’s pool deck rules, stay on site and keep your eyes on your child with your phone put away; our watching-a-lesson checklist shows what to look for. Coming from parent-and-me? Our parent-and-me to independent class checklist covers that first big step.

📅 Expect a Dip in the First Few Weeks

A child who sailed through the last level may look less sure for a few classes. That is common: the skills are new, and confidence often catches up after the body does. After each class, ask open questions and name one thing that went well; our after-class debrief checklist helps. After three or four classes, check in with the instructor using our parent-instructor check-in card. If your child stays very unhappy or seems stuck for a long time, read our guides to progress plateaus and realistic swim progress timelines, and ask the school about options. At the end of the session, our re-enrollment decision checklist can help you decide what comes next.

🛟 A New Level Is Not a Safety Pass

Moving up means your child showed certain skills in a lesson, with an instructor close by. It does not mean your child can swim safely alone, and it does not make any child drown-proof. Keep close, undistracted adult supervision around all water, stay within arm’s reach of young or weaker swimmers and use barriers and life jackets where they are needed. If a child is in trouble in the water, get help and call 911 or your local emergency number.

Frequently Asked Questions

What changes when my child moves up a swim level?

Usually the skills get harder and the class asks for more independence. Depending on the school, a new level can also mean a different instructor, a new group of children, a different day or time, a longer class or work in deeper water. Ask your school exactly what will change for your child.

How do swim schools decide when a child moves up?

Each school sets its own rules. Some move children when they show the skills for the current level, and others move groups at the end of a session. Ask the instructor which skills your child has shown, what the next level works on and how your school makes the decision.

Is it normal for my child to struggle after moving up a level?

Yes. New skills are harder, and a new instructor or group takes some getting used to, so a short dip in confidence or progress is common in the first few classes. Keep the routine the same, praise effort and talk with the instructor if the struggle continues for several weeks.

Should I ask the school to keep my child in the same class with the same instructor?

You can ask, but the school may not be able to keep the same instructor, time or group. Ask what your options are, and focus on the right level for your child. If a familiar face matters a lot to your child, tell the school before the move.

What if my child does not want to move up?

Find out what worries them, such as a new teacher, deeper water or leaving friends. Talk about what will stay the same, visit or watch the new class if the school allows and celebrate effort rather than the level name. If worries continue, share them with the instructor.

Does moving up a level mean my child is safe in deeper water?

No. A new level means your child showed certain skills in a lesson setting. It does not make a child drown-proof. Children still need close, undistracted adult supervision around water at all times, plus barriers and life jackets where they are needed.

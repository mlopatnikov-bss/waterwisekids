Fishing Water Safety Checklist for Kids: Keep Children Safe on Every Fishing Trip

A fishing trip is one of childhood's best days out — and one where the water dangers hide in plain sight. Most child drownings on fishing trips happen from the bank or dock, not a boat, while the grown-ups are focused on rods and bait. This printable checklist gives your family the four things that matter most: the gear to pack, how to set up a safe spot, the rules while lines are in the water, and exactly what to do if a child falls in.

Fishing is one of the most common reasons families end up standing at the edge of deep, open water — and the risk is easy to underestimate. The setting feels calm and recreational, but a pond bank, a lake dock, and a riverside are all open-water environments with the same core hazards: slippery footing, sudden drop-offs, cold water, and currents you cannot see from the surface. The U.S. Coast Guard consistently reports that fishing is among the top activities involved in fatal boating accidents, and drowning is the cause of death in the large majority of those cases.

What makes fishing uniquely risky for children is where the adult's attention goes. Baiting a hook, casting, untangling a line, and landing a fish all pull a supervising grown-up's eyes and hands away from the child — often at the exact moment a curious toddler steps toward the water to see what everyone is looking at. And drowning does not look like the movies: it is silent and fast, with no splashing or calling out. A child can slip in and disappear beneath the surface in the time it takes to net a fish.

This guide — and the free printable checklist — walks you through the four parts of a safe fishing trip: what to pack before you go, how to set up a safe spot on the bank or dock, the rules to keep while lines are in the water, and exactly what to do if a child ends up in the water. It draws on guidance from the U.S. Coast Guard, the National Safe Boating Council, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and the American Red Cross. For the bigger picture on how open-water drowning risk breaks down by age and setting, see our child drowning statistics dashboard.

What water safety gear do kids need for a fishing trip?

Before you go, pack a properly fitted Coast Guard-approved life jacket for every child, plus a throwable flotation device, dry clothes, and a charged phone. Fishing safety is decided at home, when you load the car. The right gear turns a slip into a scare instead of a tragedy.

Before You Go

  • Pack a Coast Guard-approved life jacket for every child. Choose one sized to the child's weight, in good condition, with all straps and buckles working. Water wings, pool floaties, and puddle jumpers are toys — not life jackets — and have no place near open water.
  • Bring a throwable rescue device. A ring buoy or throw rope, or even a spare life jacket on a line, lets you help from shore without going in. Boats are required to carry a throwable for a reason — keep one bankside too.
  • Dress for the water, not the weather. Water is often far colder than the air, especially in spring and fall. Pack a change of dry clothes and a towel in case anyone goes in, and add layers for cool or moving water.
  • Charge a phone and know your location. Save the address or GPS coordinates of your fishing spot so you can direct 911 to a remote bank or boat ramp. Cell service can be spotty — know the nearest landmark.
  • Check the weather and water conditions. Avoid fishing during storms, high water, or after heavy rain when banks are muddy and currents run fast. Lightning near open water is a serious hazard — when thunder roars, go indoors.
  • Talk through the rules in the car. No running near the edge, life jackets stay on, and you fish only where a grown-up says. Setting expectations before you arrive prevents the excited dash to the water.

The life jacket point is the one families most often shortcut. The temptation to "just take it off for a quick cast" is exactly how children end up in the water without one. Decide before you leave that life jackets go on at the car and come off at the car — see our full life jacket guide for how to choose and fit one correctly.

How do you set up a safe fishing spot for children?

Set up back from the edge on stable, dry ground, scout the drop-off and current before anyone casts, and assign one adult as a Water Watcher whose only job is the kids. Where you plant your chairs and cooler shapes the whole day. Two minutes of scouting prevents most fishing-trip emergencies.

At the Water's Edge

  • Scout the footing and the drop-off first. Look for slippery mud, wet rocks, algae-covered docks, and steep or undercut banks. Test how quickly the water gets deep — many ponds and lakes drop off sharply within a step or two of the shore.
  • Position kids back from the edge. Set up the base camp and seat young children a safe distance from the water. Let them approach the edge only with a grown-up, holding a hand.
  • Assign a dedicated Water Watcher. One adult, no phone, no fishing, no distractions — eyes on the children at all times. Rotate the role every 15–30 minutes and hand off out loud so there's never a gap. Touch supervision is the standard for toddlers.
  • Keep hooks, knives, bait, and tackle out of reach. Store sharp gear closed and elevated. Barbed hooks and fillet knives are their own hazard, and a child reaching for tackle near the edge is a child near the water.
  • Mind docks and piers. Boards can be slick and gaps can trap small feet. Know where the ladder is, and be aware that some docks with electricity can pose an electric shock drowning risk — never let kids swim near a powered dock.
  • Point out the boundaries to the kids. Show children exactly where they may stand and where they may not. Clear, physical boundaries work far better than a general "be careful."

A Water Watcher is not the same as "everyone's keeping an eye out." When supervision is shared among a group of busy adults, it quietly becomes no one's job. Naming one person, handing off the role out loud, and keeping that person phone-free is what actually keeps a child from slipping in unnoticed. For lakes and rivers specifically, our open water safety checklist covers the added hazards of currents, cold, and murky water.

What are the water safety rules while fishing with kids?

While lines are in the water, keep life jackets on, keep young children within arm's reach, never let kids wade or swim to retrieve tackle, and take breaks before anyone gets tired or careless. The middle of the trip — when everyone has relaxed and the novelty has worn off — is when supervision slips. Hold the line on the core rules.

Life jackets stay on. Not just while casting, and not just on the boat. A child near the edge is a child who can fall in, and the jacket only works if it's being worn when that happens.

Keep toddlers within arm's reach. For children under about five, or any non-swimmer, "supervision" means close enough to touch. Fishing and touch supervision cannot be done by the same person at the same time — that's why you bring a second adult.

Never wade or swim after gear. A snagged lure or a dropped rod is not worth entering the water. Teach kids that gear is replaceable and that they never go in after it — tell a grown-up instead.

Watch for tangled lines and slippery movement. Fishing line around ankles, a sudden lunge for a bite, or a slip on a wet rock can all put a child in the water. Keep walkways clear and remind kids to move slowly near the edge.

Take rest and food breaks away from the water. Tired, hungry, or bored children take risks. Move back from the edge to eat and rest, and use the break to rotate the Water Watcher. Skills like calm floating come from real practice, which is why water competency matters as much as any rule on this list.

What should you do if a child falls in the water while fishing?

If a child falls in, shout for help, reach or throw a flotation device — do not jump in unless you are trained — and call 911. Teach children to flip onto their back, float, and follow a safe path to the edge. Every second counts, and the instinct to leap in after a child is how a rescuer becomes a second victim.

Reach or throw — don't go. Extend a fishing rod, a branch, or a pole, or throw a ring buoy, rope, or spare life jacket. Lie down or brace yourself so you can't be pulled in. The American Red Cross teaches "reach or throw, don't go" precisely because untrained rescuers drown trying to help.

If they're wearing a life jacket, it's doing its job. A properly fitted jacket keeps a child's head above water even if they panic. This is the whole reason it goes on before the trip — it turns a fall into a rescue you can manage from shore.

Teach the flip-float-follow response. A child who can roll onto their back, float, and stay calm buys the time an adult needs to reach them. Panic and thrashing burn energy fast. This calm-in-the-water instinct is exactly what quality swim lessons build — see our five layers of protection for how skills, supervision, and barriers work together.

Call 911 and start rescue breathing if needed. Once the child is out, if they are not breathing, begin CPR immediately and have someone call 911. Knowing what to do in a drowning emergency before it happens is part of every family's water safety plan.

Why do swim skills matter for fishing safety?

Swim skills matter because a fishing trip puts children next to open water for hours, and the child who can float and stay calm after a fall has a dramatically better chance than one who panics. Life jackets and supervision are the front line, but swim ability is the backup layer that works when everything else fails — and it's the only layer that travels with the child everywhere they go near water.

Open water is harder than a pool: it's colder, deeper, murkier, and moving. A child whose only experience is a warm, shallow, calm pool can be caught off guard by a lake or river. Structured, safety-first swim lessons that teach floating, treading, breath control, and how to get to safety build exactly the instincts a fishing environment demands. The goal isn't to make a child "drown-proof" — no child ever is — but to add a genuine survival skill on top of the life jacket and the Water Watcher. How do you teach kids fishing water safety?

Teach kids a few short, repeatable rules and practice them every trip: life jacket on, stay back from the edge, never go in after gear, and if you fall in, flip and float. Children remember short phrases far better than long lectures, and repetition turns a rule into a reflex.

  • “Life jacket on at the car.” Make it the first and last thing that happens, every single trip, with no exceptions.
  • “Ask before the edge.” Kids approach the water only with a grown-up, holding a hand.
  • “Gear is not worth the water.” Never wade or swim after a lure, rod, or hat — tell an adult instead.
  • “If you fall in, flip and float.” Roll onto your back, float, stay calm, and call for help.
  • “Walk, don't run, near the water.” Wet banks and docks are slippery — slow feet stay upright.

Pair these rules with a solid foundation of swim skills. The calm confidence to float after an unexpected fall comes from real practice in the water, and that's exactly what structured, safety-first swim lessons provide. Frequently Asked Questions

Do kids need to wear a life jacket while fishing from the shore or a dock?

Yes. Most child drownings on fishing trips happen from the bank, a dock, or a pier — not from a boat. Wet rocks, mud, and dock edges are slippery, drop-offs are often just a step away, and a child who slips in can be underwater in seconds. Put non-swimmers and weak swimmers in a Coast Guard-approved life jacket any time they are within about ten feet of the water's edge, and keep it on for the whole trip. A life jacket you take off to 'just cast a few times' is the one that isn't there when a child slips.

What is the biggest water danger when fishing with children?

The biggest danger is an unnoticed slip into the water while the adults are focused on rods, bait, or a bite. Fishing pulls a supervising adult's eyes and hands away from the child at exactly the wrong moments, and drowning is silent — there is no splashing or shouting. Slippery banks, sudden drop-offs, tangled lines, and cold or moving water all add risk. The fix is a dedicated Water Watcher whose only job is to watch the kids, plus a life jacket on every young child near the edge.

At what age can a child fish safely near the water?

There is no age at which a child is safe near water without supervision. Toddlers and preschoolers need touch supervision — an adult within arm's reach — every moment near a bank, dock, or boat, plus a properly fitted life jacket. School-age children can fish with more independence but still need constant adult eyes and, for weak swimmers, a life jacket. Swimming ability lowers risk but never removes it: even strong young swimmers can be pulled under by cold water, a current, or a fall that knocks the wind out of them.

Should kids wear life jackets on a fishing boat?

Always. The U.S. Coast Guard reports that most people who die in boating accidents drown, and the large majority were not wearing a life jacket. Many states legally require children under a certain age to wear a Coast Guard-approved life jacket at all times on a moving boat. Choose a jacket sized to the child's weight, fastened snugly so it cannot be pulled up over the chin, and put it on before stepping aboard — not after the boat is underway. Fishing is consistently one of the top activities involved in fatal boating accidents, so life jackets are non-negotiable.

What should a child do if they fall in the water while fishing?

Teach children to stay calm, roll onto their back, and float to keep their head above water while they call for help — the same 'flip, float, and follow' skill taught in quality swim lessons. If they are wearing a life jacket, it will hold them up. A child who panics and thrashes tires quickly, so the calm-and-float response is what buys time for an adult to reach them with a pole, rope, or throwable device. Adults should reach or throw — never jump in unless trained — to avoid becoming a second victim.

How do you keep a toddler safe on a fishing trip?

Keep a toddler in a properly fitted Coast Guard-approved life jacket the entire trip, and assign one adult to touch supervision — within arm's reach — whose only job is the child, not the fishing. Set up well back from the edge, keep hooks, bait, knives, and tackle out of reach, and bring a second adult so supervision never lapses when someone needs to rebait or land a fish. If you cannot guarantee undivided eyes on a toddler, it is safer to fish another day or trade off in short shifts.

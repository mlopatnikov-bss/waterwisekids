How to Teach a Child to Swim: A Step-by-Step Guide by Age

Teaching a child to swim is mostly about order. Children who stall usually skipped a step — they were asked to kick before they were comfortable putting their face in, or to swim before they could float. Here is the sequence swim instructors use, and what a parent can realistically teach at each age.

Quick Answer: To teach a child to swim, go in this order: water comfort, blowing bubbles, going underwater on a cue, floating on the back and front, kicking, arm strokes, and then the safety skills — turning back to the wall and climbing out. Practice in warm, shallow water with an adult within arm’s reach, keep sessions to 20–30 minutes, and only move on when the current step feels easy.

How do you teach a child to swim, step by step?

The nine steps below build on each other, and each should feel easy before you add the next. A calm child who can float will learn to kick in a week. A tense child who is being pushed to kick will fight the water for a whole summer. So the fastest route is the one that does not skip.

Why this is worth doing well: Drowning is the leading cause of death for children ages 1–4 in the United States, according to the CDC. A widely cited case-control study referenced by the American Academy of Pediatrics found that participation in formal swim lessons was associated with an 88% lower risk of drowning among children ages 1–4. Swim skills are one layer of protection — they never replace supervision, fences and life jackets.

Step 1: Set up for safety before you start

Choose warm, calm water shallow enough for you to stand comfortably, with the child at your chest or waist. Young children lose body heat quickly, and a shivering child cannot relax, so a heated pool is worth the trip for toddlers. Stay within arm’s reach the whole time — what the AAP calls touch supervision — and put your phone away.

Take off inflatable arm bands and puddle jumpers for practice time. They hold a child upright, the opposite of the horizontal position swimming needs, and they teach a child that they can jump in and bob back up. Our guide to the hidden dangers of pool floaties explains why; if your child is already used to them, wean them off gradually.

Step 2: Build water comfort

Before any skill, a child has to feel that water is a normal, pleasant place. Walk around together, pour cups of water over their shoulders and then the back of their head, sit on the step and splash, hold the wall and kick for fun. Sing. Count. Let them pour water on you. Early sessions should be mostly play. You are done with this step when your child moves freely in the shallow end and does not cling to you.

Step 3: Teach breath control (blowing bubbles)

This is the most skipped step and the one that matters most. A child who breathes out through the mouth and nose when the face is wet will not inhale water. Start by blowing a ping-pong ball across the surface, then blow bubbles with the lips touching the water, then with the mouth under, then humming with the nose under. Make it a game: “Can you make big bubbles? Tiny bubbles? Bubbles that last until I count to three?”

Step 4: Go underwater on a cue

Once bubbles are easy, lower the face in stages over several sessions: chin, mouth, nose, eyes, then the whole head. Use the same cue every time, such as “Ready, set, go,” and have the child take a breath on “set.” Predictability is what makes it feel safe. Never pour water over a child’s face without warning or dunk them against their will; one bad surprise can undo weeks of comfort. The full progression is in the underwater section below.

Step 5: Float on the back and the front

Floating teaches body position, and it is also the skill that buys time in an emergency. For a back float, stand behind or beside your child, rest the back of their head on your shoulder or in your palm, and put your other hand under the lower back. Ears in the water, tummy up, arms out like a starfish. Over several sessions, move from two hands to one, to fingertips, to a hand hovering just below. The front float is the same idea face-down with a breath held, and it leads straight into the glide. Our step-by-step guide to teaching a child to float covers each age in detail.

Step 6: Add the kick

Have your child hold the wall with both hands, stretch out on their tummy and kick with fairly straight legs, moving from the hips with pointed toes. The common mistake is bicycle-pedaling with bent knees, which sinks the legs. Next, hold both of their hands and walk backward while they kick toward you, face in with bubbles and then chin up for a breath. Back kicking, with your hand under the head, comes after the back float is steady.

Step 7: Add the arms

For young children, a simple “reach and pull” paddle works better than a formal stroke: reach one hand forward, scoop the water back to the hip, then the other hand. Practice it standing, then while you support the tummy, then combined with the kick for short glides from you to the wall — two or three body lengths at first. Formal front crawl, with side breathing, is easier for most children from about age 5 or 6 and is best learned from an instructor.

Step 8: Teach the safety skills

A child who can paddle to you but cannot get back to the wall is not safe yet. Practice these every session once the basics are in place:

  • Turn around and return to the wall. Most children who fall in are within a body length of the edge.
  • Climb out without a ladder using the “elbow, elbow, tummy, knee” method — see teaching kids to climb out of the pool.
  • Roll from front to back to breathe, then roll back and keep going. This is the core of the swim-float-swim method.
  • Enter the water safely and only with permission — how to teach safe pool entry.

Step 9: Build distance, then hand off to lessons

Lengthen swims a little at a time, add treading water for older children, and start working toward the American Red Cross water competency sequence: enter water over your head, return to the surface, float or tread for a minute, turn around and find the exit, swim 25 yards, and get out. Our water competency skills checklist lets you track each one. This is usually the point where structured lessons earn their keep.

What can you teach a child to swim at each age?

Age changes what is realistic, not the order. Every age starts with comfort and breath control; older children simply move through the steps faster and can learn real strokes.

AgeWhat to focus onRealistic goal
Under 1Parent-and-baby water play, pouring water, supported back floatsEnjoys the water; no fear of a wet face
1–3Bubbles, going under on a cue, supported floats, kicking at the wall, turning to the wallShort glides to a parent; grabs the wall after a turn
4–5Independent floats, kick plus paddle, rolling to breathe, climbing outSwims 3–5 body lengths; floats unassisted
6+Front crawl with side breathing, backstroke, treading water, deep-water skillsWorks toward 25 yards and the water-competency sequence

The AAP says swim lessons can start as early as age 1 for many children, while noting there is no evidence that lessons before age 1 reduce drowning risk. For more detail on timing, see when kids should learn to swim and our swim milestones by age.

How do you teach a child to swim underwater?

Teach them to breathe out first, then lower the face in small stages on a predictable cue, and never force it. Children panic underwater when they are surprised or when they try to breathe in. Remove both problems and most children go under happily.

  1. Bubbles on the surface, then with the mouth under, then humming with the nose under.
  2. Face in for a count of one, on “ready, set, go.” Praise it, even if it was a quick dip.
  3. Whole head under, holding your hands or the wall, and come up smiling. Wipe nothing — teach them water on the face is fine.
  4. Eyes open underwater: count your fingers, find a toy. Open eyes calm children down. Whether to use goggles is covered in should kids learn to swim with goggles.
  5. Glides under the surface from you to the wall, then picking up sinking rings in water shallow enough to stand in.

Two cautions. Never play breath-holding contests or hyperventilate before going under; the American Red Cross warns that prolonged breath-holding can cause a swimmer to black out without warning. And keep underwater play in water where you are standing within reach.

How can you teach a child to swim quickly?

Practice often and briefly, and do not skip steps. Frequency beats length: two or three sessions of 20–30 minutes a week move a child faster than one long weekly session, because young children forget between sessions. A one- or two-week daily “intensive” can jump-start a nervous beginner. End every session on a success.

What does not work is rushing. A child pushed to kick before they are comfortable underwater will lift their head, drop their legs and stall. Pushing a fearful child usually adds weeks. For an honest picture of how long each stage takes, see how long it takes to learn to swim.

What if your child is afraid of the water?

Slow down to the last step they found easy and stay there until they ask for more. Fear is common and usually passes with gentle, predictable exposure. Let the child control the pace: they choose when to put their face in, and you keep your promises (“I will not let go until you say so”). Bath-time bubbles and water activities at home help between pool visits. Our guide to helping a child with a fear of water has more.

What mistakes should parents avoid?

  • Forced dunking. It teaches that the water is unpredictable, the opposite of what you want.
  • Relying on floaties during practice. They put the body in the wrong position.
  • Sessions that run too long. A cold, tired child learns nothing and remembers the struggle.
  • Teaching strokes before floating. Floating is what lets a child rest and breathe.
  • Treating a swimmer as “water safe.” Even strong young swimmers need an adult watching, a fence around home pools, and a life jacket in open water.

When should you switch to swim lessons?

Parents are excellent at comfort, bubbles, floating and practice; instructors are better at technique and a consistent progression. Consider lessons when your child can go under calmly but stalls on kick-and-paddle, when you want to build real strokes, or when you want an objective check on the water-competency skills. Instructors work through defined stages — our guide to swim levels for kids explains what each one covers — and you can keep practicing the current level at home between classes.

If you are deciding on a format, compare private and group swim lessons, then find swim lessons near you. Swim schools that want to reach families researching lessons can learn about being listed on WaterWiseKids.

Frequently asked questions

How do you teach a child to swim step by step?

Work in this order: water comfort, breath control (blowing bubbles), going underwater on a cue, floating on the back and front, kicking, arm strokes, and then the safety skills of turning back to the wall and climbing out. Each step should feel easy before you add the next. Practice in warm, shallow water with an adult within arm’s reach at all times.

What age should you start teaching a child to swim?

The American Academy of Pediatrics says swim lessons can start as early as age 1 for many children. Babies under 1 can enjoy parent-and-child water play, but there is no evidence lessons at that age lower drowning risk. Most children are developmentally ready for stroke technique around ages 4 to 6.

How do you teach a child to swim underwater?

First teach them to blow bubbles, so they breathe out instead of in. Then lower the face in stages: chin, mouth, nose, eyes, and finally the whole head, always on the same “ready, set, go” cue so there are no surprises. Never force a child under. Once they can go under calmly, add short glides to you and practice picking up sinking toys in shallow water.

How can I teach my child to swim quickly?

Frequency matters more than length. Two or three short sessions a week, 20 to 30 minutes each, move a child faster than one long weekly session, and a one- or two-week daily intensive can jump-start a nervous beginner. Skipping steps does not speed things up; a child who is not comfortable underwater will stall at kicking and arms.

Can parents teach their child to swim, or do they need lessons?

Parents can teach water comfort, bubbles, going under, floating and a basic kick very well, and practice between lessons speeds progress. Structured lessons with a trained instructor are still recommended for stroke technique, a consistent progression and the water-competency skills, and no amount of swimming ability replaces close adult supervision.

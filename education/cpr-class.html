CPR for Kids Class: How to Find and Choose One

Most CPR classes are built for workplaces and spend their time on adults. Here is how to find one that puts an infant manikin in front of you — and what to ignore when you are comparing them.

Quick Answer: To find a CPR class that covers kids, search the American Red Cross or American Heart Association class finders for “Adult, Child and Infant CPR/AED” — not plain “CPR/AED,” which is usually adult-only. Expect 2–4 hours, roughly $60–$120, and a certification valid for two years. Hospitals, YMCAs, fire departments and parks departments often run the same courses free or at cost. Choose an in-person or blended class over online-only: a purely online course cannot certify you, because certification requires a hands-on skills check.

Which CPR Class Actually Covers Kids?

This is where most parents waste their money, and it is an easy mistake to make because the course names are genuinely confusing.

The default CPR course sold to the general public is built for workplace compliance. It is about an adult collapsing in an office. It will teach you excellent adult technique and may spend ten minutes on children at the end, if that. What a parent needs is the version that puts an infant manikin on the table in front of you.

  • “Adult, Child and Infant CPR/AED” — this is the one. The name says it. Red Cross and AHA both offer it.
  • “Pediatric First Aid/CPR/AED” — also good, and usually required for childcare workers, coaches and camp staff. Adds pediatric first aid (choking, allergic reactions, bleeding) on top.
  • “CPR/AED” or “Heartsaver CPR AED” — check carefully. Often adult-only. Ask before you book.
  • “BLS” (Basic Life Support) — the healthcare-provider course. It covers children thoroughly but assumes you will be working with a team and a bag-valve mask. More time, more money, more than a parent needs.
  • “Hands-Only CPR” — a free 20-minute community demonstration, not a certification, and specifically the wrong protocol for drowning. Useful awareness; not the class.

That last point matters more than it looks. Hands-only CPR omits rescue breaths, and for a drowning victim the breaths are the treatment. Our article on what changes between CPR for kids and adults explains why the drowning sequence starts with breaths at every age.

Where Do You Find a Class Near You?

Start with the two national providers, because their certifications are the ones employers, camps and licensing bodies recognise:

  • American Red Cross class finder — enter a ZIP code, filter by CPR, look for the infant-inclusive course names above.
  • American Heart Association course catalog — same idea, and the AHA courses tend to be what hospitals teach.

Then, before you pay full price, work through the local list. In most towns at least one of these runs the same certification for free or close to it:

  1. The hospital with the pediatric unit. Community education departments often run parent CPR classes as outreach, and the instructors are pediatric nurses.
  2. The fire department. Many run monthly community CPR sessions. Some certify; some are awareness-only, so ask which.
  3. The YMCA. Frequently discounted for members, sometimes free, and they already have the manikins for lifeguard training.
  4. Parks and recreation. Check the same seasonal brochure that lists swim lessons.
  5. Your child's swim school. Some host a parent CPR session once or twice a year, particularly before summer. Worth asking — and if they do not, suggesting it.
  6. Your employer. If your workplace runs annual CPR compliance training, ask whether you can take the adult-child-infant version instead of the adult-only one. It is often the same cost to them.

Searching locally works best with the town name attached — “infant CPR class” plus your city will surface hospital and fire department listings that the national finders miss entirely.

What Does It Cost and How Long Does It Take?

The honest summary: roughly $60–$120, two to four hours, once every two years. That is about ninety minutes a year to be the person who knows what to do. Set against it: the CDC reports drowning as the leading cause of death for children ages 1–4, and most fatal child drownings happen with an adult nearby.

Format changes the time more than the price. A fully in-person class runs three to four hours, most of it hands-on. A blended class puts the knowledge portion online — you do it on the sofa over a couple of evenings — and then books you for a 60- to 90-minute in-person skills session. For a working parent, blended is usually the format that actually gets completed.

What you are paying for is the manikin time. Proper compression depth takes noticeably more force than people expect, and the moment that lands is when you push on a manikin and the feedback clicker does not click. You cannot get that from a video, which is the whole reason the hands-on assessment exists.

Certification lasts two years from both providers, but your actual skill does not. Research on skill retention consistently finds measurable decay within three to six months. Instructors who know this tell parents to run the rhythm on a sofa cushion occasionally. It feels silly and it works.

Can You Get Certified Entirely Online?

No — and the distinction is worth understanding before you buy something.

Neither the American Red Cross nor the American Heart Association will issue a certification without a hands-on skills assessment conducted by an instructor. The reason is simple: the failure mode in real CPR is not knowing the steps, it is not pushing hard enough. The only way to verify that is to watch someone do it.

What you will find online is two very different things wearing similar names:

  • Blended learning (legitimate) — online coursework from a recognised provider, followed by a scheduled in-person skills session. You end up with the same certification as a fully in-person student.
  • Instant online “certification” (usually not accepted) — a quiz and a downloadable PDF, no skills component, often from a provider you have never heard of. Employers, childcare licensing bodies, camps and coaching organisations routinely reject these.

If nobody is checking — you are doing this purely so you know what to do for your own child — then a knowledge-only course is better than nothing. But you will have learned the steps without ever finding out how hard you have to push, and that is the part that fails under pressure.

What Should You Ask Before You Book?

Five questions. Ask them on the phone or by email and you will avoid every common disappointment:

  1. “Does this class cover infant CPR, or adults only?” The single most important question. Course titles lie by omission.
  2. “Will I get a certification card, and from whom?” Red Cross or AHA are the recognised ones. If the answer is a name you do not know, ask whether your employer or your child's camp will accept it.
  3. “Is there an infant manikin for each small group?” One demonstration doll for a room of twenty means you will watch rather than practise.
  4. “Does it cover choking and AED use?” Both should be included. Child choking rescue is a different technique from the adult version, and AED pad placement on a small child differs too.
  5. “Does it address drowning specifically?” The best answer is that the instructor explains why drowning starts with rescue breaths instead of compressions. If the class treats all CPR as hands-only, it is not built for a pool.

A sixth, optional one: ask whether they will run a session for a group. Several families, a swim team, a preschool's parent association — most instructors will come to you, and per-person cost drops sharply.

Where Does CPR Fit in the Bigger Picture?

CPR training is the layer you hope stays unused. It sits at the far end of a chain of prevention, and it only comes into play once everything ahead of it has already failed.

The layers ahead of it are the ones that actually keep children alive: four-sided pool fencing with a self-latching gate, an adult whose only job is watching the water, properly fitted life jackets in open water, and swim lessons. Our guide to the five layers of protection explains how they compound — no single layer is sufficient, and the whole point is that a failure in one is caught by the next.

Formal swim instruction is the layer parents most often postpone. The American Academy of Pediatrics supports swim lessons for most children from around age 1, and our free directory of swim programs covers all fifty states with no fee and no signup. If you run a program yourself, you can add your school to the directory — and hosting a parent CPR session is one of the more genuinely useful things a swim school can offer its families.

Take the class. Then go back to the layers that mean you never need it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What CPR class should a parent take?

Look for a course named “Adult, Child and Infant CPR/AED” or “Pediatric First Aid/CPR/AED.” A plain “CPR/AED” or “Heartsaver CPR” listing is frequently the adult-only workplace version, which skips infant technique entirely. You do not need Basic Life Support (BLS) — that is the healthcare-provider course, and while it covers children well, it is longer, more expensive and aimed at people working in teams with equipment you will not have at a pool.

How much does a CPR class for kids cost?

A typical Red Cross or AHA adult-child-infant CPR/AED course runs about $60 to $120 for an individual, and blended courses (online coursework plus a short in-person skills session) sit at the lower end. But price is often not the binding constraint: hospitals with pediatric units, local fire departments, YMCAs, parks and recreation departments and some pediatricians' offices run the same certification classes free or at cost for parents and caregivers. Call before you pay.

How long does a CPR certification last?

Two years, from both the American Red Cross and the American Heart Association. The renewal course is shorter than the original. Two years is not an arbitrary bureaucratic interval — hands-on CPR skill measurably degrades within three to six months of training, which is why instructors encourage practising the compression rhythm at home between certifications.

Can you get CPR certified online?

Not fully. Both the Red Cross and the AHA require a hands-on skills assessment with an instructor before they will issue a certification, because the only way to confirm you can compress a chest to the correct depth is to watch you do it. What does exist is a blended format: you complete the knowledge portion online at your own pace, then attend a short in-person session for the skills check. Beware of sites selling instant “certificates” with no skills component — employers and licensing bodies generally do not accept them.

Do you need a CPR class if your child takes swim lessons?

Swim lessons and CPR training address different moments. Lessons reduce the chance a child gets into trouble; CPR is what you need in the minutes after they already have. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends both, and treats CPR training for parents and caregivers as one of the layers of drowning protection rather than an optional extra. If your child is in lessons and you are not trained, you have built the front half of the plan.

Water Safety for Family Camping Trips

Camping puts families next to lakes, rivers, and streams with no lifeguards, no fences, and unfamiliar conditions. A little preparation keeps the water the best part of the trip.

Quick Answer: At a campsite, treat every body of water as a hazard: scout it before kids explore, have children wear Coast Guard-approved life jackets near rivers and lakes, assign a dedicated water watcher, beware of cold water and strong currents, and never let kids swim alone or at night. There are no lifeguards or fences in the wild, so your supervision and life jackets are the main safeguards.

Why is camping water different from a pool or beach?

Camping water has no lifeguard, no posted depth, no fence, and murky conditions that hide hazards — and help may be far away. Camping often means swimming where there is no lifeguard, no posted depth, no fence, and no clean, clear pool to see the bottom. Natural water at a campsite is unpredictable, and the remote setting means help may be far away.

Natural water risk: The CDC reports that natural bodies of water — lakes, rivers, and ponds — are the most common drowning sites for older children and teens. Currents, cold, uneven bottoms, and limited visibility make them riskier than a controlled pool.

The upside is that nearly all of these risks are manageable with awareness and a few firm rules. Our guides to lake and ocean safety and river and stream safety for kids go deeper on each setting.

What should I do before the kids go near the water?

Scout the water first — check depth and bottom, look for current, note the temperature, set boundaries, and say the rules out loud. Scout the water before your children explore it. Five minutes of recon prevents most problems.

  • Check depth and bottom. Wade in yourself to feel for drop-offs, rocks, mud, and sudden depth changes. Never let kids dive into water of unknown depth.
  • Look for current. Even a calm-looking river can have a strong pull. Toss in a stick and watch how fast it travels.
  • Note the temperature. Mountain lakes and rivers can be shockingly cold even in summer — cold enough to trigger a dangerous gasp reflex. Read our guide to cold water shock.
  • Identify entry and exit points and a clear boundary for where kids may go.
  • Set the rules out loud with your kids before anyone gets in.

What gear keeps kids safe at the campsite?

The essentials are Coast Guard-approved life jackets, a throwable flotation or rope, a first aid kit and a way to call for help, water shoes, and bright swimwear. The U.S. Coast Guard recommends a properly fitted, approved life jacket for every child near rivers, lakes, and any paddling. A few key items make a big difference when you are far from help.

  1. Coast Guard-approved life jackets for every child, fitted to their weight. These are essential near rivers, lakes, and any boating or paddling. See our life jacket guide.
  2. A throwable flotation device or rope for reaching a struggling swimmer without entering dangerous water yourself.
  3. A stocked first aid kit and a charged phone or other way to call for help.
  4. Water shoes to protect against sharp rocks and slippery surfaces.
  5. Bright swimwear so kids are easy to spot in murky or moving water.

If your trip includes a canoe, kayak, or paddleboard, life jackets are non-negotiable for everyone aboard, every time.

What water rules matter most while camping?

The key rules: never swim alone, assign a dedicated water watcher, stay within boundaries, no swimming at night, and keep little ones within arm's reach. Clear, consistent rules do the heavy lifting in a setting with no lifeguards. Keep them simple enough that kids remember them.

  • Never swim alone. Use the buddy system, and an adult must always be watching.
  • Assign a dedicated water watcher. One sober adult keeps eyes on the kids, with no phone or campfire cooking distracting them. Rotate the job to stay fresh.
  • Stay within set boundaries and out of fast-moving or deep water.
  • No swimming at night. Darkness makes supervision and rescue nearly impossible.
  • Keep little ones within arm's reach at the water's edge — toddlers can wander to water in seconds around a campsite.

Because a campsite has no fence between your tent and the lake, supervision is constant, not occasional. Treat the water's edge with the same care you would a busy road.

How do I prepare for an emergency in a remote setting?

Know in advance how to call for help and where the nearest signal is, learn CPR before the trip, and use "reach or throw, don't go" to assist a struggling swimmer. Far from a lifeguard station or quick ambulance, your preparation is the safety net. Know in advance how you would call for help and where the nearest ranger station or cell signal is. Learning CPR basics before a trip is one of the most valuable things a camping parent can do.

Teach the "reach or throw, don't go" principle: if someone is struggling, reach with a branch or paddle or throw a flotation device rather than jumping in, which often leads to two victims instead of one. Review our open water safety checklist before you leave, pack it with your gear, and run through the plan with everyone old enough to understand. With the basics covered, the lake at the campsite becomes a place of great memories — not anxiety.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do kids need life jackets while camping near water?

Yes. Near rivers, lakes, and any boating or paddling, children should wear Coast Guard-approved life jackets fitted to their weight. Campsites have no lifeguards or fences, so life jackets and supervision are your main safeguards.

Why is natural water more dangerous than a pool?

Lakes, rivers, and streams have currents, cold temperatures, uneven bottoms, drop-offs, and murky water that hides hazards, plus no lifeguards or clear depth markers. The CDC reports natural water is the most common drowning site for older children and teens.

How cold is too cold for kids to swim while camping?

Mountain lakes and rivers can be cold enough to trigger a dangerous gasp reflex even in summer. Always test the temperature, ease in gradually, keep sessions short, and watch for shivering or bluish lips. When water is very cold, it is safer to keep kids out.

What should I do before letting my kids swim at a campsite?

Scout the water first: check depth and the bottom for drop-offs and rocks, look for current, note the temperature, and set clear boundaries. Then assign a dedicated adult water watcher and make sure kids wear life jackets near moving or deep water.

What's the safest way to help someone struggling in water while camping?

Use the 'reach or throw, don't go' principle: extend a branch or paddle, or throw a flotation device, rather than jumping in yourself, which often creates two victims. Have a way to call for help and know basic CPR before your trip.

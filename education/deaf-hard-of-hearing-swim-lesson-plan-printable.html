Swim Lesson Plan for Kids Who Are Deaf or Hard of Hearing

For families of children who take hearing aids or a processor off for the pool, or who rely on lip-reading or sign. Check the devices with the audiologist, keep them safe and dry, brief the instructor and lifeguard, agree on visual signals and keep supervision close.

🩺 Step 2: Questions for the Audiologist

  • Water rating. Is this device rated for splashes only, or for swimming with a waterproof accessory? Model: ______
  • Accessory. Is there a waterproof cover made for this model, and is it right for lessons?
  • Remote microphone. Would a microphone the instructor wears help on the deck?
  • Ears. Any ear tubes, infection or procedure that affects swimming? Ear plugs or a swim band needed?

🧳 Step 3: Device Handoff and Wet-Device Plan

Devices come off at: ☐ Locker room ☐ Pool edge ☐ Other
Dry case is kept in: ☐ Parent bag ☐ Locker ☐ Other
Who holds the case
Devices back in: once out of the water and dried off
If a device gets wet: take it off right away, dry the outside with a soft cloth, open the battery door and remove the battery if it has one, and put it in a drying kit. No direct heat. Not working or sounds different: call the audiologist.

🗣️ Step 4: Tell the Instructor and Lifeguard

  • Hearing. My child is deaf or hard of hearing and communicates by ______. With devices off they can hear ______.
  • Attention first. Please wave or tap before you speak, then face my child with light on your face.
  • Explain on the deck. Give instructions while devices are still in; demonstrate in the water instead of calling out.
  • Whistle. Please don’t talk with the whistle in your mouth or while walking away; my child may not hear it.
  • Lifeguard and front desk. Told on ______; they have our signals. Hearing details are on the medical form.

👀 Step 6: Supervision and Ear Care

  • Lifeguarded pools. We swim where lifeguards are on duty and introduce our child to them.
  • Sight and reach. A named Water Watcher stays where our child can see them and they can reach our child.
  • Pool words. We have shown our child the shallow and deep areas and practiced shallow, deep and no diving.
  • After the lesson. Rinse, dry outer ears well, and check every device is in its case before we leave. Ear pain, swelling or discharge: call the doctor.
Signs to mention to the instructor and audiologist: copying other children instead of the instructor, starting skills late, missing the stop signal, looking lost when the group moves, or frustration by the end of class. These are prompts to adjust the plan, not a diagnosis.
Remember: swim lessons and visual signals are layers of protection. They never replace close adult supervision, barriers around water and a named Water Watcher.

This plan is a family planner, not medical or audiology advice. Follow your child’s audiologist, doctor and device user guide. Sources: National Deaf Children’s Society; Royal Life Saving Australia; Central Institute for the Deaf; Cochlear (manufacturer); American Academy of Pediatrics (HealthyChildren.org).

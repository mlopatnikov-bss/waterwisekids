Cloudy Pool Water: Why You Shouldn't Swim If You Can't See the Bottom

A hazy or green pool looks unappealing, but the real danger is not how it looks — it is what it hides. Water you cannot see through can hide a child in trouble, and that turns a maintenance problem into a drowning-prevention one.

Quick Answer: Do not swim in cloudy or green pool water. If you cannot clearly see the bottom drain, you cannot see a child who has slipped under the surface — and drowning is silent and fast. Cloudy water also signals that sanitizer and pH are off, raising the risk of waterborne illness. Treat any pool you cannot see through as closed, and report a cloudy public or hotel pool to staff.

Why cloudy water is a safety issue, not a cosmetic one

Most parents think of cloudy pool water as gross but harmless — a sign the pool needs a cleaning, nothing more. The truth is more serious. The single biggest reason to stay out of hazy water has nothing to do with germs and everything to do with sight lines.

Drowning does not look like the thrashing and shouting we see in movies. It is usually silent, and a struggling child often slips quietly beneath the surface. The only defense against that is constant visual supervision — an adult or lifeguard who can see a child in distress and reach them within seconds. When the water is cloudy, that defense disappears. A child resting on the bottom of a murky pool can be completely invisible from the deck just a few feet away.

Clear water is what makes split-second recognition possible. For the national picture on how often silent drownings occur, see our water-safety statistics.

Seconds matter: According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), drowning is the leading cause of unintentional injury death for children ages 1 to 4, and it can happen in the time it takes to answer a text. Visibility is what shortens the gap between a child going under and an adult noticing — and cloudy water erases it.

The rule: if you can't see the bottom, don't get in

Aquatics professionals use a simple, memorable standard: you should be able to clearly see the main drain at the deepest point of the pool. If the drain is blurry, shadowy, or invisible, the pool fails the test and no one should swim. It is the same logic lifeguards use when they close a pool — if they cannot see the bottom, they cannot guard it.

Teach this rule to your children the way you teach them to look both ways before crossing a street. "If you can't see the bottom, you don't get in" is short enough for a five-year-old to remember and absolute enough that there is no negotiating. It applies to backyard pools, community pools, hotel pools, and the murky end of a neighbor's above-ground pool alike.

What makes water turn cloudy or green?

Cloudiness is the water telling you something is out of balance. The usual culprits are:

  • Low or imbalanced chlorine. When sanitizer drops, particles and microorganisms are no longer being controlled and the water hazes over.
  • Off-balance pH. Water that is too alkaline causes minerals to cloud, and it also makes chlorine less effective.
  • Poor filtration or circulation. A failing pump or clogged filter lets fine debris stay suspended.
  • Heavy bather load. Lots of swimmers, sunscreen, and sweat overwhelm the chemistry, especially on a hot, crowded day.
  • Algae. A green tint specifically means algae is growing, which happens fast when sanitizer falls behind.

Every one of these reflects a maintenance gap. A pool that has gone cloudy has, by definition, not been kept up — which is exactly why the cloudiness doubles as a warning about the water you and your children would be swimming in.

The hidden second risk: getting sick

Beyond visibility, cloudy water often means the chlorine is too low to do its real job: killing germs. Pools transmit recreational water illnesses when sanitizer cannot keep up, leading to diarrhea, ear infections, and skin and eye irritation. The CDC stresses that correct chlorine and pH are what protect swimmers from these germs. So a pool you cannot see through is often a pool that is not being disinfected properly, either. We cover this in depth in our guide to recreational water illness prevention.

Cloudy water at public, hotel, and apartment pools

At your own pool you control the chemistry. Away from home, you are at the mercy of someone else's maintenance — and that is where families most often face the temptation to swim anyway. A child has been promised the hotel pool all day, you arrive, and the water looks off. The disappointment is real, but the answer is firm: keep everyone out and tell the front desk or lifeguard.

Do not assume staff have noticed or that a slightly hazy pool is "probably fine." Shared pools at hotels, apartments, and HOAs frequently run thin on maintenance, and many have no lifeguard at all — a combination we examine in apartment and condo pool safety. A pool no one can see the bottom of should not be open, and a polite report protects every other child who shows up after you.

If it's your pool: close it until it clears

For pool owners, the response to cloudy water is straightforward: close it to swimmers and fix the chemistry before anyone gets back in. Test the chlorine and pH, run the filter, and balance the water according to your test results — or call your pool service. Resist the urge to let kids take "just a quick dip" while the pool clears; the visibility hazard is fully present even when the water is only moderately hazy.

Clear water is not a luxury or a vanity standard. It is one of the layers of protection that let supervision actually work. Combined with secure fencing, touch supervision, swim skills, and a plan for emergencies, water you can see through is part of what keeps a backyard pool safe.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to swim in cloudy pool water?

No. The most important reason is that cloudy water hides a submerged or struggling child, making a silent drowning impossible to spot in time. It also usually means the chemical balance is off, raising the risk of illness. If you cannot clearly see the bottom drain, no one should swim.

Why is clear water a drowning-prevention issue?

Drowning is usually silent and fast, and a child in trouble often sinks below the surface. A lifeguard or parent can only respond to what they can see. If the water is cloudy, a child on the bottom can be invisible from the deck. Clear water is what makes constant visual supervision possible.

What makes pool water turn cloudy or green?

The common causes are low or imbalanced chlorine, off-balance pH, poor filtration, heavy use, and algae growth. Green water specifically points to algae, which thrives when sanitizer drops. All reflect maintenance problems, so a cloudy pool should be treated as closed until it clears.

Can cloudy water make my child sick?

It can. Cloudiness often signals sanitizer levels too low to kill germs, raising the risk of recreational water illnesses that cause diarrhea, ear infections, and irritation. The CDC notes that correct chlorine and pH protect swimmers, so cloudy water is both a visibility and a hygiene hazard.

What should I do if a public or hotel pool looks cloudy?

Keep your family out and tell the lifeguard or front desk. A pool you cannot see the bottom of should not be open. Do not let your child swim in it to avoid disappointment — no swim is worth a pool where a struggling child would be invisible.

Swim Lesson Plan for Kids Who Are Deaf or Hard of Hearing (Free Printable)

Is your child deaf or hard of hearing? In the pool, hearing aids usually come off, and the whistle, the instructor’s voice and the lifeguard’s warning may never reach them. A child who cannot hear the instruction may look like they are not listening when they simply did not get the message. This guide and free one-page plan help you ask the audiologist the right questions, protect the devices, brief the instructor and lifeguard, agree on visual signals and keep supervision close.

Quick Answer: Most hearing aids are not waterproof and come off for swimming; some cochlear implant processors can be used in water with a manufacturer’s waterproof accessory, so ask your child’s audiologist what their exact device allows. Before the first class, tell the instructor and lifeguard how your child communicates, ask them to get your child’s attention visually and face them when speaking, and agree on a few visual signals such as stop, come to the wall and get out now. Store devices in a dry case in one agreed place, keep your child within sight and reach of a named adult, and follow the doctor’s advice on ear care. 🦻 Why Plan for Hearing Before Lessons

Swim lessons and pools run on sound. Instructors call out the next skill, whistles mean stop, and lifeguards shout warnings. As Royal Life Saving Australia points out, lifeguard whistles, verbal warnings, public address announcements and shouted instructions all rely on sound. For a child who is deaf or hard of hearing, and especially once hearing aids or a processor come off for the water, much of that may not get through. A child who misses the instruction can hesitate, follow the wrong child or seem not to be listening. A few minutes of planning before the first class fixes most of it: the right answer for the devices, an instructor and lifeguard who know, a handful of agreed visual signals and an adult who stays close. Families of children who use sign language or another home language may also like our bilingual swim lesson cue word card.

🩺 Step 1: Questions for the Audiologist

Bring the device details to your child’s next audiology appointment and ask a few swim-specific questions. Is this hearing aid or processor rated for water, and for splashes only or for full submersion? Is there a waterproof cover or accessory made for this model, and is it right for a child in lessons? Would a remote microphone the instructor can wear help on the deck? The National Deaf Children’s Society says most hearing aids are not waterproof and should be removed before swimming or bathing; water-resistant aids can survive a splash but should not be fully submerged or exposed to chlorinated or salt water. For cochlear implants, some processors can be used in water with a manufacturer’s waterproof accessory, but Cochlear’s own guidance ties water ratings to specific models and points families to the product’s user guide, so check yours rather than relying on another family’s experience. If your child has ear tubes, frequent ear infections or a recent ear procedure, ask the doctor whether and when swimming is fine, and whether ear plugs or a swim band are needed; our swim lessons with ear tubes checklist covers those questions. Write the answers on the printable.

🧳 Step 2: The Device Handoff and Wet-Device Plan

Hearing aids and processors are small, expensive and easy to lose on a pool deck. Agree on one routine and repeat it every week. Give any instructions your child needs on the deck while their devices are still in, then take them off at the same moment each time, place them in a labeled dry case, and keep the case in one agreed place such as your bag or a locker, never on a bench or the pool edge. The Central Institute for the Deaf suggests storing devices in a dry place such as a dry-aid storage kit. Decide who holds the case if a grandparent or carpool driver brings your child, and put the devices back in once your child is out of the water and dried off. If a device gets wet, take it off right away, dry the outside gently with a soft cloth, open the battery door and remove the battery if it has one, and place it in a drying kit; avoid direct heat, and contact the audiologist if it stops working or sounds different. Our gear labeling checklist helps the case come home every time.

🗣️ Step 3: Tell the Instructor and the Lifeguard

Instructors and lifeguards adjust easily when they know. Before the first class, tell the instructor, the front desk and the lifeguard on duty that your child is deaf or hard of hearing, how your child communicates (speech, lip-reading, sign language or a mix), and roughly what they can hear with devices off. Guidance for swim teachers from the National Deaf Children’s Society gives a simple checklist you can pass on: get the child’s attention first with a wave or a tap, stand still and face the child with light on your face, don’t talk with a whistle in your mouth or while moving away, and don’t talk and demonstrate at the same time. Ask the instructor to explain the next skill on the deck while devices are still in, to demonstrate instead of calling out in the water, and to check that your child understood. If your child uses a sign language interpreter, share the plan with them too. Add your child’s hearing details and any ear condition to the swim lesson medical information form, and use our parent–instructor check-in card to follow up after a few weeks. If a busy group class makes visual cues hard to follow, compare private and group swim lessons.

✋ Step 4: Agree on Visual Signals

Agree on a small set of visual signals before your child gets in the water, and practice them on the deck until they are automatic. A useful starting set is look at me, stop, come to the wall, and get out now for an emergency, plus a thumbs-up for I’m OK. Teach your child a clear way to ask for help without sound, such as waving one arm high overhead, and to look for the instructor or a parent whenever the group stops. Share the same signals with the lifeguard and anyone who supervises your child at the pool, and ask the facility how it alerts swimmers who cannot hear an alarm or whistle; Royal Life Saving encourages pools to pair sound-based warnings with visual ones. The printable has a signal card you can fill in and hand to the instructor.

👀 Step 5: Keep Supervision Close

Because a child who cannot hear may miss a whistle, a warning or a call to get out, supervision has to be visual and close. Choose pools with lifeguards on duty, stay where your child can see you and you can reach them, and name one adult as the Water Watcher whenever your child is in or near water outside the lesson. The Central Institute for the Deaf suggests showing your child where the shallow and deep areas are and teaching pool words such as shallow, deep and no diving. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends close, constant supervision and layered protection for young children around water, and that applies to every child, whatever their hearing. Our layers of protection checklist covers barriers and the rest of the plan at home. A lifeguard on duty is a backup, not a substitute; read why lifeguards don’t replace parent supervision.

🧼 Step 6: Ear Care After the Lesson

Rinse off pool water, dry the outer ears well and check that every device is back in its case before you leave the building. Follow your child’s doctor or audiologist on ear plugs, ear moulds and swim bands; the Central Institute for the Deaf notes that ear plugs or ear putty can help some children avoid ear infections. If your child has an ear infection, ask the doctor whether to pause swimming and the hearing aid until the ear heals. Our swimmer’s ear prevention checklist covers drying and the signs worth a call to the pediatrician, such as ear pain, swelling or discharge.

👀 Signs the Plan Needs Adjusting

Watch a lesson or two from the viewing area. Watching other children to copy them, starting a skill late, missing the stop signal, looking lost when the group moves or seeming tired and frustrated by the end of class can all have many causes, but for a child who is deaf or hard of hearing they often mean the communication plan needs a tweak. Mention what you see to the instructor and your child’s audiologist, and adjust the signals or where the instructor stands. This is a prompt for a conversation, not a diagnosis. Our guide to evaluating swim instructor feedback can help you frame it.

🎽 Clear Signals, Steady Lessons

Once communication is sorted, steady lessons build the skills that lower risk around water: getting back to the wall, floating, breath control and safe entries and exits, and indoor lessons keep that routine going all winter. You can browse swim lessons near you or read our guides to adaptive swimming and winter swim lessons. Families in New Jersey and Pennsylvania can see our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia, which link to the official location pages; ask the location how instructors communicate with children who are deaf or hard of hearing. Swim lessons and visual signals are layers of protection and never replace close adult supervision, barriers and a Water Watcher.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should my child wear hearing aids in the pool?

Usually not. Most hearing aids are not waterproof, so the National Deaf Children’s Society advises removing them before swimming or bathing; water-resistant aids can survive a splash but should not be submerged or exposed to chlorinated or salt water. Ask your child’s audiologist what their specific device is rated for.

Can children with cochlear implants swim?

Many children with cochlear implants swim and take lessons. Some sound processors can be used in water with a manufacturer’s waterproof accessory, while others must come off before swimming. Water ratings depend on the exact model, so follow your child’s audiologist and the processor’s user guide.

What should I tell the swim instructor about my child’s hearing?

Tell the instructor how your child communicates, such as speech, lip-reading or sign language, what they can hear with their devices off, and how to get their attention first with a wave or a tap. Ask the instructor to face your child, keep the whistle out of their mouth while talking, explain on the deck while devices are still in, and demonstrate instead of talking during a skill.

What visual signals should we agree on for swim lessons?

Agree on a small set of signals before your child gets in the water and practice them on the deck: for example look at me, stop, come to the wall, and get out now for an emergency. Teach your child a clear way to signal for help, such as waving one arm overhead, and give the same signals to the lifeguard.

What should I do if my child’s hearing aid gets wet?

Take it off right away, dry the outside gently with a soft cloth, open the battery door and remove the battery if it has one, and place the aid in a drying kit. Do not use direct heat. If it stops working or sounds different, contact your child’s audiologist.

How will a lifeguard know my child cannot hear the whistle?

Only if someone tells them. Lifeguard whistles, shouted warnings and announcements all rely on sound, so introduce your child to the lifeguard and front desk, share your agreed visual signals, and ask how the pool alerts swimmers who cannot hear. Keep your child within sight and reach of a named adult as well.

Poolside CPR Quick Card — Drowning

Drowning is a breathing emergency, so rescue breaths come first. Check · Call 911 · 2 Breaths · then 30 compressions to 2 breaths until help arrives.

WaterWiseKids.com — Free water safety education for families

Cut out the wallet cards below, laminate, and keep one in the pool bag, on the fridge, and on the boat.

Not a substitute for CPR training. This card is a memory aid only. Take a hands-on infant, child, and adult CPR class (American Red Cross or American Heart Association), and always follow your 911 dispatcher's instructions in a real emergency.

The Drowning-CPR Sequence (Once They're Out of the Water)

  • CHECK for response and breathing. Tap and shout; look for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Occasional gasping is not breathing — begin CPR.
  • CALL 911 — assign it to a person. "You in the red shirt, call 911 and bring the AED." Put the phone on speaker. If you're alone with a child and have no phone, do about 2 minutes of CPR first, then call.
  • Give 2 RESCUE BREATHS. Tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose (cover nose+mouth for an infant), and give 2 breaths of about 1 second each. Watch for the chest to rise.
  • Start COMPRESSIONS. Push hard and fast in the center of the chest at 100–120 per minute, letting the chest come all the way back up between pushes.
  • Keep the 30:2 cycle going. 30 compressions, then 2 breaths — repeat without long pauses until the person breathes normally, an AED is ready, or trained responders take over.

Hands, Depth & Breaths by Age

  • Adult / older child: heel of two hands, center of chest, about 2 inches deep. Full breaths.
  • Child (about 1 yr–puberty): one or two hands, about 2 inches or one-third the chest depth. Normal breaths.
  • Infant (under 1 yr): two fingers or two thumbs, about 1.5 inches deep. Gentle puff-sized breaths only.
  • Ratio stays 30:2 for a single rescuer at every age. Push fast, allow full recoil, minimize pauses.
  • Water or vomit in the mouth? Turn the head or roll to clear it, then continue. Don't waste time trying to "push water out" of the lungs.

After the Rescue — Even If They Seem Fine

  • Get any submerged child evaluated by a medical professional, even after they recover — rarely, breathing trouble appears hours later.
  • Watch for: persistent coughing, fast or labored breathing, chest pain, extreme fatigue, or unusual irritability. Seek care right away if these appear.
  • Take a CPR class before you ever need it — reading a card is not the same as practicing the skill on a manikin.
  • Prevention is the real plan: four-sided fences, a designated Water Watcher, life jackets, and swim lessons stop most emergencies before they start.

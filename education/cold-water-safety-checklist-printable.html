Cold Water Safety Checklist for Families

Cold water overwhelms even strong swimmers — know the 1-10-1 steps before you go

❄️ Know the Danger (the 1-10-1 Principle)

  • Cold water shock comes first, not hypothermia — sudden immersion below about 60°F triggers an involuntary gasp and uncontrollable breathing that can drown a strong swimmer in seconds.
  • 1 — about 1 minute to control your breathing as the gasp reflex passes. Do not try to swim yet.
  • 10 — about 10 minutes of useful movement before cold robs your muscles of strength. Use it to self-rescue or grab a float.
  • 1 — about 1 hour before hypothermia causes unconsciousness. Conserve heat and stay still if you can't get out.
  • Treat all open water as cold water — lakes, rivers, and the ocean stay cold even on hot days and cool fast in fall.

🎒 Before You Go

  • Everyone WEARS a properly fitted life jacket — not just carries one. It floats you during the gasp reflex when your body can't.
  • Dress for the water temperature, not the air — if there's any chance of ending up in the water, dress for it.
  • Never go alone — a second person can call 911 and start a shore-based rescue.
  • Tell someone on land your plan and return time — where you're going and when you'll be back.
  • Charged phone in a waterproof case — and know how to describe your exact location.

🌊 If You Fall In: Float to Live

  • Don't fight the water — the instinct to thrash and swim hard is what drowns people in the first minute. Resist it.
  • Lean back and float — head back, ears in the water, arms and legs spread. Keep your airway clear.
  • Let your breathing settle (about 1 minute) — the gasp and hyperventilation feel terrifying but they pass.
  • Then self-rescue or signal — once calm, swim to safety if it's close, grab anything that floats, or hold a heat-conserving float and call for help.
  • Keep your head and neck out of the water — thrashing sheds body heat faster. Stay as still as you can.

🛟 If Someone Else Falls In

  • Call 911 first, then shout for help — cold-water rescues escalate fast and they'll need medical care even if they get out.
  • Reach or Throw — DON'T Go — extend a pole, branch, or rope, or throw something that floats. Stay on shore.
  • Never jump in after them — the same cold water shock will overwhelm you too. Most double tragedies start with a rescuer.
  • Once they're out, treat for cold — remove wet clothing, wrap them warm and dry, keep them still, and get medical help.
  • Be ready to start CPR — at least one adult should know infant/child and adult CPR.

Critical Reminders for Cold Water

  • Cold water shock kills fast — most cold-water deaths happen in the first minutes, long before hypothermia sets in.
  • Swimming ability does not make you safe — cold robs even strong swimmers of breath and strength within minutes.
  • A worn life jacket is the single biggest factor in survival — it does the floating when your body can't.
  • Swim lessons lower risk — they don't replace the rules — lessons cut drowning risk for ages 1–4 by up to 88% (AAP), but no one is ever cold-water-proof.

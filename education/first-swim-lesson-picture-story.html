📖 Why a Story Helps Before the First Lesson

For a young child, a first swim lesson is a lot of new things at once: a big building, echoing voices, a changing room, a teacher they have never met, cool water and other children splashing. Not knowing what comes next is often what makes a child cling, cry or refuse to get in. A short picture story about their own first lesson, read together in the days before, turns the unknown into a routine they have already heard. This idea comes from social stories, which Carol Gray developed in 1990 as a teacher of autistic children (her approach, Social Stories™, has its own detailed criteria). The Raising Children Network explains that these stories describe the setting, what usually happens there and what the child can do, to help children notice cues and manage anxiety. Our printable is a simple preview story for any child, not a clinical tool, and it pairs well with our first swim lesson guide and first swim lesson checklist.

🏊 What the Story Covers, Page by Page

The printable has ten short pages, each with a box for a photo or drawing and one or two sentences with blanks to fill in. It starts with who, where and when (my name, my swim school, my lesson day), then going to the pool and noticing that the building may be warm and a little loud. Next come changing into a swimsuit and using the bathroom, meeting the teacher by name, and sitting on the side until the teacher says it is my turn. The middle pages cover the water itself: it may feel cool at first and my body gets used to it, my teacher shows me how to kick, blow bubbles and float, and if water splashes my face I can wipe it and try again. One page is about feelings: it is OK to feel nervous, I can take a big breath and tell my teacher, and my grown-up is watching from a place you fill in. The last pages cover saying goodbye, the towel and the ride home, and the most important pool rule: I only go in or near water when a grown-up says yes and is watching. Our first month of swim lessons guide covers what usually happens after week one.

📷 Step 1: Make It About Your Child’s Real Pool

A story works best when it matches what your child will actually see. Fill in your child’s name, the swim school, the day and time, and the teacher’s name if you know it. Photos beat drawings for many children: snap the entrance, the changing room, the pool and the viewing area during a visit, or ask the swim school whether they can share photos. Our swim school pool tour checklist lists what to look at while you are there. Cross out any line that does not fit, such as a changing room you will not use, and add one that does: a parent-and-me class, a swim cap or goggles. Match the words to your child’s age and language; families who speak another language at home can pair the story with our bilingual swim lesson cue word card.

🛋️ Step 2: Read It Together at the Right Times

Start a few days to a week before the first lesson. Read it at a calm time, let your child hold the pages and turn them, and answer questions honestly. The Raising Children Network says stories are usually read with an adult, often daily at first and just before the event, and then used less often once the child understands; it notes that when you use the story matters more than how long. A good rhythm is once a day in the run-up, once more before you leave for the pool, and a quick look in the car or the waiting area if your child wants it. Our night-before prep checklist covers the bag, the snack and the sleep that make the morning easier.

💬 Step 3: Keep It Honest, Not a Promise

Children remember what the story said. If it promised the water would be warm, that nobody would splash, or that the lesson would be fun, a cool pool or a splashed face can feel like a broken promise. Keep the sentences positive but true: it is OK to feel nervous, the water may feel cool, splashes happen and I can wipe my face, my teacher helps kids learn. Avoid lines like “you won’t get wet” or “I’ll be in the water with you” unless that is how the class works. Our what to say to encourage your swimmer checklist has more phrases that help, and the nervous parent plan helps if you are the one feeling anxious.

🌟 Step 4: After the Lesson, Add What Really Happened

On the way home or at bedtime, look at the story again and talk about what really happened: what the teacher’s name was, which part was easy, which part was hard and what your child did when they felt nervous. The printable has a short “My first lesson” page to draw or write on. Notice effort rather than results, and save rewards for trying, such as a sticker on our swim lesson reward chart. If your child is still very upset after a few weeks, talk to the instructor; our separation anxiety plan and swim lesson reluctance plan walk through next steps, and our guide to helping water-anxious kids covers fear of the water itself.

🧩 Tips for Autistic Children and Kids Who Need More Support

Social stories were first developed for autistic children, and the Raising Children Network says research shows they can have positive effects on behaviour, especially when the story is customised to the child and used at the right time. It also cautions that they may be less effective for children with limited comprehension and may not suit children who are nonverbal; a photo schedule or a first/then board may work better for them. If your child has a therapist, speech pathologist or teacher who already writes stories with them, share this printable and let them adapt it. Tell the swim school about your child’s needs before the first class and ask about adaptive or private options; our swim lessons for autistic and sensory-sensitive kids guide and adaptive aquatics first session prep cover what to ask. Many autistic children are drawn to water, so a story about lessons is never a substitute for barriers and close supervision at home.

🛟 The Most Important Page: The Pool Rule

The last story page teaches a rule that matters long after the first lesson: I only go in or near water when a grown-up says yes and is watching. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends swim lessons as a layer of protection against drowning that can begin for many children at age 1, but says lessons do not make children drown-proof. It recommends close, constant supervision, barriers that block pool access when nobody is swimming and life jackets near open water as other layers. Name a Water Watcher whenever children are in or near water, and use our layers of protection checklist at home.

🎽 Ready for the First Lesson

A calm first lesson is the start of steady weekly practice that builds the skills the AAP highlights: getting back to the surface, moving through the water and getting out safely. Indoor lessons keep that going through the winter. You can browse swim lessons near you, compare private and group lessons for a hesitant child, or read about story-based swim lessons. Families in New Jersey and Pennsylvania can see our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia, which link to the official location pages; ask the location whether you can visit or take photos of the pool for your child’s story. Swim lessons are one layer of protection and never replace close adult supervision, barriers and a Water Watcher.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a first swim lesson picture story?

It is a short, simple story about your child’s own first swim lesson, read together before the day. Each page shows one step, such as arriving at the pool, meeting the teacher, waiting for a turn, feeling the water and going home, with a photo or drawing and one or two plain sentences, so your child knows what will happen and what to do.

How do I use a picture story before swim lessons?

Fill in your child’s name, the pool, the day and the teacher’s name, add photos of the real pool if you can, and read it together a few times in the days before the first lesson and again just before you go. Keep it calm and short, let your child turn the pages and ask questions, and then talk about what really happened after the lesson.

When should I start reading the story?

A few days to a week before the first lesson is enough for most young children. Reading it every day at first and again right before the lesson works well. Once your child knows the routine, read it less often and let it fade out.

Do picture stories help autistic children with swim lessons?

Social stories were developed for autistic children, and the Raising Children Network says research shows they can have positive effects on behaviour. They work best when customised to the child and read at the right time, and they may be less suitable for children with limited comprehension or who are nonverbal, who may do better with a photo schedule. Ask your child’s therapist or an adaptive swim program for help.

What should a first swim lesson story not say?

Avoid promises you cannot keep, such as ‘it will be fun’, ‘you won’t get wet’ or ‘I will be in the water with you’ if that is not true. Keep the story honest and positive: it is OK to feel nervous, the water may feel cool, a splash may get your face wet, and the teacher helps kids learn.

Will swim lessons make my child safe around water?

No. The American Academy of Pediatrics says swim lessons are one important layer of protection but do not make children drown-proof. Keep close, constant adult supervision, barriers around pools and life jackets near open water, and teach your child to go in or near water only when a grown-up says yes and is watching.

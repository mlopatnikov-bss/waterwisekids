Boating Safety Activities for Kids: 12 Games and Drills That Actually Stick

Children do not learn boating safety from a lecture on the dock. They learn it from a drill they have already run once, on a calm day, when nothing was going wrong. These are the twelve activities worth running.

Quick Answer: The best boating safety activities for kids are the ones a child physically performs rather than hears about — an untimed life jacket fit check, a game matching life jackets to their labels, dry-land reach-or-throw practice, a pre-departure safety scavenger hunt, drawing a float plan, and a stay-with-the-boat walk-through on shore. An adult leads every one, and children never practice entering the water or moving a boat to rescue someone.

Why do hands-on activities teach boating safety better than a talk?

Because the moment a child needs boating safety, they will not be in a position to think it through. A boat that swamps or capsizes does it in seconds, usually without warning, and almost always while everyone aboard is relaxed. What survives that moment is not a rule a child was told — it is a motion a child has already made.

This is the same logic behind fire drills. Nobody expects a seven-year-old to reason about smoke on the spot; we expect them to do the thing they practiced in October. Boating deserves the same treatment, and it is not hard to arrange: you can practice several of these activities on shore with a life jacket and basic boating gear.

The rule that drives all of this: Federal law requires children under 13 to wear a U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket while a vessel is underway, with limited exceptions, and states may add their own rules, according to the U.S. Coast Guard. The Coast Guard’s annual Recreational Boating Statistics report publishes the current drowning and life jacket figures. Boating safety for children is, to a first approximation, one habit: the jacket goes on before the boat leaves the dock and stays on.

A note on where this fits. These are teaching activities, not the rules themselves. If you need the underlying rules — life jacket requirements by age, required onboard equipment, what to do if someone goes overboard — start with our complete guide to boating safety with children and come back here to make it stick.

What activities teach kids to wear and trust a life jacket?

Resistance to life jackets is nearly always a fit problem wearing a behavior costume. A jacket sized too large rides up over a child's chin and ears in the water, which is genuinely unpleasant, and children are not wrong to object to it. Fix the fit, then let them feel the jacket work.

1. The life jacket fit check

No stopwatch. Each child picks a U.S. Coast Guard-approved jacket whose label matches their weight and chest size, puts it on, and fastens every buckle and strap. An adult then checks the fit: the U.S. Coast Guard says a jacket should be secure, comfortable and adjustable, and should not rise above the chin or ears. If it does, it is too big. Praise a careful fit, not a fast one, and repeat on the next outing until the child can do it with little help.

2. Match the life jacket to its label

On shore, help your child find the approval label and the listed size or weight range. Have them match each jacket to the person who should wear it. An adult checks each fit before departure. Ask a qualified instructor if you want an in-water fit check.

3. Adults wear theirs too

Not a game, but the highest-leverage thing on this page. Children read the rule you follow, not the rule you state. If you want a family standard that holds up on a hot afternoon in August, it has to include you.

Getting the fit right is its own small project. Our boating life jacket checklist walks through sizing, the four Coast Guard type ratings, and what to check before every trip, and our guide to what to do when a child refuses a life jacket covers the harder cases. If you are not sure which size to buy, our life jacket sizing chart and fit test matches jackets to weight and chest size.

What activities teach a child to help someone in the water?

The rule to teach children is the American Red Cross WHALE Tales lesson Reach or Throw, Don’t Go: help from land with a reach or a throw, and never enter the water. All practice below happens on dry land with an adult leading. Children do not practice rowing or swimming to someone. Families who paddle can rehearse the same drills from a board, too; our paddleboard safety guide for kids covers leashes, jackets and falling off safely.

4. Reach practice (on dry land)

On grass or a deck, well back from any water’s edge, a child lies flat on their stomach — never standing, never squatting — and extends a pool noodle, a paddle or a towel to an adult sitting a few feet away on the ground. Lying flat and staying low is the lesson. First step every time: shout for an adult. Nobody is in the water for this practice.

5. Throw-rope target practice (on dry land)

Lay a hula hoop, towel or chalk circle on the grass and let the child practice tossing a throw bag, ring buoy or boat cushion so it lands at or just beyond the target. Score it like a game. Finish each turn by saying the rule out loud: reach or throw, don’t go.

6. The "never go" conversation

After the first two activities, make the last one explicit: a child never enters the water, and never takes a boat out, to rescue someone. Give them the script out loud — yell for an adult, have someone call 911, throw something that floats, and keep pointing at the person so the adult can find them. Our guide to what drowning actually looks like explains why it is so easy to miss, which is worth reading with an older child.

What activities teach the rules of being aboard?

7. The pre-departure scavenger hunt

Before the boat leaves, hand the child a list and let them find things: one wearable life jacket per person, the throwable cushion or ring, the fire extinguisher, the sound signal (horn or whistle), the navigation lights, the bailer or bilge pump, the anchor and line. Let them physically touch each one. Children who have run this ten times know where the throwable is without looking — which is the entire point, because in the moment you need it, nobody will be reading a label.

8. Three points of contact

Moving around a boat that is underway is where a lot of injuries happen. Practice the rule in a boat tied up at the dock, life jackets on and an adult aboard: to move, keep three points of contact — two feet and a hand, or two hands and a foot. Stay low. Never step on a gunwale. Turn it into a slow-motion obstacle course from bow to stern and back.

9. The quiet-and-seated game

For younger children, the rule is simply that when the engine is running, everyone is seated with their bottom on a seat. Practice at the dock with the engine off: an adult calls "underway," everyone sits; "docked," everyone may move. Small children learn this quickly as a game and it transfers directly. Standing up in a moving small boat is one of the fastest ways to put someone in the water.

How do you teach trip planning and weather?

10. Draw the float plan

A float plan is simply a note left with someone ashore: who is aboard, what the boat looks like, where you are going, and when you will be back. Have a child of eight or older draw it — the boat, the route, the return time — and physically hand it to a neighbor or text a photo of it to a relative. The exercise teaches a genuinely lifesaving habit, because the only thing that starts a search early is somebody ashore knowing you are overdue.

11. Read the sky, then check the forecast

Before a trip, have the child look at the sky and make a call: go or no-go. Then check the marine forecast together and compare. Children get good at this faster than adults expect, and they learn the honest lesson — that the sky is a useful clue and a bad sole source. Build in the flat rule for lightning: at the first rumble of thunder, the boat comes in and everyone gets off the water, which is the same rule we cover in lightning and pool safety. Thunder means lightning is already close enough to strike.

What should kids rehearse for a capsize?

12. The stay-with-the-boat walk-through

Do this on shore. With a canoe, kayak or dinghy sitting on the grass, life jackets on, talk through what happens if a small boat tips: stay with the boat and hold on, stay together, and wait for an adult’s instructions instead of swimming for shore, which is almost always farther than it looks. Let each child show where they would hold on. Do not tip a boat with children in it as a family drill; in-water capsize practice belongs in a paddling course with a qualified instructor. Our kayak and canoe safety guide for kids covers how to choose a calm spot for a first paddle and what to wear.

Pair it with a headcount drill: an adult calls "count off" and everyone calls out their own name in order, so the group knows immediately who is accounted for.

The honest framing: None of these activities substitutes for the layers that actually prevent drowning — a properly fitted life jacket worn at all times on the water, an undistracted adult whose only job is watching, and swimming ability appropriate to the environment. Activities make the habits stick. They do not replace them. See the five layers of protection for what a complete plan looks like.

Which boating safety activities fit which age?

Ages are guidelines, not gates — a cautious six-year-old may be ready for a drill a bold eight-year-old is not. Judge the child, not the birthday.

  • Ages 2–4: Life jacket fit check (with help), the jacket-label game, the quiet-and-seated game. Nothing more. At this age the entire curriculum is "the jacket is comfortable and it works."
  • Ages 5–8: Add reach practice, the pre-departure scavenger hunt, three points of contact, and the never-go conversation. This is the window where the rules become habits.
  • Ages 9–12: Add throw-rope practice, the float plan, weather reading, and the stay-with-the-boat walk-through. Boater education age rules vary by state, so check your state boating agency.
  • Teens: Run everything above, then hand over real responsibility — let the teenager own the pre-departure check and write the float plan for the family. Responsibility is what converts compliance into judgment.

If you are teaching a group rather than a family — a scout troop, a camp session, a classroom — our guides to water safety games for kids and summer camp water safety cover the group-management side, and the open water safety checklist is a printable you can hand out.

Where can families get free boating safety materials and gear?

More is free than most parents realize.

  • U.S. Coast Guard Auxiliary vessel safety checks. A trained examiner inspects your boat at no cost and issues no citations — it is purely educational. Ask your local flotilla whether it also offers life jacket fitting.
  • Life jacket loaner stations. Many state wildlife and boating agencies, and organizations such as the National Safe Boating Council, stock loaner boards at public boat ramps and beaches where a correctly sized child's life jacket can be borrowed for the day at no charge.
  • State boating safety courses. State boating agencies list approved courses, some free online, and many publish children's activity sheets alongside it.
  • Red Cross water safety resources. The American Red Cross publishes free water safety materials, including the WHALE Tales lesson Reach or Throw, Don’t Go used above.
  • CDC and AAP guidance. The CDC's drowning prevention guidance and the American Academy of Pediatrics both set out the layered-protection approach these activities are designed to support.

Frequently asked questions

What are good boating safety activities for kids?

Try a life jacket fit check and label game, dry-land reach-or-throw practice, a safety gear scavenger hunt, drawing a float plan, and talking through a capsize on shore. Have an adult lead the practice.

At what age can kids learn boating safety?

Young children can practice putting on a life jacket with help. Give older kids tasks they can handle, such as spotting safety equipment or drawing the route and return time. Check local age requirements for boating courses.

How do you teach a child to wear a life jacket without a fight?

Let your child practice on shore with a U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket sized for them. Check that it fits snugly and that all straps and buckles are fastened. Wear yours every time you go boating together.

What is reach, throw, row, go?

For children, use the American Red Cross rule “Reach or Throw, Don’t Go.” Have them call for an adult, practice reaching and throwing from dry land, and stay off the water during a rescue.

Where can families get free boating safety materials for kids?

The American Red Cross publishes free WHALE Tales lessons. Ask your state boating agency or local U.S. Coast Guard Auxiliary about activity sheets, life jacket fitting, and loaner programs in your area.

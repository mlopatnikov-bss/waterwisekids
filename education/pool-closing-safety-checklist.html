The Pool Closing Safety Checklist: How to Keep a Closed Pool From Becoming an Off-Season Drowning Hazard

When the last warm weekend passes and the pool cover goes on, it is easy to feel the danger is over until next summer. But a closed pool is not a safe pool. Off-season is when supervision drops the most, loose covers hide the water rather than block it, and rain collects on top in pools deep enough to drown a small child. This free printable pool closing safety checklist walks through what actually keeps a closed pool safe: the right cover, active barriers, and the one thing families forget once the fun stops — that the water is still there. Off-season pools remain a real hazard — our drowning statistics dashboard tracks how many child drownings happen outside peak summer months.

Quick Summary: A pool does not stop being dangerous when you close it. Off-season supervision drops, loose winter covers are not safety barriers, and a child can drown in the few inches of rainwater that collect on top of a cover. Keep every layer active: an anchored ASTM F1346 safety cover (not a floating tarp), a self-latching four-sided fence, powered gate and door alarms, a removed or locked above-ground ladder, and drained-and-stored kiddie pools. ⚠️ Is a closed pool still a drowning risk?

Yes — and in some ways the risk is higher, because the guard comes down. During swim season the pool is the center of attention: adults are nearby, eyes are on the water, rules are top of mind. Once it closes, all of that quietly switches off, but the water does not go anywhere. A toddler who wanders out a back door, a visiting child, or a family pet can reach a closed pool in seconds, and now there is no one watching. The CDC ranks drowning as the leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 4, and that risk does not follow the pool schedule.

There is a second, less obvious hazard that appears only after closing: standing water on top of the cover. Rain and melting snow collect on solid and loose covers, sometimes forming several inches of cold, murky water that a young child can drown in just as easily as in the pool below — while the cover hides them from view. Keeping a closed pool safe means treating it like an active hazard all winter, not a problem you have put away until June. Our layers of protection checklist explains why no single barrier is ever enough on its own.

🛡️ Safety cover vs. winter cover: what actually counts as a barrier?

Only an anchored safety cover is a barrier. A floating or tarp-style winter cover is not. The loose solid covers held down with water bags or clips are designed to keep leaves and debris out — they rest on or just above the water and will not hold a child's weight. A child or pet who steps onto one can slip underneath and become trapped, out of sight and out of reach. These covers have a real job, but keeping people out of the water is not it.

A true safety cover is stretched taut and anchored into the deck, built to hold significant weight and to shed water rather than pool it, meeting the ASTM F1346 safety-cover standard. That is the only kind of cover that should ever be counted as a layer of protection — and even then, it works best alongside a fence and alarms, never as your only defense. If you are choosing new equipment this season, treat the safety cover and the fence as a pair, not either-or. Our family pool safety checklist covers how these layers fit together during the season, too.

🔒 Keep the barriers working through the off-season

The fence, gate, and alarms that protected your family in July have to keep working every day through fall and winter. It is tempting to let maintenance slide once no one is swimming, but this is exactly when barriers matter most, because the human layer — active supervision — is at its lowest. Leave the four-sided isolation fence up, keep the gate self-closing and self-latching, and test it. Keep door and gate alarms powered, swapping in fresh batteries so a cold-weather drain does not silence them. Walk the perimeter for gaps, wobbly panels, or anything a determined toddler could climb. Our pool fence and gate inspection checklist gives you a repeatable way to check them.

Don't forget the above-ground and portable pools, which often get less attention than an in-ground pool but drown children every year. Remove or securely lock any ladder or steps so the water cannot be reached. For kiddie and inflatable pools, empty them completely, drain them, and store them upside down or deflated — a forgotten kiddie pool that refills with rainwater is a hidden hazard all its own. A designated water watcher still matters any time children are near a pool area, even a closed one.

🏊 The pool closed — but water risk didn't

Closing the backyard pool is the moment to keep swim skills alive, not shelve them. Water stays in a child's life all year — bathtubs, indoor hotel and gym pools, and holiday or spring-break travel to warm destinations all keep the risk present long after the cover goes on. And swimming is a perishable skill: a child who stops in September and returns the next June often slides back a level, relearning what they had already mastered. Our guide to why year-round swim lessons matter walks through how quickly that fade sets in.

The most protective response to a closing pool is to move to weekly, year-round lessons at an indoor, warm-water pool so skills keep building through the colder months. Our swim lesson frequency guide explains how often lessons need to happen to actually move a child forward, and many programs let you start with a free trial lesson to test the fit. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a safety cover and a regular winter pool cover?

A regular solid or tarp-style winter cover simply floats on the water or rests loosely over the pool, held by water bags or clips. It keeps debris out but is not a safety barrier: a child or pet who walks onto it can slip underneath and become trapped. A true safety cover is anchored taut across the pool and is built to hold significant weight and shed standing water, meeting the ASTM F1346 safety-cover standard. Only an anchored safety cover that meets that standard should be treated as a barrier, and even then it works best alongside a fence and alarms, never as your only layer of protection.

How do I keep my pool safe over the winter?

Keep every layer of protection active. Leave the four-sided isolation fence up and keep the gate self-closing and self-latching. Keep door and gate alarms powered, replacing batteries if needed. Use an anchored ASTM F1346 safety cover rather than a loose floating cover, and pump off any standing water that collects on top after rain or snow. On above-ground pools, remove or lock the ladder and steps so the water cannot be reached. Empty, drain, and store any kiddie or inflatable pools upside down. Finally, keep supervising, closed does not mean unwatched.

Can a child drown in water on top of a pool cover?

Yes, and it is one of the most overlooked off-season hazards. Rain and melting snow pool on top of solid and loose covers, sometimes forming several inches or more of cold, murky water. A young child can drown in that water just as they can in the pool itself, and the cover can hide them from view. Pump standing water off the cover promptly, keep the barrier fence and gate locked so children cannot reach the cover, and never let kids walk or play on a pool cover of any kind.

Should kids keep taking swim lessons after the pool closes?

Yes. Water risk does not close for the winter, bathtubs, indoor hotel and gym pools, and holiday travel all keep water in a child's life year-round, and swimming is a perishable skill that fades over a long break. Closing the backyard pool is the natural moment to move to weekly, year-round lessons at an indoor pool so your child's skills keep building instead of sliding back by spring. Layered safety at home plus continuous skill-building is the combination that keeps children safest through every season.

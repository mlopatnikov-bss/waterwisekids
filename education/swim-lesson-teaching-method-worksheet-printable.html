Swim Lesson Teaching Method Worksheet

Two swim schools can both call their beginner class “Level 1” and teach almost opposite things in it. The difference is not the level names, the price, or the pool — it is the method, and it comes down to four forks: what gets taught first, how a child moves up, how the lesson is delivered, and who is in the water. None of the four has a universally right answer. This sheet is how you find out which side of each fork a school sits on, in one phone call and one watched lesson, and which fork should weigh most for your particular child.

🧭 Start Here — The Level Names Are Not the Method

  • Ignore the level names on the first pass. “Level 1”, “Starfish” and “Guppy” are naming conventions, not curricula. Two schools can use the same word for classes that teach opposite things in a different order.
  • Ask one question before any other: “What will my child be able to do after eight weeks, and in what order do you teach it?” A school that teaches deliberately answers this in about thirty seconds.
  • Then watch one real lesson at the age and stage your child would join — not a trial lesson arranged for you. Ten minutes on the bench settles Forks 3 and 4 on their own.
  • Score each fork as A, B or Unclear. There is no total and no winning column. Unclear after a direct question is itself the finding.
  • Do this for two schools, not one. A method only reads as a choice when you can see the alternative beside it.
Why this beats comparing prices and schedules: price, drive time and class size are visible on a website and easy to compare, so families compare those and assume the teaching is roughly the same everywhere. It is not. The method decides what your child can do in eight weeks, how long enrollment runs, and whether an anxious child comes back for week three.

🔀 Fork 1 — What Do They Teach First?

  • Side A — survival first. The opening weeks build a self-rescue sequence: getting to the surface, rolling onto the back, floating and breathing, then reaching an edge. Stroke comes after.
  • Side B — propulsion first. The opening weeks build water comfort, breath control, kicking and gliding, then front crawl. The back float arrives as one skill among many rather than as the first goal.
  • Phrases that signal Side A: survival, self-rescue, aquatic survival, roll to back, float first, swim-float-swim, swimming in clothes.
  • Phrases that signal Side B: learn-to-swim, water acclimation, bubbles and glides, kick first, stroke development, progressive strokes.
  • Ask it directly: “If my child fell in fully clothed after eight weeks with you, what would you expect them to do?” The answer places the school on this fork faster than any brochure.
Neither side is the safe one. Both are teaching a child to survive water; they disagree about the order. Weigh Side A more heavily if there is a pool at the house, a lake or an open-water summer coming, or a child who is already mobile and curious about water. Weigh Side B more heavily if your child already floats calmly and the goal is swimming properly rather than surviving a fall.

📈 Fork 2 — How Does a Child Move Up?

  • Side A — mastery-based. A child advances when they can demonstrate the skills of the level, whenever that happens. Enrollment usually runs continuously rather than in terms.
  • Side B — session-based. A child advances at the end of a term or session, and repeats the level if they are not ready. Enrollment runs in fixed blocks you re-book each time.
  • Phrases that signal Side A: advance when ready, mastery-based, continuous enrollment, perpetual, monthly tuition, evaluated every lesson.
  • Phrases that signal Side B: session, term, semester, 6-week or 8-week class, end-of-session evaluation, registration opens.
  • Ask for the mechanics, not the philosophy: “Who decides my child has moved up, when do they decide it, and what happens to my day and time slot when they do?”
This fork decides what “six months of lessons” costs you. Mastery-based means a fast child is not held to the calendar and a slower child is not pushed on — but it usually means an open-ended monthly commitment. Session-based gives you a defined end date and a defined bill, at the cost of a child sometimes repeating a level for scheduling reasons rather than skill reasons. Ask what happens to the time slot on advancement; losing a convenient slot is the most common reason families quit a program that was working.

🎈 Fork 3 — How Is the Lesson Delivered?

  • Side A — play-led. The skill is carried inside songs, stories, games and characters. The child is often not told they are practising anything.
  • Side B — drill-led. The skill is named, demonstrated, repeated and corrected. The lesson looks more like a class and less like play.
  • What to watch for, rather than ask: in ten minutes on the bench, count how many times an instructor names a skill and corrects it versus how many times they start a song or a game.
  • Most good programs mix both and shift along this fork with age. What you are placing is the weighting at your child’s age, not an absolute.
  • Watch the quiet child, not the confident one. The instructor who keeps the reluctant child engaged is showing you the thing you actually came to see.
Match this fork to temperament, not to your own preference. An anxious, sensory-sensitive or newly water-shy child usually tolerates a play-led room far longer, and tolerance is what produces attendance. A child who already likes the water and wants to get better is often bored by a play-led class and improves faster with naming and correction.

👪 Fork 4 — Who Is in the Water?

  • Side A — parent and child together, with the adult holding, supporting and being coached alongside the child. The parent is being taught as much as the child is.
  • Side B — child with the instructor, parent on the deck or behind glass, from the earliest age the school accepts.
  • Ask when the handover happens and what triggers it — an age, a skill, or a conversation. The handover, not the starting point, is what most families are unprepared for.
  • Ask what the school expects of you between lessons: whether there is home practice, whether bath-time work is part of the method, and whether you are expected in the water at all.
  • Ask about the crying policy in plain words: “What do you do if my child cries through the lesson, and at what point do you stop?”
Two answers should end the conversation, whatever method a school claims. The first is prolonged breath-holding or underwater-distance work used as training or as a game — the American Red Cross, USA Swimming and the YMCA all prohibit it, because it can cause a blackout in a strong swimmer with no warning and no visible struggle. The second is repeated forced submersion of a distressed infant. Neither is a teaching method; both are reasons to walk.

🗣️ The Six Questions That Get a Plain Answer

  • “What will my child be able to do after eight weeks, and in what order do you teach it?” The single most useful sentence in the call.
  • “Whose curriculum is this — your own, the Red Cross, the YMCA, or a franchise program?” Then ask who trained the instructor who will actually be in the water.
  • “Who decides my child has moved up, and when?” Fork 2, asked as mechanics rather than as philosophy.
  • “How many children per instructor at my child’s age, and is that a maximum or an average?” Ask for the maximum; averages hide the bad weeks.
  • “Can I watch a full class at my child’s stage before I enroll?” A no to this is itself an answer.
  • “What is your policy on breath-holding and underwater work?” You want a school that answers this immediately and correctly, without being walked to it.

🎯 Which Fork Should Weigh Most for Your Child?

  • A pool at the house, a lake house, or open water this summer → Fork 1 outweighs the rest. Weight the survival sequence and ask what it looks like fully clothed.
  • An anxious, water-shy or sensory-sensitive child → Fork 3 and Fork 4 outweigh the rest. A play-led room and a longer parent-in-water stage buy the attendance that everything else depends on.
  • A child who already floats and wants to swim properly → Fork 3 flips. Naming and correcting the skill beats another term of games, and Fork 1 matters much less.
  • A tight budget or an irregular schedule → Fork 2 outweighs the rest, because it is the fork that sets what six months actually costs and whether you can pause.
  • A hard deadline — a trip, a camp swim test, a move → Fork 2 again, from the other side: ask what happens if your child is not through the level by the date, before you book.
Whatever the four forks say, the method is one layer and not the whole plan. No swim program at any level makes a child drown-proof, and none of these methods claims to. Lessons sit alongside four-sided pool fencing with a self-closing and self-latching gate, undistracted supervision with a named watcher, properly fitted life jackets in open water, and an adult trained in CPR. A school that implies otherwise has told you something about itself.

Once You Know the Method, Find a School That Matches

No teaching method makes a child drown-proof, and no single method wins for every child. What changes outcomes is a formal, progressive lesson program that can state plainly what it teaches and in what order — and participation in formal lessons is associated with a substantially lower drowning risk for young children.

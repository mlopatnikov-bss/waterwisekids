Daytona Beach Water Safety for Families: Rip Currents, Flags and Beach Driving

Daytona Beach is wide, flat and famously open to cars, which makes it a wonderful place for a family day and an easy place to lose track of a child. Here is the water safety plan we would want every visiting family to have.

Quick Answer: Daytona Beach is a lifeguard-patrolled Atlantic beach with rip currents, changing surf and vehicles allowed on parts of the sand. Check the posted flag before anyone gets wet, swim only near a lifeguard tower, keep a child within arm’s reach in the water, and keep kids clear of beach traffic.

Why is Daytona Beach different from a hotel pool?

An ocean beach has moving water, waves and sandbars that change by the hour, none of which a pool has. A child who swims confidently in a hotel pool is not automatically ready for surf. Waves knock small children off their feet, the bottom drops away unevenly, and a current can move a swimmer farther than they realize in under a minute.

Daytona Beach adds one more variable: the sand is hard and wide enough that vehicles are permitted on parts of it. That means a family beach day involves two supervision jobs at once, watching the water and watching the traffic. The CDC reports roughly 4,000 unintentional drowning deaths in the United States each year, and drowning remains a leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 4. Vacation is when families drop the routines that keep kids safe at home, which is why our vacation water safety guide starts with the plan before the trip.

What do the beach flags mean?

Read the flag before anyone puts a foot in the water. Most U.S. beaches follow the same color code, and lifeguards change the flag as conditions change during the day.

  • Green: low hazard, calm conditions. Still stay alert.
  • Yellow: moderate hazard, surf or currents. Weak swimmers should stay out or stay in very shallow water with a life jacket.
  • Red: high hazard, strong surf or currents. Many families skip swimming and wade only at the edge.
  • Double red: water closed to the public.
  • Purple: dangerous marine life, such as jellyfish, has been spotted.

Our full beach warning flags explained guide covers what lifeguards weigh before changing a flag, and there is a free beach flag color card to screenshot before you go. Ask the lifeguard on duty what the day looks like, since local flag practice can vary.

How do you escape a rip current?

Do not fight it: float, then swim parallel to the shore until the pull stops, and angle back to the beach. A rip current is a narrow channel of water flowing away from shore. It does not pull anyone under the surface, but it can tire a swimmer who tries to swim straight back against it. The United States Lifesaving Association reports that the large majority of surf beach rescues involve rip currents.

Teach every child over about five this three-step rule before the trip: stay calm and float, wave and yell for help, then swim sideways. Rip currents often look like a darker, choppier gap between breaking waves, with foam or debris moving seaward. Learn more in our rip current safety guide and the rip current safety checklist.

If your child is the one in trouble, get a lifeguard. Parents who rush in without flotation are a common source of second victims. Throw something that floats and point at the child so a lifeguard can find them.

How do you keep kids safe around beach traffic?

Treat the driving lanes like a street, because they are one. Where vehicles are allowed on the sand, cars move slowly, but small children are hard to see from a driver’s seat, and a child who chases a ball or runs toward the water can cross a lane in seconds.

  • Set up well away from the driving lane, on the water side of your own car or between the dunes and the lane, not in the traffic path.
  • Name one adult as the traffic watcher and another as the water watcher. Our Water Watcher card makes the handoff explicit.
  • Agree on a meeting spot and a landmark, such as a numbered access ramp or a lifeguard tower, in case a child wanders.
  • Never let a child sit or play behind a parked vehicle, and walk children to the car instead of letting them run ahead.

Local rules on where and when driving is allowed, speed limits and fees change, so check with Volusia County before you go.

How close should you stay to children in the surf?

Within arm’s reach, with eyes on the child at all times. The American Academy of Pediatrics calls this touch supervision for young or weak swimmers, and it applies even when a lifeguard is on duty. Lifeguards watch a long stretch of beach, not your child. Our guide on why lifeguards do not replace supervision explains the gap, and touch supervision explained shows what it looks like in practice.

Take shifts of about 15 minutes with a phone-free adult as the designated Water Watcher, and trade off by tapping a shoulder rather than assuming someone else has it. Drowning is quiet. It rarely looks like splashing and shouting.

Do kids need life jackets at the beach?

Yes, for young children and weak swimmers, in any depth of surf. Choose a U.S. Coast Guard-approved jacket sized for your child’s weight, check that it does not ride up when you lift the shoulders, and skip inflatable toys, which are not safety devices. See our life jacket guide for fitting steps and our life jacket sizing guide for weight ranges.

Waves can knock a child over in water that is only knee-deep, so a jacket matters even if your plan is only to wade.

What other beach hazards should families plan for?

Heat, sun and holes in the sand are the everyday hazards after the surf. Plan shade, water and sunscreen reapplication, since a child who is overheated and tired is less steady in the waves. Dig only shallow holes and fill them in, because deep holes can collapse; our sand hole collapse safety guide explains why. Watch for jellyfish and sea lice warnings and the purple flag, and shuffle your feet when wading.

Make it a habit to take a headcount when you arrive, when you leave the water, and before you pack up. The beach safety tips for kids checklist is a good reference to keep on your phone.

Should kids take swim lessons before a Florida beach trip?

Yes, because floating, breath control and rolling to your back are the skills that matter most in surf. Lessons do not make a child drown-proof, and they never replace an adult within arm’s reach. They do give a child a few extra seconds and a few more tools if something goes wrong.

Start at least a few weeks before your trip. Browse our Florida swim lesson directory or use our swim lesson finder to locate programs near home. Swim schools: see how to be listed in the WaterWiseKids directory.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Daytona Beach safe for kids to swim?

It can be, with preparation. Daytona Beach is an open Atlantic beach, so surf, rip currents and shifting sandbars are part of the day. Swim near a lifeguard tower, read the posted flag first, keep young children within arm's reach, and put weak swimmers in a properly fitted Coast Guard-approved life jacket. Confirm current conditions and beach rules with local ocean rescue on the day.

What do the beach flags mean at Daytona Beach?

Most U.S. beaches use the same color system. Green means low hazard, yellow means moderate hazard, red means high hazard, and double red means the water is closed to the public. Purple means dangerous marine life is present. Flags are the fastest way to read conditions, but check the posted signs and ask the lifeguard, because local flag use can vary.

What should I do if my child is caught in a rip current?

Stay calm and call or signal for a lifeguard. Do not swim straight back against the current. The rescuer or swimmer should float or tread water, then swim parallel to the shore until free of the pull, and angle back toward the beach. Never enter the surf yourself without a flotation device if you are not a trained rescuer.

Is it safe to let kids play near cars on the beach?

Treat the driving lanes like a street. Keep children out of vehicle lanes, choose a spot away from traffic, and assign an adult to watch the kids and the cars. Vehicles are low speed, but small children are hard for a driver to see, so never let a child run between parked or moving vehicles.

Do swim lessons help before a Florida beach trip?

Yes, but they do not replace supervision. Lessons build the breath control, floating and rolling-over skills that matter most in surf, and our Florida directory lists swim schools you can contact before you travel. Even a strong pool swimmer should wear a life jacket and stay within arm's reach in ocean surf.

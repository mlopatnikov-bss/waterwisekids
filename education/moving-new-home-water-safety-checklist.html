Moving to a New Home Water Safety Checklist (Free Printable)

Many families move over the holidays and the winter break, and a move changes everything at once: a new house, a new yard, a new neighborhood and a new daily routine. The busy days of packing and unpacking are when adults are most distracted and children are exploring places they have never seen. This checklist helps you find and fix water hazards before your children find them, with a free one-page printable to tape inside a kitchen cabinet.

Quick Answer: On moving days, name one adult whose only job is watching the children, and keep doors to bathrooms, the yard and any pool closed. Before children explore the new home, walk every room and the yard for water: bathtubs, toilets, buckets, sump pits, window wells, rain barrels, ponds, hot tubs and pools. Add toilet locks, empty containers, and make sure any pool has a four-sided fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate. In the first week, walk the neighborhood to find retention ponds, creeks and neighbors’ pools, set a no-water-without-an-adult rule, and book a trial at a new swim school. 📦 Why a Move Raises Water Risk

Drowning prevention depends on layers of protection: close supervision, barriers such as locks and fences, swim skills and an emergency plan. A move can remove every layer at the same time. Adults are carrying boxes and talking with movers, doors are propped open, familiar locks and gates are gone, and children are curious about every new room and corner of the yard. Drowning is fast and usually silent, and young children can reach water in the moments an adult looks away. Our guide to the layers of protection explains how each layer backs up the others, and our drowning warning signs card shows why you cannot count on hearing a struggle.

🚚 Moving Day Supervision

Plan supervision for moving days the same way you plan the truck. Name one adult as the children’s watcher for each block of time, and make it their only job; switch with a clear hand-off, as on our water watcher card. If you can, have a relative or sitter care for young children away from the house on the busiest days; our babysitter water safety checklist helps you brief them. Keep the doors to bathrooms, the yard, the garage and any pool area closed, and pack a “first night” box with toilet locks, door knob covers and a flashlight so you can add them right away. Children who tend to wander need extra attention when everything is unfamiliar; our wandering and water safety plan has steps for families of children who elope.

🏠 Inside the New Home

Before children explore, walk every room and look for water. In bathrooms, add toilet locks and keep the door closed or latched, and never leave a child alone in the bathtub, even for a moment; our bathtub safety checklist covers bath time. Empty buckets, mop pails, coolers and pet water bowls large enough to tip into, and store containers upside down. Check the basement or utility room for a sump pit and make sure it has a secure lid. Laundry rooms, utility sinks and indoor fountains or aquariums can hold water too. Our room-by-room home water safety checklist and toddler home water safety checklist go deeper on each room.

🌧️ The Yard and Outdoor Water

Walk the yard before the children play in it. Look for rain barrels without locked lids, window wells without covers, drainage ditches, low spots that flood after rain, birdbaths, decorative ponds and fountains. A new yard may also come with an old kiddie pool or tubs left behind; empty them and store them upside down, as in our kiddie pool safety checklist. Our fall yard water hazards checklist covers many of these, and our flood and storm water safety checklist helps if your new area floods. Check fences and gates around the whole yard, especially if a pond or creek is close by.

🏊 If the New Home Has a Pool or Hot Tub

A pool you did not install comes with barriers you have not checked. Keep children away until you confirm that a four-sided fence separates the pool from the house and yard, the gate closes and latches by itself, the latch is out of a child’s reach and nothing nearby can be used to climb over. Check that drain covers are in place and that a hot tub has a locked safety cover. If the pool is covered for winter, remember that a cover is not a barrier: water can pool on top. Our new pool owner checklist, pool fence and gate inspection checklist, drain safety checklist and hot tub safety checklist walk you through each check, and our guide to pool alarms and safety devices covers extra layers. If you are unsure about any part of the setup, have a pool professional inspect it.

🗺️ Walk the New Neighborhood

In the first week, walk the neighborhood with your children and look for water: retention and stormwater ponds, creeks, drainage ditches, neighbors’ pools, the community or apartment pool and any lake or beach nearby. Retention ponds often sit right behind homes and have steep, slippery banks; our guide to retention pond dangers explains why. See our guides to creek and stream safety and apartment pool safety too. Teach one clear rule: we never go near water without an adult. Introduce yourself to neighbors with pools and ask them to keep gates latched. If the area freezes in winter, our ice safety checklist covers frozen ponds.

🎽 Restart Swim Lessons

A move often means weeks or months without lessons, and skills can fade. Book a trial at a new school soon after you arrive. Bring notes on your child’s current level and the skills they can do, and ask how the new school places children; our switching swim schools checklist, level placement guide and water competency skills checklist help you record and compare. Use our swim school comparison worksheet and questions to ask a swim instructor as you visit. You can also browse swim lessons near your new home. For families moving to Pennsylvania or New Jersey, our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia point you to the official location pages. Lessons lower risk but never make a child drown-proof, so close adult supervision always comes first.

🆘 Your New-Home Emergency Plan

Write your new address on the printable and post it where any caregiver can see it, so you can give it to a 911 dispatcher quickly. If a child is missing, check water first: the bathtub, pool, hot tub, pond and any containers, then the rest of the house and yard. If a child is in trouble in the water, use reach, throw, don’t go and call 911. Find out where the nearest emergency department is and keep a phone near any pool. Taking a CPR class gives you the skills to help while waiting for help; our CPR basics for parents guide and water emergency action plan help you prepare.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is moving day risky for young children around water?

Moving breaks every routine at once. Doors are propped open, adults are busy and distracted, boxes can become steps to reach locks, and children are exploring a place they do not know yet. A bathtub, bucket, pool or pond can be reached in moments, so name one adult to watch the children at all times on moving days.

What should I check for water hazards in a new home?

Walk every room and the yard before the children explore. Look for bathtubs, toilets, buckets, coolers, sump pits, window wells, rain barrels, ponds, fountains, hot tubs and pools. Add toilet locks and bathroom door locks, empty and store containers upside down, and cover or fence anything that holds water.

How do I check a pool or hot tub at a house we just moved into?

Check that a four-sided fence separates the pool from the house and yard, that the gate closes and latches by itself, that the latch is out of a child’s reach and that drain covers are in place. Hot tubs need a locked safety cover. Keep children away until it is secure, and have a pool professional inspect it if you are unsure.

How do I find water hazards in a new neighborhood?

Walk the neighborhood with your children in the first week. Note retention ponds, creeks, drainage ditches, neighbors’ pools and the community pool. Teach a clear rule that children never go near any water without an adult, and ask neighbors with pools to keep their gates latched.

Should we keep swim lessons going after a move?

Yes, if you can. Swim lessons are one layer of protection, and a move often means a long break. Book a trial lesson at a new school soon after you arrive, bring notes on your child’s current level and skills, and ask how the new school places children. Lessons lower risk but never replace close adult supervision.

What should we do if our child goes missing in a new home?

Check water first: the bathtub, pool, hot tub, pond and any containers, then the rest of the house and yard. Call 911 if you cannot find your child within moments. Practice a family plan and know your new address so you can give it to the dispatcher quickly.

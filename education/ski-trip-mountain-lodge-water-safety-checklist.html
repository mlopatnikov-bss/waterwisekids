Ski Trip and Mountain Lodge Water Safety Checklist (Free Printable)

Ski trips and mountain getaways often come with a heated outdoor pool, a steaming hot tub and a frozen pond out back. That water deserves a plan: decks get icy, steam can hide a small swimmer, children are worn out from the slopes and the adults are finally relaxing. This checklist walks you through the lodge pool, the hot tub, a rental cabin’s private spa, frozen water near your lodging and an emergency plan for a place far from home, with a free one-page printable to bring along.

Quick Answer: Before you book, ask the lodge or rental host about the pool and hot tub: hours, whether a lifeguard is on duty (often there is none) and whether the area is fenced with a self-closing, self-latching gate. At the pool, walk slowly on icy, wet decks and keep one adult who is not drinking or on a phone close enough to see through the steam. Follow the hot tub rules: children under 5 stay out, and children 5 to 12 go in only briefly (about 5 minutes) at around 98°F or lower with an adult right beside them. At a rental, keep the hot tub cover locked whenever it is not in use. Treat every frozen pond, creek and lake as off limits unless a trusted adult has confirmed it is safe. Save the lodge address and find the nearest hospital on your first day. Our free printable puts it all on one page.

🗺️ Before You Book: Ask About the Water

Ask the lodge, resort or rental host about the water before you book. Is there a pool or hot tub, and is it indoors or outdoors? What are the hours? Is a lifeguard on duty? Many lodge and condo pools and hot tubs have none and post a “no lifeguard on duty” sign. Is the pool area fenced, with a self-closing, self-latching gate or a door that locks after hours? For a rental, ask whether there is a private hot tub with a locking cover, and whether there is a pond, creek or lake on or next to the property. Pack swimsuits, shoes with grip for icy paths and decks, a warm robe or coat for the walk to the pool, hats and a water bottle. Our vacation rental pool safety checklist and hotel pool safety checklist have more questions to ask.

🏊 Heated Outdoor Pools in the Snow

A heated outdoor pool in the snow is a highlight of many ski trips, and it comes with its own risks. Steam rising off warm water in cold air can make it harder to see a child below the surface, so the watching adult stays close enough to see every child clearly, not across the deck or inside by the window. Splashed water can freeze on the deck and the path from the door, so children walk, never run, and wear shoes with grip until they reach the edge. Before anyone gets in, check that the drain covers are in place and tie up long hair. Keep weaker swimmers within arm’s reach of an adult. Children get cold quickly when they climb out into winter air, so keep swims short and bring them in to dry off and warm up if they start shivering. Our drain entrapment safety checklist, warm-up and cold comfort checklist and night swim safety checklist help with dark evening swims too.

♨️ Hot Tub Rules for Kids

After a cold day outside, the hot tub is tempting for everyone. It is not a warm little pool. Children overheat faster than adults, and hot tubs have strong suction at the drain. Follow the same rules every time: children under 5 stay out; children 5 to 12 go in only briefly, about 5 minutes, with the water around 98°F or lower and an adult right beside them. Lodge hot tubs are usually set hotter than that, so many families keep younger children out of shared tubs altogether. No child is ever in a hot tub alone. Heads stay above water, long hair is tied up and everyone stays away from the drain. Get out at the first sign of flushed skin, dizziness or discomfort, and offer water afterward. Teens follow the posted rules and time limits and use the hot tub with a buddy. Our hot tub and spa safety checklist and full guide to hot tub safety for children explain the age, time and temperature rules.

🏠 A Rental Cabin With a Private Hot Tub

A cabin or condo with its own hot tub means no lifeguard, no posted hours and no staff nearby. On arrival, check that the hot tub cover is in good shape and locks, and that the drain cover is in place and not cracked. If the cover, its locks or the drain cover are missing or broken, tell the host right away and keep children away until it is fixed. Lock the cover every time the hot tub is not in use, even for a quick break, and lock the doors to the deck. A simple door alarm is an easy extra layer. Set one house rule and say it out loud on the first night: no child goes near the hot tub without an adult. Our guide to pool alarms and safety devices and water safety device audit card help you check the layers.

❄️ Frozen Ponds, Creeks and Lakes

Mountain lodges often sit near water: a pond by the lodge, a creek beside the trail or a lake below the cabin. Snow can hide a frozen pond so it looks like a flat field, and creeks, inlets and outlets often have moving water under thin ice. No natural ice is guaranteed safe, and children cannot judge it. Teach one rule: stay off frozen water unless a trusted adult has confirmed it is safe through local authorities or a known, monitored rink. On your first walk around, point out every pond, creek and lake to your children. If someone falls through, call 911 and reach or throw something from solid ground; never go out onto the ice. Our ice safety checklist, guide to cold-water shock and reach, throw, don’t go card cover this in more detail.

👀 One Adult on Watch, Even on Vacation

Vacations loosen routines. Adults are tired, drinks come out after a day on the slopes, and with more grown-ups around it is easy for each one to assume someone else is watching. Name one Water Watcher whenever children are in or near the pool or hot tub: an adult who is not drinking, not on a phone and close enough to see every child. Trade the role in about 15-minute shifts with a clear hand-off. A lifeguard, if there is one, watches the whole pool; your Water Watcher watches your children. Use a buddy for locker room and restroom trips. Our Water Watcher card, guide to why lifeguards don’t replace supervision and locker room safety checklist help you set this up.

😴 Tired Kids After a Day on the Slopes

After a day of skiing, snowboarding or sledding, children are often tired, cold and hungry by the time the pool opens. Tired children are not their strongest swimmers. Plan pool and hot tub time for when they have eaten and rested, keep it short, and offer water, since dry winter air and a warm pool can both leave children thirsty without noticing. If a child seems unusually tired, cold or out of sorts, skip the swim that day. Our snack and hydration checklist has simple ideas.

🎽 Keep Swim Skills Strong All Winter

Winter trips are a reminder that children meet water all year, not just in summer. Swim lessons build the floating, breath control and turning-back-to-the-wall skills that help in any water, and indoor lessons let children keep those skills strong through the winter instead of losing ground before summer. You can browse swim lessons near you or read our guide to winter swim lessons. Families in New Jersey and Pennsylvania can see our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia, which link to the official location pages. Skills lower risk but never replace close adult supervision.

🆘 An Emergency Plan Far From Home

Help can be farther away in the mountains, and cell signal can be weak. On your first day, save the lodge or rental address and unit number in every adult’s phone, find the nearest hospital and urgent care, and note the front desk or host number and whether there is a landline. At the pool, find the emergency phone, the rescue equipment and the AED if there is one. In an emergency, call 911, give the address and follow the dispatcher’s instructions. For a person in the water, reach or throw; don’t go in after them. If a child is missing, check the water first: the pool, the hot tub, then any pond or creek nearby. Our water emergency action plan and CPR basics for parents help you prepare before you leave home.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can kids use the hot tub at a ski lodge?

Children under 5 should stay out of hot tubs. Children 5 to 12 may go in only briefly, about 5 minutes, with the water around 98°F or lower and an adult right beside them. Lodge hot tubs are usually set hotter than that, so many families skip them for younger children. No child should ever be in a hot tub alone, and anyone should get out at the first sign of flushed skin, dizziness or discomfort.

Do ski lodge pools and hot tubs have lifeguards?

Many do not. Lodge, resort and condo pools and hot tubs often post a sign saying no lifeguard is on duty. Ask before you go and read the posted rules. Even where a lifeguard is on duty, a parent or another named adult should watch their own children in and near the water.

Are heated outdoor pools safe for kids in winter?

They can be, with a few extra steps. Steam rising off warm water in cold air can make it harder to see a child below the surface, so the watching adult stays close. Splashed water can freeze on the deck, so children walk and wear shoes with grip between the door and the pool. Keep swims short, and bring children in to dry off and warm up if they start shivering.

How do I keep kids safe around a rental cabin’s private hot tub?

Check on arrival that the hot tub has a cover that locks and a drain cover that is in place and not cracked. Keep the cover locked whenever the hot tub is not in use, lock the doors to the deck, and set a house rule that no child goes near the hot tub without an adult. Tell the host right away if the cover, locks or drain cover are missing or broken, and keep children away until it is fixed.

Is it safe for kids to walk on a frozen pond near our cabin?

No natural ice is guaranteed safe, and children cannot judge it. Snow can hide a frozen pond so it looks like a flat field, and creeks and inlets often have moving water under thin ice. The safest family rule is to stay off frozen water unless a trusted adult has confirmed it is safe through local authorities or a known, monitored rink.

What should we do in an emergency at a mountain lodge?

Call 911, give the lodge address and unit number, and follow the dispatcher’s instructions. Send someone to alert the front desk and bring the AED if there is one. For a person in the water or through the ice, reach or throw something from a safe spot; don’t go in after them. If a child is missing, check the water first: the pool, the hot tub, then any pond or creek nearby.

🗣️ Why a Cue Word Card Helps

Many children start swim lessons while they are still learning English, or speak one language at home and another at school. That is not a barrier to learning to swim. Beginner lessons are taught mostly with the body: the instructor shows a skill, guides the child through it and repeats a few short cue words. What helps is making sure your child already knows those few words, and the safety words, in both languages before the first class. Our full guide to bilingual and ESL swim lessons explains why a language gap is not a learning gap. This card turns that advice into one page you can fill in, practice at home and share with the instructor.

🛑 Start With the Safety Words

Learn the safety words before anything else, because they are the ones a child must respond to right away. Stop, wait, hold the wall and get out come up in almost every beginner class, and ask first belongs in every home. Write each one next to the word your family uses, say them together and practice until your child reacts without thinking: freezing on stop, staying seated on the edge on wait, reaching for the edge on hold the wall and climbing out on get out. Tell the instructor which home-language words your child already obeys, so they can use them too if the class allows it. Our pool deck rules checklist and family water safety pledge are good ways to practice the rules behind these words.

🏊 Learn the Lesson Cue Words in Both Languages

Instructors rely on a short set of cue words that they repeat all lesson: blow bubbles, kick, reach, big arms, chin up, float on your back, ready, go and climb out. They vary by swim school, so on the first day ask the instructor which five or six words they use most and add them to the card. Fill in your home-language word beside each one. Many families find it easiest for one parent to say the English word and the other to repeat it in the home language. Our swim level translator and water competency skills checklist help you describe the skills behind these words, whichever language you use at home.

👋 Pair Each Word With the Same Gesture

A gesture works in every language. Many instructors use simple signals: a hand held up for stop, a flat hand pressed down for wait, pointing at or patting the wall, puffed cheeks for blow bubbles and a thumb pointing up out of the pool for get out. Ask the instructor which gestures they use and copy them exactly at home, so your child sees the same signal from everyone. Use the gesture column on the card to sketch or describe each one. When a word, a gesture and an action always go together, children connect them quickly.

❓ Questions to Ask the Swim School

You do not need a school that teaches in your home language, but a few questions help you find a good fit. Ask whether any instructors speak your home language. Ask how they teach a child who does not yet understand the instructor’s words; a confident school will describe demonstration, hands-on guidance and modeling by other swimmers. Ask which cue words and gestures they use, whether progress updates and safety information can be given in writing so you can translate them at home, and whether a parent can watch or be close by during the first lessons. Our swim instructor questions checklist and swim lesson enrollment checklist cover the rest of what to ask.

📅 The First Few Lessons

Bring the filled-in card to the first lesson and share the swimmer profile with the instructor: your child’s name as they like to hear it, the languages they speak, the words they already know and anything that helps them feel comfortable. Arrive a little early so your child can watch a class and see the gestures before getting in. After class, ask your child to show you one new word and its action, and write it on the card. Our parent-instructor check-in card, after-class debrief checklist and first swim lesson guide help keep everyone on the same page.

🛁 Practice the Words Safely at Home

Most cue words can be practiced with no water at all: kicking on the bed, reaching arms at the table, blowing bubbles into a cup of water or freezing on stop during a game. At bath time, an adult stays within arm’s reach the whole time and never steps away, even for a moment. Keep practice playful and short, and skip breath-holding contests or “who can stay under longest” games. Praise the action, not just the word. Our water safety activities at home, bathtub safety checklist and what to say to encourage your swimmer have more ideas.

🎽 Swim Lessons Build Skills in Any Language

Steady weekly lessons give a bilingual child the same thing they give every child: repetition, a familiar instructor and a clear next skill. Indoor lessons keep that routine going all winter. You can browse swim lessons near you or read our guide to winter swim lessons. Families in New Jersey and Pennsylvania can see our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia, which link to the official location pages; ask the location directly about instructor languages. Skills lower risk but never replace close adult supervision.

🆘 An Emergency Plan for Multilingual Homes

Make sure every adult who cares for your child at home, including grandparents and sitters who may not speak English, can get help fast. Write the home address in English next to the phone, along with the words a caller may need. In the United States, 911 call centers can usually connect a telephone interpreter: stay on the line, say the name of your language in English and wait. If a child is missing, check the water first: the bathtub, pool, hot tub and any nearby water. If a child is in trouble in the water, get them out if you can do so safely, call 911 or have someone call, and follow the dispatcher’s instructions, including sending someone for an AED if one is nearby. Name one adult as the Water Watcher whenever children are in or near water, and encourage every regular caregiver to take a CPR class. Our water emergency action plan, Water Watcher card and CPR basics for parents help you prepare.

Frequently Asked Questions

What English words does my child need to know for swim lessons?

Only a small set. Beginner lessons use a few short cue words again and again, such as blow bubbles, kick, reach, big arms, chin up and ready, go, plus safety words such as stop, wait, hold the wall and get out. Instructors pair each word with a demonstration or gesture, so most children link the sound to the action within a lesson or two. Ask the instructor which five or six words they use most and practice those.

Should we practice swim cue words in English or our home language?

Both. Write each English cue word next to the word your family uses at home and say them together with the same gesture, so your child learns that both words mean the same action. What matters most is consistency: the same word, the same gesture and the same response every time.

Which safety words matter most for a child who is learning English?

Stop, wait, hold the wall and get out matter most, because a child needs to respond to them right away in the pool. Practice them in both languages until your child reacts without thinking, tell the instructor which home-language words your child already obeys, and keep the rule that the child always asks an adult before going near water.

What should bilingual families ask a swim school?

Ask whether any instructors speak your home language, how they teach a child who does not yet understand the instructor’s words, which cue words and gestures they use, whether progress updates and safety information can be given in writing, and whether a parent can watch or be close by during the first lessons. A good school will describe demonstration, hands-on guidance and modeling by other swimmers.

Can a child who is still learning English be safe in swim lessons?

Yes. Beginner swim skills are taught mostly by showing and guiding, and instructors keep beginners close. The key is making sure your child responds to the safety words and that the instructor knows which words your child understands. Swim lessons are one layer of protection and never replace close adult supervision around water.

What if a caregiver at home does not speak English and needs to call 911?

Post the home address written in English next to the phone, along with the words the caller needs, such as the child is not breathing. In the United States, 911 call centers can usually connect a telephone interpreter: stay on the line, say the name of your language in English and wait. Make sure every caregiver knows how to call 911 from the home phone and from a locked cell phone.

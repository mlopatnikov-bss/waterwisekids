Swim Team Readiness Scorecard

A swim team tryout does not test whether your child likes the water. It tests four specific things, and a child can be strong at three of them and still have a miserable first season because of the fourth. The awkward part is that you can measure every one of them yourself, at a normal open-swim visit, weeks before tryout day — no stopwatch, no coach, no pressure on your child. This sheet is those four gates, written as things you watch rather than things you ask your child about, plus the one honest answer most families need: not yet, and here is what closes the gap.

🎯 Score This at a Normal Pool Visit — Not at Tryouts

  • Pick an ordinary swim day. A child being watched for a verdict swims worse than a child playing. Go to open swim, bring this sheet, and say nothing about scoring.
  • Watch, do not quiz. Every gate below is written as something you can see from the deck. Asking “can you swim freestyle?” gets you an answer about confidence, not ability.
  • Score each gate Yes, Almost, or Not Yet. There is no total to hit. The pattern across the four gates is the decision, and it is explained on the back of this sheet.
  • Re-score after eight to twelve weeks, not after one lesson. Stroke changes need repetition before they show up under fatigue, which is the only place a tryout looks.
Why a parent can do this at all: coaches are not looking for fast at a novice tryout — they are looking for a swimmer who can be coached in a lane without needing a rescue. Every one of those signals is visible to a parent who knows what to look for.

🏊 Gate 1 — Can They Swim the Strokes a Tryout Asks For?

  • Freestyle with the face in the water, breathing to the side rather than lifting the head straight forward. Head-up freestyle is the single most common reason a swimmer is placed in a pre-team group instead of the team.
  • Backstroke on their back the whole length, without rolling over to check where they are or sitting up to look.
  • A recognizable kick that goes somewhere. Legs near the surface, small fast kick — not a bicycle kick with the body sinking at forty-five degrees.
  • They can start from the wall and finish at the wall without a push from an adult and without stopping in the middle.
  • Butterfly and breaststroke are usually not required to try out for a novice group — most teams teach them once your child is on the team. Confirm this with the coach before you spend a season worrying about it.
The gate is legality-lite, not speed. A novice tryout generally wants freestyle and backstroke that a coach can recognize and correct. Ask the specific team which strokes and what distance they ask for; it varies, and a coach will tell you plainly if you ask before tryout day.

📏 Gate 2 — Can They Keep Going Without the Wall?

  • They swim a continuous distance without standing up, without grabbing the lane rope, and without hanging on the wall to recover mid-length.
  • They can do it twice. The second effort, a minute after the first, is the one that predicts practice — the first tells you almost nothing.
  • They are tired at the end, not distressed. Breathing hard and grinning is ready. Panicky, coughing, or climbing out is a clear not yet.
  • They can get from deep water back to the wall unhurried, after a rest, in water over their head.
  • Ask the team the actual number. Required tryout distance differs between programs; guessing at it is how families over-prepare for one team and under-prepare for another.
Never test endurance by having your child hold their breath or swim underwater for distance. The American Red Cross, USA Swimming and the YMCA all prohibit prolonged breath-holding and hypoxic training, including as a game. It can cause a blackout in a strong swimmer with no warning and no struggle. If a program you are considering runs breath-holding contests, that is information about the program.

🧠 Gate 3 — Can They Take Coaching in a Shared Lane?

  • They can listen to an instruction on the wall and then do it — not perfectly, but visibly attempting the change on the next length.
  • They can wait their turn and go when sent, rather than pushing off the moment the swimmer ahead leaves.
  • They manage their own goggles, cap and water bottle without a parent on the deck for each one.
  • They can be corrected without shutting down. Practice is a continuous stream of corrections; a child who reads every correction as failure has a hard season regardless of how fast they swim.
  • They can be at the pool without you in sight. Most teams do not want parents on deck, and for many children this is the real hurdle, not the swimming.
This gate is the one lessons quietly build. Group lesson time — taking turns, following a set, being corrected by an instructor who is not a parent — is the same skill a coach needs on day one. A child who has only ever swum at family pool days may be a strong swimmer and still score Not Yet here.

❤️ Gate 4 — Whose Idea Is This?

  • Your child can say what they want out of it in their own words — friends, racing, getting better, a specific stroke. Any answer counts. “I don't know, you signed me up” does not.
  • They know what they are agreeing to: the number of practices a week, that practice is mostly laps, and that meets are long days with short swims.
  • They have seen a practice, not just heard about one. Most teams will let you watch. Thirty minutes on a bench answers more than a month of conversation.
  • The family can genuinely carry the schedule — the drive, the cost, the weekend meets, and what it does to a sibling's calendar. This is a family gate, not a child gate.
  • There is an agreed exit. “We will finish this season, then decide” is a promise a child can hold on a hard Tuesday.
The most common false positive: a child who loves swimming and hates practicing. Loving the water is not the same as wanting to swim back and forth for ninety minutes. Watching one real practice separates these two better than any question you can ask.

🚩 What “Not Yet” Actually Means

  • It is a timing verdict, not a talent verdict — and it has a shelf life of weeks, not years. Write the re-score date on the table above before you leave the pool.
  • Gate 1 gaps close with instruction, not with more pool time. Head-up freestyle and a sinking kick are technique faults; unsupervised laps tend to groove them deeper rather than fix them.
  • Gate 2 gaps close with instruction too, more often than families expect — a child who cannot finish a length usually has a breathing or body-position problem, not a fitness problem.
  • Do not “train” for a tryout at home. Timed sprints and endurance drills from a parent on the deck raise anxiety and change nothing a coach will see.
  • Tell your child the plan, in their words. “We are going to work on your breathing for a few months and try out in the spring” lands very differently from silence after a pool visit.
This is the honest version of the answer. Most children who are not ready for team need more taught swimming — a structured class with an instructor correcting stroke under mild fatigue. That is exactly what a progressive lesson program does, and it is why a “not yet” on this sheet is usually a lesson enrollment rather than a closed door.

🗣️ Ask the Coach These Before Tryout Day

  • “Which strokes and what distance does the tryout actually ask for?” Get the number. It is the single most useful sentence in this conversation.
  • “Is there a pre-team or developmental group?” Many programs have one, it is where most not-quite-ready swimmers belong, and it is frequently not advertised.
  • “How many practices a week is the novice group, and is partial attendance allowed?” Ask before you commit the family calendar, not after.
  • “What does a season cost, all in?” Dues, suit, registration, meet fees and travel. Ask for the total, not the monthly figure.
  • “Can we watch a practice first?” A no to this question is itself worth knowing.
  • “What is your policy on breath-holding and underwater work?” You are looking for a coach who answers this immediately and correctly.

If a Gate Is Still Open, Lessons Are How It Closes

A gate scored “not yet” is a coaching gap, not a verdict. Formal, progressive swim lessons build the endurance, stroke control, wall work and pool-safety habits this scorecard tests — and are associated with a substantially lower drowning risk for young children.

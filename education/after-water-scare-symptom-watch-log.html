After a Water Scare: Symptoms and When to Seek Care

Persistent or worsening symptoms need prompt care. A symptom log records information for a clinician; it cannot establish that a child is safe.

After a water scare: act on symptoms

What matters is the child’s breathing and behavior, not a label or a countdown. If symptoms persist or you are unsure the child has returned to normal, seek medical advice promptly.

When to call 911

Call 911 for severe or labored breathing, blue or gray color, unresponsiveness, confusion with breathing distress, or a child who is difficult to wake. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the dispatcher. Anyone who needed CPR or rescue breaths after drowning needs emergency medical care.

Drowning terms and myths

No. “Secondary,” “dry,” “delayed” and “near” drowning are not accepted medical diagnoses. Drowning means breathing impairment caused by immersion or submersion in liquid, and its outcome can be fatal or nonfatal. Breathing problems after a real drowning incident require medical attention.

No fixed home observation window

There is no fixed home countdown that proves a child is safe. Hospital observation guidance is not a home clearance rule. If a clinician advises home observation, follow the individual discharge instructions. Seek care when symptoms appear or worsen; do not wait for a timer or the next log entry.

Symptoms that need prompt medical care

Persistent or worsening cough, fast or difficult breathing, chest pain, unusual sleepiness or confusion after a water incident need prompt medical attention. Do not wait until later in the day or for symptoms to become severe. Call 911 for severe breathing distress, blue or gray color, unresponsiveness or difficulty waking.

When a brief cough resolves

A brief cough or sputter that resolves promptly, followed by completely normal breathing and behavior, can be observed by an attentive caregiver. Persistent or worsening cough, rapid or difficult breathing, unusual sleepiness or confusion require prompt medical care. If unsure whether your child is back to normal, contact a medical professional.

Recording breathing for a clinician

If a clinician has advised home observation, ask what to record and how often. You can note breathing effort, cough, color and behavior. A breathing count or apparently stable log cannot rule out injury. Do not delay care to measure or complete the form.

Sleep and unusual drowsiness

Sleep does not cause delayed drowning. Follow individual medical instructions about sleep and observation after an actual drowning incident. Unusual sleepiness, confusion or difficulty waking must not be dismissed as ordinary tiredness; difficulty waking is an emergency.

What to tell the clinician

Record the time and setting of the incident, approximate submersion time if known, breathing and responsiveness afterward, symptoms and their changes, and any rescue breaths or CPR. Record observations without delaying a call for care.

Review supervision and barriers

After immediate medical needs are addressed, review how the incident happened. Improve attentive supervision, barriers, appropriate life jackets and water-safety training. Avoid blaming a child for a lapse in protection.

Returning to swimming

After a drowning injury or medical evaluation, follow the clinician’s advice about returning to swimming. Resume gently when medically appropriate. Lessons add a layer of protection but never replace supervision, barriers or life jackets.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is secondary drowning a medical diagnosis?

No. “Secondary,” “dry,” “delayed” and “near” drowning are not accepted medical diagnoses. Drowning means breathing impairment caused by immersion or submersion in liquid, and its outcome can be fatal or nonfatal. Breathing problems after a real drowning incident require medical attention.

Can a well child suddenly drown days after swimming?

The Red Cross reports no evidence for the idea that a child who is completely well after swimming unexpectedly dies of drowning days later. This does not dismiss real symptoms: persistent or worsening cough, breathing difficulty, or abnormal behavior after a water incident need prompt medical care.

Which symptoms mean call 911?

Call 911 for severe or labored breathing, blue or gray color, unresponsiveness, confusion with breathing distress, or a child who is difficult to wake. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the dispatcher. Anyone who needed CPR or rescue breaths after drowning needs emergency medical care.

What if my child coughs briefly and then seems normal?

Persistent or worsening cough, rapid or difficult breathing, unusual sleepiness or confusion require prompt medical care. If unsure whether your child is back to normal, contact a medical professional.

The End-of-Summer Swim Skills Report Card: Score Your Child Before Fall

Summer is winding down, and it's the perfect moment to ask a simple question: where do your child's water skills actually stand right now? 📅 Why Score Swim Skills at the End of Summer?

Late summer is the single best time to take stock of your child's water skills, because swimming is a perishable skill that fades over the long fall-and-winter break — and the fade is fastest in new, young, and nervous swimmers. A child who was floating and paddling confidently in July can be noticeably rustier after just a few weeks out of the water. Capture where they are now, at their summer peak, and you can protect that progress instead of watching it drain away by next spring.

This matters because the stakes are real. Drowning is the leading cause of unintentional injury death for children ages 1 to 4 in the United States, according to the CDC, and remains a leading cause for older kids. Knowing exactly which skills your child has — and which they don't — is far more useful than a vague sense that they “can kind of swim.” Our guide to the five water competency skills explains what real safety in the water looks like.

✅ How the Report Card Works

Instead of asking “can your child swim?”, the report card has you watch ten specific, observable skills and score each one as Not Yet (red), Getting There (yellow), or Solid (green). That traffic-light system turns a fuzzy judgment into a clear picture. A row of greens with one stubborn yellow tells you precisely where to aim; a page of reds tells you your child is at the very beginning and needs foundational, survival-first lessons. Either way, you leave with information you can act on.

The ten skills are grouped into three stages that build on each other:

🌊 Stage 1: Comfort (the foundation)

Before any survival skill can develop, a child has to be comfortable and relaxed in the water. The report card scores three foundational skills here: putting their face in the water and blowing bubbles, submerging fully and coming back up calmly, and getting in and out of the water safely with confidence. These look basic, but they're the bedrock — a panicked child can't float or reach the wall. If this stage is mostly red, that's completely normal for beginners and toddlers, and it's the right place to focus. Our guides on getting kids comfortable in water and overcoming water fear can help.

🦺 Stage 2: Survival Skills (the life-savers)

These are the skills that actually keep a child safer after an unexpected fall into water. The report card scores four: a front float, an independent back float, rolling from front to back to breathe, and returning to and holding the wall. Floating is the most important survival skill of all — it buys time, saves energy, and replaces panic with a plan. Our explainers on how to teach a child to float and why floating matters so much go deeper, and the swim-float-swim method shows how instructors build these abilities.

🏊 Stage 3: Water Competency (the goal)

The final three skills make up the American Red Cross definition of water competency, done as one sequence: jump or step into water over their head and return to the surface, float or tread water for one full minute, and swim about 25 yards to an exit and climb out (without the ladder if in a pool). Most children reach this stage between ages 4 and 6 with consistent, quality lessons — but many take longer, which is completely normal. If your child is solidly green across all ten skills, celebrate it, then keep practicing so it holds. If they're not there yet, this shows you exactly how far along the path they are.

🔍 Reading Your Child's Scores

The value of the report card is the pattern, not a single number. Mostly reds means your child is a true beginner — the priority is comfort and foundational survival skills, ideally with a survival-first swim program. A mix of greens and yellows means real progress with clear next steps; circle the first yellow or red skill and make it this fall's focus. Mostly greens means your child is water competent for their level — the job now is maintenance, because skills fade without practice.

One caution the report card repeats, and we will too: no child is ever drown-proof. Even a page full of greens still requires constant, undistracted supervision and every other layer of protection — barriers, life jackets for weak swimmers, and knowing the quiet, fast signs of drowning.

🍂 What to Do With the Results This Fall

The most important move after scoring your child is to keep lessons going into the fall — especially if any skill came up red or yellow. Fall is when summer's momentum is most often lost: families stop lessons, the pool closes, and hard-won skills quietly slip. Continuing through the cooler months keeps skills sharp, closes the gaps the report card revealed, and means your child starts next summer ahead instead of relearning. Fall sessions also tend to have smaller classes and more availability. Our guide to why year-round swim lessons matter and the year-round swim skills checklist explain how to keep progress steady.

According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, formal swim lessons can reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1 to 4. Look for a survival-first program — our guide to choosing swim lessons for kids covers what to look for — and use your report card in the first class so the instructor knows exactly where to begin.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do kids lose swimming skills over the summer or a break?

Yes. Swimming is a perishable, motor-memory skill, and it fades without regular practice — fastest in new, young, and nervous swimmers. A child who looked confident in July can be noticeably rustier after just a few weeks away from the water, and the gap widens over a long fall and winter with no lessons. That is exactly why an end-of-summer check-in is useful: it captures where your child really is before the break, so a small gap doesn't quietly become a big one by next spring. The fix is not panic but consistency: keeping some form of lessons or supervised practice going through the off-season.

How do I know what swim level my child is really at?

Watch them perform specific, observable skills rather than asking 'can you swim?' The report card breaks it into ten skills across three stages: comfort (face in the water, blowing bubbles, submerging), survival (front float, independent back float, rolling to breathe, returning to the wall), and competency (jumping in and surfacing, floating or treading for one minute, and swimming about 25 yards then climbing out). Score each as Not Yet, Getting There, or Solid. The pattern of greens, yellows, and reds tells you your child's true level far more accurately than a single label, and it shows you the exact next skill to work on.

Should I keep my child in swim lessons in the fall?

For most children, yes — especially if the report card shows any red or yellow skills. Summer lessons build momentum, and stopping in the fall is when hard-won skills quietly slip away. Year-round or continued fall lessons keep skills sharp, close the gaps the report card reveals, and mean your child starts next summer ahead instead of relearning. Cooler months also tend to have smaller class sizes and more availability. Think of fall as the season that protects the progress summer bought you, not an optional add-on.

What water skills should a child have by the end of summer?

It depends heavily on age and how long they've had lessons, so the report card is a progress map, not a pass-fail deadline. Broadly: toddlers and preschoolers should be building comfort and foundational survival skills like blowing bubbles, floating with support, and rolling onto their back to breathe; school-age children with consistent lessons are often working toward true water competency — jumping in, surfacing and floating for a minute, turning to find the edge, swimming roughly 25 yards, and climbing out without the ladder. No child is ever 'drown-proof,' so every skill level still requires constant supervision and layers of protection.

Does my child still need supervision if they scored well?

Absolutely. A strong report card lowers risk but never removes it. Skills that look solid in a warm, calm pool can fail in cold water, in clothing, in currents, or when a child panics — and no child is ever drown-proof. Water safety always relies on layers: constant, undistracted adult supervision, barriers like four-sided pool fencing, life jackets for weak swimmers and open water, and swimming skill. Use the report card to celebrate progress and target practice, not as a reason to relax your watch.

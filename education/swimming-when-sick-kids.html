Can Your Child Swim When Sick? A Parent's Guide

A runny nose does not always mean canceling swim lessons, but some illnesses absolutely require staying out of the pool. Here is a symptom-by-symptom guide for parents.

Quick Answer: Skip swim lessons with diarrhea, vomiting, fever or pink eye. If your child is diagnosed with Cryptosporidium (Crypto), wait two weeks after the diarrhea has completely stopped. Stay out with an open wound, especially after surgery. For a new cold or other respiratory illness, skip group lessons and follow the return guidance below. Ask your child’s clinician and the pool about restrictions.

When can a sick child swim, symptom by symptom?

Skip group lessons when your child has a new respiratory illness, even without a fever. Check the symptom-specific guidance below and follow your clinician’s advice and the swim program’s sick-day policy.

  • New cold symptoms: Skip group lessons while sick. Check the respiratory guidance below before returning.
  • Fever: Keep your child out of the pool.
  • Diarrhea: Absolutely no swimming — this is the most important rule.
  • Vomiting or stomach bug: Stay out until fully recovered.
  • Ear infection: Depends on the type — see below.
  • Pink eye (conjunctivitis): Avoid pools while your child has pink eye.
  • Open wounds, rashes, or skin infections: Keep out to protect your child and others.

The rest of this guide explains the why behind these calls so you can make confident decisions.

How Long Should a Child Stay Out of the Pool?

Use this table as a starting point. Follow any illness-specific advice from your child’s clinician and check the pool’s return policy.

One pattern runs through the whole table: the wait exists to protect other swimmers at least as much as your own child. A pool is shared water, and a single accident can sicken a dozen families. When a symptom is not on this list, the safe default is to stay out and call the pediatrician.

Why is swimming with diarrhea such a hard no?

Keep your child out of the water while they have diarrhea. If your child is diagnosed with Cryptosporidium (Crypto), wait two weeks after the diarrhea has completely stopped. Germs such as Crypto can survive for days in properly treated pool water. See CDC guidance on preventing diarrheal illnesses.

See our guides to preventing recreational water illness and public pool hygiene.

What about ear infections?

Swimming advice depends on the diagnosis and whether your child has ear pain, drainage, tubes or a ruptured eardrum. Ask your child’s clinician if the ear should stay dry and whether protection is appropriate.

See our guides to swimming with ear tubes or an ear infection and preventing swimmer’s ear.

Should a child swim with a cold or fever?

If your child has new cold symptoms, skip group lessons. CDC guidance for respiratory viruses says to wait until symptoms are improving overall and your child has been fever-free without fever-reducing medicine for at least 24 hours. Check the additional precautions in the respiratory section before returning to group lessons.

How do I help my child get back to swimming?

When your child is on the mend, ease back in with a shorter, lower-intensity session, good hydration, and thorough ear drying afterward. Start with a shorter, lower-intensity session, make sure they are well-hydrated, and dry their ears thoroughly afterward. Good post-swim habits speed recovery and prevent new problems — see our guide to post-swim care for kids.

When in doubt about any symptom, the safest move is to keep your child out of the water and call your pediatrician. Missing one lesson is a minor setback; spreading an illness or worsening an infection is not. A short rest almost always gets your child back to the pool faster and healthier. Make-up credits vary widely between programs, which is worth checking before you enroll — our swim school directory lists options in every state. If you would rather have the whole symptom list on one page, our printable sick-day swim decision card puts every call below on a single sheet for the fridge.

Can a child swim with pink eye?

The CDC advises avoiding pools while your child has pink eye. Ask your child’s clinician when swimming can resume and follow the pool’s policy. Avoid sharing towels or goggles. See CDC pink eye prevention guidance.

What about rashes, wounds, and skin infections?

Stay out with an open cut or wound, especially after surgery or a piercing. If your child does swim, completely cover the wound with a waterproof bandage and follow any clinician or facility restrictions. Ask your child’s clinician about unexplained rashes or skin infections.

  • Stitches, staples or surgical glue: Follow your surgical team’s instructions about when the area can get wet.
  • Impetigo: Ask your child’s clinician when it is appropriate to resume swimming and check the pool’s policy.
  • Hand, foot and mouth disease: Keep out while blisters are open or weeping and while the child has a fever. The virus is spread mainly by stool and by fluid from the blisters, not by pool water itself, but shared surfaces and close contact make swim class a poor choice during the contagious window.
  • Ringworm and athlete's foot: Not spread through properly treated pool water, but readily spread on wet decks and shared changing-room floors. Treat, cover if advised, and use pool shoes.
  • Molluscum contagiosum: Children can generally swim with lesions covered by a watertight dressing or swimwear. Avoid sharing towels and kickboards.
  • Eczema: Not contagious and not a reason to stay out. Chlorine can dry and irritate the skin, so rinse before and after, and apply an emollient afterward. Many children with eczema swim comfortably all season with that routine.
  • Head lice: Not a swimming risk. Lice cannot be spread through pool water; the caution is shared towels, hats, and helmets on the deck.

Swimmer's itch — an itchy rash from parasites in some freshwater lakes — is not contagious and is a reason to change lakes rather than to stop swimming. Our guide to lake and ocean safety covers where it tends to occur.

What about COVID-19, flu, RSV, and croup?

If your child has new cold symptoms, skip group lessons. CDC guidance for respiratory viruses says to wait until symptoms are improving overall and your child has been fever-free without fever-reducing medicine for at least 24 hours. The CDC’s respiratory virus guidance also recommends taking extra precautions around others for five days after resuming normal activities. If the class setup makes the recommended precautions impractical, consider postponing the group lesson.

Skip swimming if your child is wheezing or having trouble breathing and seek medical advice.

Does pool chlorine kill everything?

No. Correctly maintained chlorine kills most germs within minutes, but Cryptosporidium survives for more than seven days even in a properly chlorinated pool — which is why the diarrhea rule exists. Understanding this gap is what makes the rest of the guidance make sense to parents who assume chlorine handles everything.

If your child is diagnosed with Cryptosporidium (Crypto), wait two weeks after the diarrhea has completely stopped. Read the CDC’s full guidance.

People can also spread some infections through close contact or shared equipment. Follow the pool’s illness policy and avoid sharing towels.

Swim Lesson Encouragement Guide: What to Say (and Skip) Before, During and After Class (Free Printable)

The words you use around swim lessons shape how your child feels about the water. Here are simple, effort-based phrases for before, during and after class, calm scripts for nervous moments and a free one-page card.

Quick Answer: Keep your words short, calm and specific. Before class, say what will happen and who is in charge. During class, let the instructor lead and save coaching for later. After class, praise effort and one specific thing your child tried, then ask an open question. Skip bribes, comparisons and “don’t be scared.” Words never replace supervision: stay close to young children near water. Our free printable card puts the phrases on one page.

💬 Why Your Words Matter

Children read a lot into how a parent talks about the water. Calm, short and positive language tells them swim class is a normal, safe-feeling part of the week, while worry, pressure or big promises can make a nervous child tense up. You do not need perfect scripts. A few steady phrases, used the same way each class day, are enough. Use this guide with our parent-instructor check-in card and after-class debrief checklist to build a simple routine.

🏠 Before Class: Set Up the Day

Tell your child what will happen in plain words: who is in charge, where you will be and when you will see them. For example, “Today you swim with your teacher. I will be right here, watching.” Keep the same routine each week, including the snack, the bag and the walk to the pool. Skip long pep talks and promises such as “You’re going to swim all by yourself today.” Our night-before prep checklist and swim lesson day checklist help the morning go smoothly.

👀 During Class: Let the Instructor Lead

Once class starts, the instructor is the one giving directions. Mixed messages from the viewing area can pull a child’s attention away and confuse them. A smile or a thumbs up is plenty. Keep your phone down and your eyes on the class, and follow the school’s rules about where parents sit and when a parent may step in. If your child looks upset, give the instructor a moment to help first. See our observation day etiquette guide and pool deck rules checklist.

🌟 After Class: Praise Effort

Start with a warm greeting and a towel, then name one specific thing you saw: “I saw you kick strong and put your chin in the water.” Praising effort and trying keeps the focus on learning, which suits swimmers who are still building skills. Then ask an open question, such as “What was your favorite part?” or “What felt tricky?” Avoid grading the lesson, correcting technique or comparing your child with someone else. If the class did not go well, a simple “That sounded hard. I’m proud you stayed and tried” is enough. Our reward chart and progress plateau guide help you celebrate small steps.

😟 When Your Child Is Nervous

Name the feeling and stay calm: “It looks like the water feels big today.” Ask what part feels hard, share the answer with the instructor and follow the school’s plan for easing nervous swimmers into the water. Keep goodbyes short and steady, and avoid sneaking away. Take small steps, such as sitting on the edge or watching a friend first, if the instructor agrees. Our reluctance plan, separation anxiety plan and overcoming water fear checklist offer more ideas.

🚫 Phrases to Skip

Try to avoid “Don’t be scared,” “Big kids don’t cry,” “Look how well your friend is doing” and “You have to swim today or no treat.” Also skip anything that suggests lessons make a child safe, such as “You can’t drown now.” Swim lessons build skills, but they never make a child drown-proof, and a child is never alone in or near water. Stay within arm’s reach of young children, keep your attention on them and use layers of protection such as fences, life jackets and a designated water watcher. Frequently Asked Questions

What should I say to my child before a swim lesson?

Keep it short, calm and predictable. Say what will happen, who is in charge and when you will see them again, for example: “You will swim with your teacher, and I will be right here watching from the window.” Avoid long pep talks, promises about what your child must do and phrases like “don’t be scared,” which can make a nervous child focus on the worry.

What is the best way to praise a child after swim class?

Praise effort and one specific thing, such as “I saw you blow bubbles and put your face in the water.” Effort-based praise keeps the focus on trying, which helps children who are still learning. Avoid comparing your child with other children, and skip rating the whole lesson. Follow with an open question like “What was your favorite part?”

Is it okay to bribe a child to go to swim lessons?

It is better to avoid rewards that depend on performing a skill or on getting in the water, because they can add pressure. Many families do better with a predictable routine, a small treat that is part of every class day regardless of how it went, or a simple chart for showing up and trying. Our reward chart printable has ideas you can adapt.

What if my child says they don’t want to go to swim lessons?

Stay calm, name the feeling and listen: “It sounds like the water feels big today.” Then ask what part feels hard, share it with the instructor and follow the school’s plan for helping nervous swimmers. A short, kind routine usually works better than arguing. If reluctance continues, our reluctance plan and separation anxiety plan have steps to try.

Do encouraging words replace supervision near water?

No. Encouragement does not replace adult supervision, and swim lessons do not make a child drown-proof at any age. Stay within arm’s reach of a young child near water, keep your attention on your child rather than your phone and follow your school’s rules for where parents watch and when they step in.

Swimmer's Ear in Kids: Prevention Checklist (and What to Do If It Happens)

Every summer, swim instructors watch it happen: a child who loves the water misses two weeks of lessons because of an aching, itchy ear. Swimmer's ear is one of the most common — and most preventable — reasons kids sit out of the pool. It's caused by water trapped in the ear canal, not by a cold, which means a simple after-swim routine can stop it before it starts. This free printable checklist gives you the drain-dry-protect habits that keep your child's ears healthy and their swim lessons on track all season.

👂 What is swimmer's ear — and why does it sideline so many kids?

Swimmer's ear, or otitis externa, is an infection of the outer ear canal — the tube leading from the eardrum to the outside of the head. It develops when water stays trapped in the canal after swimming or bathing, creating a warm, moist place where bacteria multiply. This is different from the middle-ear infection kids get after a cold: that one sits behind the eardrum, while swimmer's ear affects the canal itself. A quick tell is that swimmer's ear usually hurts more when you gently tug the earlobe or press the little bump in front of the ear. Because it's tied to water exposure, it peaks in summer and among children who swim often — which is exactly why a prevention routine matters for active swimmers. It is also common: analyzing national data from 2003–2007, the CDC estimated that about 2.4 million U.S. health-care visits a year end in a diagnosis of acute otitis externa (MMWR, May 20, 2011;60(19):605–9).

Wondering if it will clear by itself? Read can swimmer's ear go away on its own.

The good news is that swimmer's ear is highly preventable, and preventing it protects something bigger than a comfortable ear: it protects your child's consistent swim lessons. Formal lessons are one of the strongest layers of protection against drowning, and every week a child sits out with a sore ear is a week of skill-building lost. Keeping ears healthy keeps kids in the water, learning to be safe.

✅ How do you dry a child’s ears after every swim?

The single most effective habit is drying the ear canal after every swim, because dry ears don't grow the bacteria that cause the infection. As soon as your child is out of the water, have them tilt their head to one side and then the other to let trapped water drain, tugging the earlobe gently to help it along. Dry the outer ear with a soft towel, and for kids prone to swimmer's ear, a hair dryer set to its lowest, coolest setting and held about a foot from the ear can evaporate the last of the moisture. Make it the same routine every time — pool day, lake day, or bath — so it becomes automatic.

One rule matters above all: never push cotton swabs, fingers, hairpins, or other objects into the ear canal. They scratch the delicate skin and strip away the earwax that naturally protects it, both of which make infection more likely, not less. Earwax is the ear's own defense; the goal is to dry the canal, not to clean inside it. If wax ever seems to be a problem, ask your pediatrician rather than digging it out at home.

🏊 What extra steps help a child who swims several times a week?

Children who swim several times a week or who keep getting swimmer's ear can add a physical barrier to keep water out. Well-fitting swim earplugs designed for the job, or a swim cap that covers the ears, both cut down how much water enters the canal in the first place. A pediatrician may also recommend an over-the-counter drying drop (often an alcohol-and-vinegar mix) to use after swimming — but only for children without ear tubes or a history of a perforated eardrum, so always check first. Pack the earplugs and a small towel in the swim bag so the whole routine travels with your swimmer.

Good pool habits help, too. Swimmer's ear is more likely after time in water that isn't well maintained, so choosing programs and pools that keep chlorine and pH in the right range lowers the risk — our guide to public pool hygiene covers what a clean, safe pool looks like. And if your child wears goggles, a snug fit means less rubbing and fussing that tempts kids to poke at their ears and eyes afterward.

🚨 When should a child stop swimming and see a doctor?

Catching swimmer's ear early keeps it minor, so know the signs. It often starts with itching inside the ear, mild redness, and discomfort that gets worse when you tug the earlobe or press near the ear. As it develops, a child may complain of stronger ear pain, a plugged or muffled-hearing feeling, or clear-to-pus-like drainage. If you see any of these — especially pain, drainage, or reduced hearing — keep your child out of the water and see a doctor. Swimmer's ear is treated with prescription ear drops and usually clears quickly once treatment starts.

Some symptoms mean call the doctor promptly: fever, severe pain, swelling or redness spreading around the ear, or pain moving toward the face or neck. This checklist is educational and doesn't replace medical care — when in doubt, a quick call to your pediatrician is always the right move. Preventing the infection in the first place, though, keeps most kids out of the doctor's office entirely and in the pool where they're building lifesaving skills.

Is swimmer's ear an ear infection, and how is it treated?

Yes: swimmer's ear (otitis externa) is a bacterial infection of the outer ear canal, and it is usually treated with prescription antibiotic ear drops. It is different from the middle-ear infection kids get with colds. Because the canal is swollen and painful, a doctor typically examines the ear, may gently clean it, and prescribes drops that your child uses for several days, often with an over-the-counter pain reliever recommended by the pediatrician. Keep the ear dry during treatment and ask your doctor when swimming can safely resume. Do not put homemade drops in the ear if your child has ear tubes or a possible hole in the eardrum — see our guides on swimming ear infections and swimming with ear tubes. This is general education, not medical advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is swimmer's ear and how is it different from a regular ear infection?

Swimmer's ear (otitis externa) is an infection of the outer ear canal, the tube between the eardrum and the outside of the head. It happens when water stays trapped in the canal, creating a moist environment where bacteria grow. This is different from a common middle-ear infection (otitis media), which sits behind the eardrum and usually follows a cold. Swimmer's ear typically hurts more when you tug the earlobe or push the little bump in front of the ear, and it is directly linked to water exposure rather than a head cold.

Is swimmer's ear an ear infection?

Yes. Swimmer's ear is an infection of the outer ear canal caused by bacteria growing in trapped water. It is different from a middle-ear infection. A pediatrician usually treats it with antibiotic ear drops, so call the doctor if your child has ear pain, drainage, or fever.

How can I prevent swimmer's ear in my child?

The core of prevention is keeping the ear canal dry. After every swim or bath, tilt your child's head to each side to let water drain, then gently dry the outer ear with a towel; a hair dryer on the lowest, coolest setting held a foot away can help evaporate trapped water. Never push cotton swabs, fingers, or other objects into the canal, since that scratches the skin and removes protective earwax. For children who swim often or are prone to swimmer's ear, well-fitting swim earplugs or a swim cap that covers the ears reduce how much water gets in, and a pediatrician may recommend over-the-counter drying drops for kids without ear tubes or a perforated eardrum.

Can my child still take swim lessons if they get swimmer's ear a lot?

Yes. Swimmer's ear is very preventable, and it is not a reason to stop lessons, which are one of the most important layers of drowning protection. Most children who are prone to it can keep swimming by drying their ears carefully after each session, wearing properly fitted earplugs or an ear-covering swim cap, and following any drying-drop routine their doctor suggests. If your child has ear tubes or a history of eardrum problems, ask your pediatrician which products are safe before using earplugs or drops. The goal is to protect the ears so your child can keep building the swim skills that keep them safe.

What are the warning signs of swimmer's ear?

Early signs include itching in the ear canal, mild redness, and discomfort that gets worse when you tug the earlobe or press the ear. As it progresses, children may have more intense ear pain, a feeling of fullness or muffled hearing, and clear or pus-like drainage from the ear. Fever, severe pain, swelling around the ear, or pain spreading to the face or neck are signs the infection may be serious. If you notice pain, drainage, or reduced hearing, stop swimming and see a doctor, because swimmer's ear is treated with prescription ear drops and clears quickly once treated.

Should we keep swimming during the school year to avoid summer ear problems?

Swimmer's ear is driven by trapped water and technique, not by the calendar, so year-round lessons do not increase the risk when ears are dried properly afterward. In fact, keeping lessons going through the cooler months in a warm indoor pool builds consistent skills and lets your child practice good ear-drying habits all year. The most important thing is a simple, repeatable after-swim routine: drain, dry, and protect the ears every single time, whether it is a July pool day or a January lesson.

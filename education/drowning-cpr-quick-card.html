Poolside CPR Quick Card for Drowning: The Rescue-Breaths-First Steps Every Parent Should Know

In a drowning, seconds decide everything — and drowning CPR is not the same as the hands-only kind you may have seen on TV. Because drowning starves the body of oxygen, breaths come first. Learn the current steps, then print the free poolside quick card for the pool bag, fridge, and boat.

🫁 Why Do Rescue Breaths Come Before Compressions in Drowning?

A drowning victim goes into cardiac arrest because their body ran out of oxygen, not because their heart suddenly failed — so getting air back into the lungs is the single most important thing you can do. That is the opposite priority from the "hands-only" CPR taught for a collapse on dry land, where the blood is still oxygen-rich and compressions alone can buy time.

The reason every second counts in a water emergency is clear once you look at the scale of the problem. Our drowning statistics dashboard breaks the U.S. numbers down by age group and setting.

In November 2024, the American Heart Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics issued a focused update making this explicit: anyone in cardiac arrest after drowning should receive CPR with rescue breaths, and trained rescuers should start ventilation as early as safely possible. For a struggling but breathing child, rescue breaths may even begin the moment they are reached. The takeaway for families is simple: in the water, air comes first.

This is why a drowning rescue does not end when the child reaches the edge. A submerged swimmer can look fine one minute and stop breathing the next. Knowing the steps ahead of time is part of the last layer in our five layers of drowning protection — emergency readiness.

🧭 In What Order Should You Check, Call, Breathe, and Pump?

Once the person is out of the water, the sequence is check, call, breathe, then pump — and keep going without long breaks. Run it top to bottom, fast, and let a 911 dispatcher coach you the whole time.

1. Check. Tap and shout — "Are you okay?" Look for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Gasping is not normal breathing.

2. Call. Point to someone specific: "You — call 911 and bring the AED." If you are alone with a child and no phone, give about 2 minutes of CPR first, then call. Put the phone on speaker so your hands stay free.

3. Breathe. Tilt the head back, lift the chin, and give 2 rescue breaths, each about one second, watching for the chest to rise. For an infant, cover the nose and mouth and give small puffs.

4. Pump. Push hard and fast in the center of the chest, 100–120 compressions per minute, letting the chest fully recoil each time. After every 30 compressions, give 2 breaths.

5. Continue. Keep 30:2 cycles going until the person breathes normally, an AED is ready to use, or trained responders take over. Follow the AED prompts as soon as one arrives, drying the chest first.

👶 How Deep Should Compressions Go for an Infant or Child?

The rhythm stays the same across ages — 30 compressions to 2 breaths for a lone rescuer — but the hands, depth, and breath size change with the size of the child. Getting these right matters, which is exactly why a hands-on class is irreplaceable.

Adult or older child: heel of two hands, center of chest, about 2 inches deep. Child (roughly 1 year to puberty): one or two hands, about 2 inches or one third the chest depth. Infant (under 1 year): two fingers or two thumbs, about 1.5 inches deep, with gentle puff-sized breaths. In all cases, push fast, let the chest come all the way back up, and minimize pauses.

A drowning victim often has water and vomit in the airway. Turn the head or roll the body to clear the mouth if needed, then continue. Do not waste time trying to "push water out" of the lungs — that delays the breaths and compressions that actually help.

🩺 Why Should a Rescued Child Be Examined Even If They Seem Fine?

Any child who was submerged, needed rescue breaths, or swallowed a lot of water should be evaluated by a medical professional, even if they recover and look normal. Rarely, breathing problems can appear hours after a water incident. The popular terms "dry drowning" and "secondary drowning" are not medical diagnoses, but delayed respiratory distress after submersion is real and worth watching for. For the hours afterwards, the symptom watch log after a water scare sets out which signs mean 911, which mean get seen today, and what to write down.

Watch for persistent coughing, fast or labored breathing, chest pain, extreme fatigue, or unusual irritability in the hours after a scare, and seek care right away if they appear. We cover exactly what to watch for in our guide to secondary and dry drowning.

🛡️ Which Layers Keep a Family From Ever Needing CPR?

Knowing CPR is essential, but it is the layer you hope never to use. The layers that keep the emergency from happening in the first place are barriers, supervision, life jackets, and swim skills. The simplest supervision tool is a designated Water Watcher, and the surest home barrier is a four-sided fence, covered in our family pool safety checklist.

The layer that travels with your child everywhere is water competency. According to research cited by the American Academy of Pediatrics, formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1–4. Survival skills like back-floating and rolling to breathe are what buy the seconds before anyone needs to start CPR at all. Frequently Asked Questions

Is CPR for drowning different from regular CPR?

Yes. Drowning cardiac arrest is caused by a lack of oxygen, not a heart problem, so breaths matter more than in a typical cardiac arrest. In 2024 the American Heart Association and American Academy of Pediatrics updated their guidance to recommend CPR with rescue breaths for anyone in cardiac arrest after drowning. If you are trained, give 2 rescue breaths first, then begin cycles of 30 chest compressions to 2 breaths. Hands-only CPR is still far better than doing nothing if you cannot or will not give breaths.

What are the steps of CPR after someone is pulled from the water?

Check for response and normal breathing. If there is none, shout for help and have someone call 911 and get an AED. Give 2 rescue breaths, watching for the chest to rise. Then start CPR: push hard and fast in the center of the chest about 100 to 120 compressions per minute, then give 2 breaths after every 30 compressions. Keep going without long pauses until the person breathes normally, an AED arrives, or trained help takes over. These are quick-reference reminders and do not replace a hands-on CPR class.

How is CPR different for an infant or small child?

For an infant under 1 year, use 2 fingers or two thumbs in the center of the chest and compress about 1.5 inches deep. For a child, use one or two hands and compress about 2 inches, roughly one third the depth of the chest. Give gentle puffs for breaths, just enough to make the chest rise, because a child's lungs are small. The ratio stays 30 compressions to 2 breaths for a single rescuer. Always take an infant and child CPR class so these steps are automatic in an emergency.

Should I call 911 before or after starting CPR on a drowning victim?

If you are alone with an unresponsive drowning victim, give about 2 minutes of CPR first, then call 911 if no one else can, because oxygen is the priority in drowning. If another person is present, have them call 911 and find an AED immediately while you start rescue breaths and compressions. Put the phone on speaker so the dispatcher can coach you while your hands keep working.

Does knowing CPR replace swim lessons and supervision?

No. CPR is the last layer of protection, used only after prevention has failed. The layers that stop a drowning from happening at all are barriers, active supervision, life jackets, and water competency from formal swim lessons. According to research cited by the American Academy of Pediatrics, formal swim lessons are associated with a reduction in drowning risk of up to 88% for children ages 1 to 4. Learn CPR and enroll in swim lessons; the two protect your child at opposite ends of the same emergency.

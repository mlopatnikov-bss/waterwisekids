Poolside CPR Quick Card for Drowning: Breaths, Compressions and AED

Emergency: Call 911. Start CPR if unresponsive and not breathing normally. Give breaths if able; if unable or unwilling, give chest compressions and follow the dispatcher. Begin CPR before applying an AED after drowning; use the AED as soon as available without delaying CPR.

After drowning, CPR should include rescue breaths if able. Review recognition, calling 911, age-appropriate compressions and AED use; then print the memory aid.

Why breaths matter after drowning

Breaths are especially important after drowning. The Red Cross drowning sequence starts with 2 rescue breaths, then cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths for a single rescuer. AHA guidance allows trained rescuers to begin with breaths or compressions; do not delay CPR to debate the sequence. If unable or unwilling to give breaths, start chest compressions and follow the dispatcher.

Drowning CPR: check, call and start care

Make the rescue safe

Call for a lifeguard. Reach or throw a flotation aid from a safe position; do not enter dangerous water or attempt an in-water rescue beyond your training. Once safely removed, place the person on a firm, flat surface.

Check responsiveness and breathing

Tap and shout; for an infant, tap the bottom of the foot. Check for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, including only gasping, begin CPR. Lay rescuers should not delay for a pulse check.

Activate emergency help

Send someone to call 911 and get an AED. If alone with a mobile phone, call on speaker and start care while following the dispatcher. If alone with a child or infant and you must leave to reach a phone, give about 2 minutes of CPR before leaving to call.

Include rescue breaths after drowning

Breaths are especially important after drowning. The Red Cross drowning sequence starts with 2 rescue breaths, then cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths for a single rescuer. AHA guidance allows trained rescuers to begin with breaths or compressions; do not delay CPR to debate the sequence. If unable or unwilling to give breaths, start chest compressions and follow the dispatcher.

Give age-appropriate compressions

Compress at 100–120 per minute on the lower half of the breastbone, allowing full recoil and minimizing pauses. For a child from age 1 until puberty, use one or two hands; press at least one third of the chest depth, about 2 inches (5 cm). For an infant under 1 year, use the heel of one hand or two thumbs with hands encircling the chest; press at least one third of its depth, about 1.5 inches (4 cm). The 2025 AHA guidance no longer recommends two-finger infant compressions.

Give breaths and use an AED

For each breath, open the airway with a head tilt and chin lift. Seal over a child’s mouth while pinching the nose; cover an infant’s mouth and nose. Give each breath over about 1 second, only enough for visible chest rise. Continue 30 compressions and 2 breaths. Begin CPR with breaths before applying an AED after drowning. As soon as available, expose and dry the chest, attach pads and follow its prompts; use pediatric pads/attenuation if available. Do not delay CPR to find an AED. Nobody should touch the person during analysis or shock.

Continue and reassess

Continue until normal breathing or other clear signs of life return, trained help takes over, you are too exhausted to continue, or the scene becomes unsafe. Pause when the AED instructs, then promptly resume. If breathing normally but still unresponsive, maintain an open airway and follow the dispatcher; use a side-lying recovery position if appropriate and no suspected spine injury prevents it. Watch breathing continuously and restart CPR if normal breathing stops.

Hand placement and depth by age

Compress at 100–120 per minute on the lower half of the breastbone, allowing full recoil and minimizing pauses. For a child from age 1 until puberty, use one or two hands; press at least one third of the chest depth, about 2 inches (5 cm). For an infant under 1 year, use the heel of one hand or two thumbs with hands encircling the chest; press at least one third of its depth, about 1.5 inches (4 cm). The 2025 AHA guidance no longer recommends two-finger infant compressions.

For adults and people with signs of puberty, use two hands and compress at least 2 inches (5 cm), avoiding more than 2.4 inches (6 cm).

When medical care is needed

Call 911 for severe or labored breathing, blue or gray color, unresponsiveness, confusion with breathing distress, or a child who is difficult to wake. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the dispatcher. Anyone who needed CPR or rescue breaths after drowning needs emergency medical care.

A brief cough or sputter that resolves promptly, followed by completely normal breathing and behavior, can be observed by an attentive caregiver. Persistent or worsening cough, rapid or difficult breathing, unusual sleepiness or confusion require prompt medical care. If unsure whether your child is back to normal, contact a medical professional.

Prepare before an emergency

Take hands-on CPR/AED training. Use attentive supervision, barriers, life jackets and swimming instruction together to reduce drowning risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start CPR after a water rescue?

Tap and shout; for an infant, tap the bottom of the foot. Check for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, including only gasping, begin CPR. Lay rescuers should not delay for a pulse check.

What compression rate and depth should I use for a child?

For a child age 1 until puberty, use one or two hands on the lower half of the breastbone at 100–120 compressions per minute. Compress at least one third of the chest depth, about 2 inches (5 cm), and allow full recoil.

How is infant CPR different?

For an infant under 1 year, use the heel of one hand or two thumbs with hands encircling the chest. Compress at least one third of the chest depth, about 1.5 inches (4 cm), at 100–120 per minute. Cover the mouth and nose for breaths, using only enough air to make the chest rise. This is not newborn-at-birth resuscitation guidance.

Are rescue breaths needed after drowning?

Breaths are especially important after drowning. The Red Cross drowning sequence starts with 2 rescue breaths, then cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths for a single rescuer. AHA guidance allows trained rescuers to begin with breaths or compressions; do not delay CPR to debate the sequence. If unable or unwilling to give breaths, start chest compressions and follow the dispatcher.

Does reading this guide replace CPR training?

No. Take hands-on infant, child and adult CPR/AED training through the American Red Cross, American Heart Association, or another qualified provider. In an emergency, call 911 and follow the dispatcher; do not wait for certification to help.

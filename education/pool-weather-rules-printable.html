Pool Weather Go/No-Go Card

Two kinds of weather end a swim day, and both of them are usually argued about while the children are still in the water. A storm is close enough to be dangerous long before it is overhead, and a hot afternoon turns into a medical problem faster in a child than in an adult. This card holds the decision for both — when to clear the water, where to go, when it is genuinely safe to return, and the point at which overheating stops being a rest-and-drink problem and becomes a 911 call. Decide it once, here, so nobody has to decide it under pressure.

📝 Start Here — How to Use This Card

  • Fill it in before the season, not during a storm. The National Weather Service asks organised programmes to write the plan down and then follow it without exception, precisely so nobody is making a judgement call with thunder overhead. The same logic works for one family and one backyard pool.
  • Name one person as the weather watcher for the day. Not whoever notices first. One named adult, who is not the person teaching, refereeing or supervising the swimming.
  • Agree the rule out loud with the children before they get in. “When I call it, everyone is out — no finishing the game.” A rule announced in advance is obeyed in about a tenth of the time of one announced in a panic.
  • Two hazards, two different triggers. Storms are a hard stop with a fixed clock. Heat is a gradual thing you manage with shade, water and breaks — until it isn’t, and then it is an emergency. Keep the two sections separate in your head.
  • Check the forecast before you pack the car. The cheapest weather decision is the one made in the kitchen, when cancelling costs nothing but disappointment.
Why a card rather than a judgement call. The National Weather Service is blunt about the failure mode: officials who have no written plan end up deciding from personal experience or from a desire to finish the activity, and both put people at risk. Nothing about that is unique to a swim meet. The parent who has already decided is the parent who calls it early.

Time the walk to the shelter before you need it. The stop rule has to fire early enough that everybody is inside before the storm arrives — so a shelter four minutes away changes when you call it, not just where you go.

⛈️ 2. Storms — The Stop Rule

  • You hear thunder. Clear the water. Thunder normally carries about ten miles, and traffic, wind and rain shorten that — so if you can hear it at all, the storm is close enough.
  • You see lightning. Clear the water, even with blue sky overhead. In clear air, and especially at night, lightning is visible from storms more than ten miles away.
  • The sky simply looks threatening. That is a stop criterion in its own right. Storms can build directly overhead, and some produce their first lightning as they move in.
  • Everyone out, not just the small children. Wading, sitting on the steps and standing in the shallow end are all in the water.
  • Go straight there. Do not stop to fold towels, coil hoses or pull the cover across. Those are twenty seconds you have not budgeted for.
The number behind the rule. A significant lightning threat reaches roughly six to ten miles out from the base of a thunderstorm cloud, which is why the water is cleared on the first thunder rather than on the first raindrop. The familiar “30/30” shorthand — count the seconds between the flash and the bang, and act if it is under thirty — describes the same distance in a way that is easy to remember. You do not have to count, though. Hearing thunder is already the answer.
Water is not what makes lightning dangerous — being outside is. There is no safe place outdoors during a thunderstorm, which is why getting out of the pool is only the first half of the instruction. Standing on the deck, sheltering under a tree, or waiting it out beside the fence is still outdoors.

🏠 3. Where to Go — and Where Not To

SafeNot safe, despite appearances
A substantial building with wiring and plumbing — a house, a school, the facility’s own indoor spaceA dugout, a rain shelter, a picnic pavilion, a shed or a lifeguard hut
A hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows closedA convertible, a golf cart, an open-sided cabana
Indoors, away from windows and doorsThe pool deck, the porch, under a tree, beside a chain-link fence
  • Once inside, stay away from anything that conducts. Corded phones, wiring, plumbing and anything plugged into them. That includes the indoor shower.
  • Point at the shelter when you announce the rule. Children who know where they are going move faster than children who are being herded.
  • If you are at a lake, river or beach, the swim ends earlier. Shelter is often far away and swimmers are often far from shore, so the walk you have to budget for is much longer.
Indoor pools are not automatically exempt. Being under a roof solves the outdoor problem, but the plumbing does not stop at the door. Ask the facility what its own storm protocol is rather than assuming the building answers the question — a well-run indoor programme will have one written down.

⏱️ 4. The Restart Clock

  • Wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder or the last flash. Not thirty minutes after the rain stops, and not thirty minutes after the storm looks like it has moved on.
  • The clock restarts every single time. One distant rumble at minute twenty-eight buys a fresh thirty minutes. This is why a “half-hour delay” routinely becomes two hours, and why meets get moved rather than paused.
  • Blue sky is not the signal. Charge lingers in clouds after a storm appears to have passed, which is the whole reason the wait is measured from the last thunder rather than from the weather.
  • Write down the time of the last thunder. On this card, on a phone, on the back of a hand. Memory under pressure rounds downwards, always in the wrong direction.
  • Decide in advance when the day is simply over. Agreeing a cut-off before you start — “if we’re still waiting at four, we go home” — removes the argument at the moment you least want to have it.
Time of last thunder / flash: _______
Earliest safe return (add 30 min): ___
Reset again at: ______________
Today’s give-up time: ___________

🌡️ 5. Heat — Read the Index, Not the Thermometer

Heat indexNational Weather Service classificationWhat it means for a swim day
80–90°FCautionFatigue possible with prolonged exposure or activity. Normal precautions: shade, drinks, breaks.
90–103°FExtreme CautionHeat cramps, heat exhaustion and heat stroke all become possible. Shorten sessions, force the breaks.
103–124°FDangerCramps or heat exhaustion are likely and heat stroke is possible. Move to early morning, indoors, or another day.
125°F or higherExtreme DangerHeat stroke highly likely. Not a swim-lesson afternoon.
  • Add up to 15°F if you are standing in direct sunlight. The published heat index values are calculated for shade. A pool deck at noon is not shade, and this correction alone can move an afternoon from Caution into Danger.
  • Look up the heat index, not the temperature. Humidity is half of it: at 100°F the index reads 96°F when the air is dry and 124°F when it is humid. The thermometer alone cannot tell you which day you are having.
  • Being in the water hides the warning signs. A swimming child is wet, so you cannot see whether they are sweating, and they rarely feel hot enough to complain until they are already in trouble.
  • Water every 20 to 30 minutes, on a schedule. Not when they ask — thirst already means mild dehydration has started.
  • Watch the child, not the group. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that children overheat faster than adults, and within a group the youngest, the newest and the one trying hardest to keep up are all carrying more risk than the average.
The newer NWS tool, and how to treat it. The National Weather Service also publishes HeatRisk, a five-level scale from 0 (little to no risk) to 4 (extreme), developed with the CDC and colour-coded green through magenta. It is experimental and explicitly supplementary to official heat watches, warnings and advisories — useful as a second opinion on how punishing a given day will be, not as a replacement for the heat index bands above or for your local forecast office’s alerts.

🚑 6. Heat Exhaustion or Heatstroke

 Heat exhaustionHeatstroke — call 911
How they seemTired, dizzy, headachy, nauseous, irritableConfused, agitated, slurring, not making sense
Their skinCool and clammy, heavy sweatingHot to the touch — may be dry, may still be sweaty
TemperatureRaised but below 104°FAround 104°F (40°C) or higher
Also watch forMuscle cramps, faintness, cold handsVomiting, seizures, loss of consciousness
What you doCool place, remove extra clothing, sips of cool water if alert, cool wet clothsCall 911 first, then cool aggressively while you wait
What you expectImprovement within about 30 minutesDo not wait to see. This is time-critical.
  • Confusion is the line. A hot, miserable, complaining child is usually heat exhaustion. A hot child who is confused, agitated or not making sense is a 911 call, whatever the thermometer says.
  • If heat exhaustion does not improve in about 30 minutes, get medical help. Same if symptoms worsen or they cannot keep fluids down.
  • Cool water, not ice-cold. Cool wet cloths to the skin, or sitting in cool water. Do not give fluids to a child who is vomiting or not fully alert.
  • Heat exhaustion looks like drowning behaviour. Clumsy, confused and panicky in the water reads the same either way — which is a reason to get them out and assess on the deck rather than to watch and wonder.
If someone is struck by lightning, they are safe to touch. A lightning victim carries no electrical charge. Call 911, start CPR if they are not breathing, use an AED if one is available, and keep going until help arrives. Most people struck by lightning survive, and the ones who do are usually the ones somebody started on immediately.

Start the day hydrated. A proper drink with breakfast does more than a sip at the pool, and a scheduled break that nobody asked for is the whole point — by the time a child says they are thirsty, you are already behind.

❓ 8. Five Questions for a Swim School, Camp or Pool

  • When do you stop activities? You want a criterion, not a philosophy. “First thunder or first flash” is an answer; “when it looks bad” is not.
  • Where do people go? A named building. If the answer is the pavilion or the equipment shed, that is the wrong answer and it is worth saying so.
  • When do you resume? Thirty minutes after the last thunder, with the clock restarting. If they quote you fifteen minutes, they have invented their own rule.
  • Who monitors the weather, and is it their only job? The National Weather Service is specific about this: the monitor should not be the coach, umpire or referee, because those people are busy and cannot watch conditions properly. In a swim programme, that means not the instructor in the water.
  • What happens if somebody is struck? Who calls 911, who starts CPR, where the AED is. A programme that has thought about the first four questions has usually thought about this one.
These are the National Weather Service’s own five questions. They are what the NWS says a written lightning safety plan has to answer for any organised outdoor activity. Asking them is not being difficult — it is reading back the standard, and a well-run programme will answer all five without hesitating. Ask what the heat policy is in the same breath: the temperature or index at which sessions shorten, move indoors or cancel.

🚩 9. Weather Answers That Should Worry You

  • “We wait until it’s directly overhead.” The threat reaches six to ten miles out. Overhead is far too late.
  • “We get everyone under the pavilion.” Small open structures are specifically listed as unsafe. This is the single most common wrong answer.
  • “Fifteen minutes and we’re back in.” The standard is thirty, and it resets. Half of it is not most of it.
  • “The lifeguard keeps an eye on the sky.” The lifeguard is watching the water, which is the job you want them doing. The weather needs its own person.
  • “They’re in the water, so they can’t overheat.” They can. Being wet hides the sweating you would otherwise notice.
  • “We go by the temperature.” The temperature without the humidity, and without the correction for standing in the sun, understates the day by a wide margin.
One wrong answer is a conversation, not a verdict. Plenty of good programmes have simply never been asked and will fix the shelter question the week you raise it. What you are listening for is whether there is a plan at all, or whether the decision gets made from scratch each time by whoever is nearest.

⭐ 10. Five Rules to Remember

  • Thunder means out. No counting, no negotiating, no finishing the lap.
  • Out of the water is only half of it. There is no safe place outdoors. Get inside a real building or a hard-topped car.
  • Thirty minutes from the last thunder, and it resets. Write the time down.
  • Confusion in a hot child is a 911 call. Everything else about heat is shade, water and breaks. That one is not.
  • Weather never removes the need for supervision. A cancelled lesson is a day nobody is watching the water at the same intensity — and no lesson, and no weather rule, makes a child drown-proof. Swim lessons are one layer alongside four-sided pool fencing, constant undistracted supervision within arm’s reach of a young or non-swimming child, properly fitted life jackets in open water, and an adult trained in CPR.
Why the boring version wins. The National Weather Service documents about 30 lightning deaths a year in the United States, with hundreds more injured, and roughly two thirds of those deaths are connected to outdoor recreation. Almost none of them involve anyone deciding to take a risk. They involve people finishing something — one more game, one more lap, one more ten minutes — which is exactly what a rule written down in advance is for.

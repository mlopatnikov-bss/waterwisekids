Public Pool Hygiene: How to Swim Safely in Community Pools

Public pools are one of the best places for children to learn, play, and build water confidence — but they are shared spaces that require shared responsibility. Understanding pool hygiene protects your family and every other swimmer in the water.

Quick Answer: The most important public pool hygiene rules: shower before entering (60 seconds removes most contaminants), never swim when sick with diarrhea, take bathroom breaks every 60 minutes, don't swallow pool water, and wear swim diapers for children not fully toilet trained. A strong "chlorine smell" is often a warning sign, not reassurance.

Most outbreaks are preventable through basic hygiene practices.

What keeps public pool water safe?

Safe pool water depends on chlorine (or bromine) held within the right pH range (7.2–7.8) and concentration, plus temperature control — and a strong "chlorine smell" actually signals contamination, not good sanitation. A public pool's water safety depends on the careful balance of several chemical and physical factors. Chlorine (or alternative sanitizers like bromine) kills bacteria and viruses, but it has limits. Chlorine works most effectively within a specific pH range (7.2–7.8) and at adequate concentration (typically 1–3 parts per million in pools). When either pH or chlorine levels drift outside this range, the pool's ability to sanitize degrades rapidly. If you dose your own pool rather than swim in someone else’s, how to store and handle pool chemicals safely covers the part that happens on the deck.

Temperature matters too. Warmer water accelerates bacterial growth, which is why spas and splash pads require more frequent chemical management than standard pools. And here's a fact that surprises many parents: a very strong "chlorine smell" at a pool is not a sign of good sanitation. It's a sign of high chloramine levels — compounds formed when chlorine reacts with sweat, urine, sunscreen, and organic matter from swimmers' bodies. A well-maintained pool should have a very faint, barely perceptible chemical smell.

Responsible facilities maintain daily records of chemical levels and make this information accessible. Don't hesitate to ask pool staff when the water was last tested — it's a legitimate question that well-run facilities are happy to answer.

Why should you shower before a public pool?

A 60-second pre-swim shower with soap removes most of the sweat, sunscreen, bacteria, and fecal matter that would otherwise consume chlorine before it can kill pathogens — making it the single most impactful pool hygiene habit. The mandatory pre-swim shower at most public pools feels like a formality to many families. It isn't. According to the CDC's Healthy Swimming guidelines, the average swimmer introduces about 0.14 grams of fecal matter into the pool in the first 15 minutes of swimming — despite feeling clean. That number increases with young children who are not fully toilet trained.

A thorough pre-swim shower — not a quick rinse, but 60 seconds with soap, paying attention to the groin, underarms, and feet — removes the vast majority of sweat, bacteria, sunscreen, cosmetics, and fecal material that would otherwise immediately react with pool chlorine. When everyone showers before entering, chlorine is available to do its job of sanitizing the water. When they don't, chlorine is consumed rapidly by body contaminants before it can kill actual pathogens.

Make the pre-swim shower a non-negotiable household rule, not a suggestion. Explain to children why it matters — "we shower before the pool so the water stays clean for everyone" is a lesson that sticks and that children will carry into adult pool use.

What are recreational water illnesses and how do they spread?

Recreational water illnesses (RWIs) are caused by pathogens in contaminated water and spread mainly by swallowing it — the most common in pools are Cryptosporidium ("crypto"), bacterial infections like E. coli, swimmer's ear, and eye irritation. Recreational Water Illnesses (RWIs) are illnesses caused by pathogens present in contaminated pool, spa, lake, or ocean water. In pools, the most common RWIs are:

Cryptosporidiosis ("Crypto"). Caused by the parasite Cryptosporidium parvum, crypto is the leading cause of pool-associated diarrheal outbreaks in the United States. It is extraordinarily chlorine-resistant — it can survive for days in properly chlorinated pool water. The only reliable protection is preventing fecal contamination in the first place: don't swim when you have diarrhea, take regular bathroom breaks, and use swim diapers for children who aren't toilet trained.

E. coli and other bacterial infections. While chlorine kills most bacteria within minutes, a pool with high contamination load or improper chemical balance can harbor E. coli long enough to cause illness. Symptoms include nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, and diarrhea.

Swimmer's ear (Otitis externa). Water trapped in the ear canal creates a warm, moist environment where bacteria thrive. Swimmer's ear is extremely common in children who swim frequently and is easily prevented with earplugs, thorough ear drying after swimming, and preventive ear drops. Our full guide on preventing swimmer's ear covers all the effective strategies.

Eye irritation and infection. Chloramines and pathogens in pool water can irritate or infect eyes. Well-fitting goggles prevent eye contact with pool water entirely — the most effective prevention for swimming-related eye issues.

What pool hygiene rules protect every swimmer?

Never swim with diarrhea, take bathroom breaks every 45–60 minutes, use swim diapers correctly, don't swallow pool water, and shower and wash hands after swimming. Public pool hygiene isn't just about protecting your own family — it's about being a responsible member of a shared aquatic space. These behaviors protect every child and adult in the water:

Don't swim when sick with diarrhea. This is the single most important rule. A person with active diarrhea — from any cause — can introduce enough Cryptosporidium or E. coli into a pool to cause an outbreak affecting hundreds of swimmers. The rule applies to everyone: children, parents, and adults. Most facilities require that children with diarrhea not swim for two weeks after symptoms resolve.

Take bathroom breaks. Adults should take young children to the restroom every 45–60 minutes. Build this into your pool routine regardless of whether the child says they need to go. Young children often aren't aware of the urge until it's urgent.

Use swim diapers — correctly. For infants and toddlers who aren't toilet trained, properly fitted swim diapers (disposable or reusable) dramatically reduce fecal contamination. However, swim diapers do not eliminate it — they contain solid waste but allow water through. A swim diaper is not a substitute for a toilet break. Check the swim diaper every 30–60 minutes and change it away from the pool deck.

Don't swallow pool water. Teach children from the beginning that pool water is not drinking water. While this can't be perfectly controlled (especially with young children), reducing intentional swallowing matters. Games that involve drinking pool water should be strongly discouraged.

Shower and wash hands after swimming. The post-swim shower removes residual chlorine, chloramines, and any pathogens that may have adhered to skin. Washing hands before eating poolside snacks prevents hand-to-mouth contamination. Federal safety resources such as the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's Pools & Spas guidance reinforce that proper pool operation and swimmer hygiene work together to keep shared water safe.

How do you evaluate a public pool's safety?

Before your child swims, check that the water is clear enough to see the main drain, that there is only a faint chemical smell, that inspection records are posted, and that staff can describe their chemical-testing schedule. Not all public pools maintain the same standards. Before your child swims at an unfamiliar pool, a few quick checks can tell you a great deal about the facility's management quality:

Look at the water clarity. You should be able to clearly see the main drain at the bottom of the deep end. Hazy or murky water indicates chemical imbalance or high contamination. Walk away from any pool where the bottom isn't clearly visible.

Smell the air. A barely perceptible chemical smell is normal and acceptable. An overwhelming "pool smell" (really a chloramine smell) indicates high contamination — often from inadequate pre-swim showering by many swimmers combined with insufficient chemical management.

Check for inspection records. Most public pools are required to post their most recent health inspection results. Look for the certificate near the entrance, or ask staff. A facility that doesn't know where to find their inspection certificate is a red flag.

Observe the deck and changing areas. Well-maintained facilities take pride in the whole environment, not just the water. Dirty changing rooms, broken fixtures, and neglected pool deck maintenance often correlate with less rigorous water treatment practices.

Ask about chemical testing frequency. Responsible facilities test water chemistry at least twice daily (more often in heavy use periods). If staff can't answer this question, consider it a warning sign.

What hygiene matters for swim lessons?

Swim-lesson pools are smaller and host concentrated groups of young, often not-yet-toilet-trained children, so ask programs about pre-swim shower rules, swim-diaper policies, and their water-testing schedule. Swim lesson pools have unique hygiene dynamics. Typically smaller than public recreational pools, they host concentrated groups of young children — often including infants and toddlers who are not yet fully toilet trained. This elevates the contamination load and makes hygiene practices by families especially important.

When evaluating a swim school or lesson program, ask about their pool hygiene policies. Good programs will have: mandatory pre-swim shower or rinse requirements, swim diaper policies for non-toilet-trained children, and transparency about their water testing and chemical management schedule.

If you arrive at a lesson program and notice the water has an unusual color, cloudiness, or an overwhelming chemical smell, it's appropriate to ask staff about the current water chemistry results before putting your child in the water. A well-run program welcomes this question.

What should you do after swimming?

Rinse or shower right away, wash hair, dry the ears thoroughly, wash hands before snacks, and change out of the wet swimsuit promptly to prevent irritation and infection. The post-swim routine closes the hygiene loop. After swimming, children should rinse or shower immediately to remove chlorine and chloramines that cause skin and eye irritation with prolonged contact. Wash hair with a gentle, chlorine-removing shampoo. Dry ears thoroughly — tilt the head to each side and use a clean towel to gently remove water from the outer ear canal.

Wash hands before eating any post-swim snacks, and change out of the wet swimsuit promptly. Prolonged wear of a damp swimsuit creates conditions favorable for bacterial skin overgrowth, particularly in skin folds.

If your child develops any rash, eye irritation, ear pain, or gastrointestinal symptoms in the 24–72 hours after pool swimming, consult your pediatrician and mention recent pool exposure. Most RWIs are mild and self-limiting, but some (particularly Crypto in immunocompromised children) require medical management. For a comprehensive guide to swimming and ear infections, and more on pool chemical safety, explore those dedicated guides on our site. Healthy-swimming resources from the American Red Cross reinforce that good hygiene before, during, and after swimming protects every family at a shared pool.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are public swimming pools safe for children?

Yes, properly maintained public pools are safe for children. The CDC reports that well-managed pools with correct chlorine and pH levels effectively eliminate most bacterial threats. The key is choosing facilities with transparent maintenance records, showering before entering, and following hygiene guidelines during your visit.

Does chlorine kill all germs in a pool?

Chlorine kills most bacteria within minutes, but some chlorine-resistant parasites like Cryptosporidium can survive for days even in properly chlorinated pools. This is why the CDC emphasizes that swimmers should shower before entering, avoid swallowing pool water, and never swim when ill.

What is a Recreational Water Illness (RWI) and how do I prevent it?

Recreational Water Illnesses (RWIs) are illnesses spread by swallowing, breathing, or having contact with contaminated pool water. Common RWIs include diarrheal illness (Cryptosporidium), skin infections, and eye irritation. Prevention includes showering before swimming, not swallowing pool water, not swimming when sick, and taking bathroom breaks every 60 minutes.

What does a 'chlorine smell' actually mean about a pool?

Surprisingly, a strong 'chlorine smell' often indicates a pool that needs MORE chlorine, not less. The smell comes from chloramines — compounds formed when chlorine reacts with sweat, urine, and other contaminants. A well-maintained pool should have very little odor. An overwhelming chemical smell signals high contamination load.

Should my child shower before going in a public pool?

Yes — this is one of the most important pool hygiene behaviors. A 60-second shower before entering removes sweat, sunscreen, lotion, fecal matter, and other contaminants that would otherwise react with pool chlorine, forming chloramines and overwhelming the pool's sanitizing capacity.

Winter Swim Lesson Routine Card

The lesson itself happens in warm water. The hard part of a winter swim lesson is the fifteen minutes after it — wet hair, a cold changing room, a car seat and a parking lot. Get those fifteen minutes right and your child keeps wanting to go; get them wrong a few weeks running and January is when the lessons stop. One routine, one car seat rule, one myth to drop, and an eight-week log to see it working.

🏠 Before You Leave Home

  • Dress for the exit, not the entrance. Swimsuit on under loose, easy clothes, and pack the outfit your child will put on wet: elastic waists, a zip-up top rather than a pull-over, socks that are not tight, slip-on shoes. Every button and lace you remove is a minute less standing damp in a changing room.
  • Pack the warm layer on top of the bag. A hooded towel or towel poncho, a hat, and a dry fleece go in last so they come out first. Digging past goggles and shampoo for the hat is how the shivering starts.
  • A small snack before, a warm drink after. Swimming burns more energy than it looks like, and a hungry child gets cold faster and melts down sooner. Keep it light before the lesson; save the cup of something warm for the car.
  • Bathroom before getting changed. The lesson that stops halfway for a trip across a cold deck is the lesson that ends in tears.
  • Arrive five to ten minutes early, not thirty. Enough time to settle without rushing, not so long that a child sits on a cool deck in a swimsuit. Wait in warm clothes and strip down at the last minute.

🧺 The Poolside Exit — The First Two Minutes

  • Towel waiting at the edge, open and ready. The biggest heat loss of the whole routine is the walk from the water to the changing room. A child who is wrapped the second they climb out has already won most of the battle.
  • Hood up, then walk. Never run. Hood on a wet head straight away. Decks are slippery, and a hurried child in a long towel is a fall waiting to happen.
  • Hear the instructor out. The minute of feedback at the end is worth taking — wrapped up. Ask what to practise at home this week and write it on the log below.
  • Shoes or sandals on for the walk. Warm feet make a surprising difference to how cold a child feels, and they keep feet off wet floors.

⏱️ The Ten-Minute Changing Room Sequence

  • 0–2 min: quick warm rinse. A short rinse under a warm shower takes the pool water off skin and hair and warms a chilled child up. Warm, not hot, and short — long hot showers dry skin out.
  • 2–4 min: hair and ears first. Squeeze and blot hair with the towel rather than rubbing. Tilt the head to each side to let water drain from the ears, then dry the outer ear gently with a towel corner. Nothing goes inside the ear — no cotton swabs.
  • 4–6 min: dry the body fully, feet last. Damp skin under dry clothes still feels cold. Between the toes counts. Moisturiser now if your child’s skin is dry or prone to eczema.
  • 6–8 min: dress core first. Vest or T-shirt and a warm top before pants and socks. A warm chest takes the edge off everything else.
  • 8–10 min: hair and hat. If a hair dryer is available, a few minutes on warm (not hot), held well away from the scalp, takes long hair from dripping to damp. Then the hat goes on and stays on until you are home.
The target: water to car in about fifteen minutes. Write down how long it actually takes on the log below — most families find the first week takes twice as long and the third week takes half.

🚗 The Car — The Winter Coat Rule

  • No puffy coat under the car seat harness. The American Academy of Pediatrics warns that bulky coats and snowsuits compress in a crash and leave the harness too loose. Thin fleece layers under the straps; the big coat goes on after.
  • Coat on backwards, or a blanket over the straps. Buckle and tighten first, then put the coat on backwards over the harness or tuck a blanket over the top. Your child stays warm and the harness stays snug.
  • Do the pinch test once. Buckle your child in wearing the coat and tighten. Take them out, remove the coat, and buckle back in without loosening. If you can now pinch slack in the strap at the shoulder, that coat is too bulky to wear under the harness.
  • Warm the car before you go in. If you can safely start the heater a few minutes early, do. A cold car seat against a damp back undoes a good changing room.

🧠 The Myth — “Wet Hair Will Give Them a Cold”

  • Colds come from viruses, not from cold air. The CDC is clear that colds are caused by viruses, and children catch them all year round. Wet hair in winter air makes a child uncomfortable; it does not, by itself, give them a cold.
  • So why bother with the routine? Because a child who is miserable after every lesson starts dreading the next one, and dread is what makes families quit in January. The routine is about keeping lessons something your child wants to come back to.
  • Wash hands, and keep sick kids home. The germs that actually spread colds live on hands and shared surfaces. Hand-washing after the changing room does more for winter illness than any hat.
When to skip a lesson. Fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, an ear that hurts, an open wound or a rash that might be contagious — keep them home, and follow your pool’s return rules. A runny nose with a happy, energetic child is a judgement call; our sick-day decision guide walks through it. Ear pain that lasts, or pain when the outer ear is touched, is a reason to call your pediatrician.

Winter Is When Swimmers Are Made

Skills fade fastest in the months nobody swims. A child who keeps a weekly lesson going through winter starts next summer where they finished this one — floating, breathing and getting to an edge on their own — instead of spending June relearning what they knew in August. Find a program with a warm indoor pool and a changing room you can live with, and this card does the rest.

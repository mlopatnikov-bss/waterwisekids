Kids’ Swim Gear Fit Check

A packing list tells you the goggles are in the bag. It does not tell you whether they still seal, whether the cap is a size too small, or whether the warmth suit your baby wore in spring still reaches the wrists. This sheet is the other half: one test per item, what a pass looks like, and the signal that says replace it. Run it on the kitchen table before lessons start.

📝 Start Here — How to Use This Fit Check

  • Test dry, at home, before lesson day. Every check on this sheet works on a kitchen table. Finding out at the pool that the goggles leak costs you half a lesson.
  • Fit beats brand, price and character print. A poorly fitting pair with a favourite character on it gets abandoned after one swim. Let your child choose from the pairs that already passed the fit test.
  • Re-run it every season and every growth spurt. Silicone hardens, heads get bigger, and a suit sized by weight goes out of range faster than one sized by age.
  • None of this gear is safety equipment. Goggles, caps, clips and warmth suits make swimming more comfortable. Supervision, a life jacket where one is needed, and the skills underneath are what keep your child safe.

🥅 1. Goggles — The Suction Test

Four checks, in order. Stop at the first failure and try a different pair — a strap cannot fix a gasket that does not match the face

The checkWhat a pass looks likeWhat a fail tells youPair APair B
The suction testPress the goggles gently against the eyes with the strap off. They hold with a light vacuum seal for a few secondsIf they drop straight off, the gasket does not match your child’s eye socket shape. No strap tension will make that pair seal in the water____________
The nose bridgeThe bridge holds both eyecups flat against the face. Many kids’ goggles ship with interchangeable bridges — try the other sizesToo narrow pulls the cups outward at the sides; too wide breaks the seal in the center. More goggle failures are bridge mismatches than size mismatches____________
The strapSnug, sitting across the back of the head at roughly ear height. A split strap holds better for diving and underwater swimmingIf it only seals when cranked tight, the pair is wrong. Over-tightening distorts the gasket and actually breaks the seal____________
The 20-minute markNo deep red rings after 20–30 minutes of wear, and no immediate fogging on entering the waterDeep marks or headaches mean too small or too tight. Persistent fog from the first minute usually means the anti-fog coating is spent____________
Never size up to adult goggles. Adult goggles on a child’s face almost never seal — the bridge is too wide and the gasket too large for the eye socket — no matter how good the sale is. And whatever pair you settle on, keep some practice time with no goggles at all: a child who has only ever opened their eyes behind a lens is unprepared for the day they go in without one.

🧢 2. Swim Cap — Size by Hair Volume, Not Age

A child with thick or long hair may need an adult-size cap at eight years old. Fit the hair, not the birthday

What you seeWhat it meansWhat to doOurs
Red marks on the forehead, pops off mid-swim, or will not cover all the hairToo smallMove up a size. Check the manufacturer’s head-circumference measurement in centimeters rather than the age label______
Air pockets, slides off easily, hair escapes at the edgesToo largeMove down a size or try a different brand — cap shape varies more between makers than size labels suggest______
Snug without pain, covers all the hair, stays on through turns and dives, leaves no marks when removedCorrectNote the size and the head measurement so the next one can be bought without a trip to the shop______
Child refuses to wear it at allUsually a material or choice problem, not a size problemSilicone suits most children; Lycra is gentler on sensitive scalps and thick hair; latex only if a program requires it. Let them pick the design______
The cap is not the first line of hair defense. Pre-wetting hair with fresh water before getting in does more than the cap does — hair already saturated with clean water absorbs far less pool water. Pre-wet, add a leave-in conditioner or oil barrier, then put the cap on. A cap never keeps hair fully dry; it cuts total exposure.

🌡️ 3. Thermal Wear — Does Your Child Need One at All?

Answer the temperature question first. Most families who buy a wetsuit for an indoor infant class did not need one

Ask the programThe answer that decides itOurs
“What temperature do you hold the water at for infant classes?”At 88°F or above with lessons of 30 minutes or less, thermal wear is typically not necessary. Consistently below 87°F, it becomes appropriate______
“Is the pool indoor, outdoor, or open water?”Outdoor pools vary seasonally and open-water programs vary daily — both push toward thermal wear regardless of the headline number______
“How long is the in-water portion, really?”Duration multiplies temperature. A 45-minute class at 87°F is a colder experience than a 20-minute one at 86°F______
Has your own child shown a pattern of cold sensitivity?Lethargy, blue lips or fussing within about 15 minutes in a standard heated pool is your answer, whatever the thermometer says______

If you do need one — the shoulder pinch test.

  • Pinch the neoprene at the shoulder or upper back. A small bit of fabric means the fit is right. A large fold means it is too big. Nothing to pinch at all may mean too tight — check that arms and legs move through full range.
  • Cuffs reach the wrists and ankles, and the neck opening sits flush without gapping at the back or sides.
  • Size by weight, not by age. Weight varies enormously between babies of the same age. Use the weight chart, and when you are between sizes, size up — slightly loose is manageable, too tight restricts movement.
  • If the suit rides up or the cuffs leave red marks, it is the wrong size or the wrong cut for your baby’s proportions. Return it rather than working around it.
Cold stress — leave the water at the first sign, suit or no suit. Blue or purple lips, fingernails or skin around the mouth; shivering or fine trembling; limpness or reduced responsiveness; pale, blotchy skin; crying that worsens rather than settles. Get out, wrap in a warm towel, hold skin-to-skin somewhere warm and draft-free. If normal color and activity do not return within 10 minutes of warming, call your pediatrician. A wetsuit slows cold stress down — it does not remove the need to watch for it.

👂 4. Ear Plugs and Nose Clips — Optional Unless a Doctor Says Otherwise

ItemWhen it earns its placeHow to get it rightOurs
Ear plugsEar tubes, recurrent ear infections, or a doctor’s recommendation. Otherwise optionalSwim-specific soft silicone or moldable plugs — not foam plugs made for noise. For children with tubes, some families get custom-molded plugs from an audiologist. Plugs reduce moisture, they do not eliminate it: still tilt the head to drain and dry the outer ear afterwards______
Nose clipSettling a nervous child, or backstrokeComfort aid, not a fixture. Keep teaching nose-breathing and bubble-blowing alongside it so the clip does not become the only way your child can put their face in______
Prescription gogglesA child whose vision correction genuinely changes how they experience the waterRun exactly the same four-step suction test. Corrective lenses do not exempt a pair from having to seal______
Medical decisions belong with your pediatrician. If your child has tubes, grommets or a history of ear infections, ask the doctor what to use before buying anything — the right answer differs by child and by procedure.

🎽 5. The Swimsuit — Choose the Color First

The one piece of gear on this sheet where the choice does affect safety — because it decides how fast someone can find your child in the water

  • Pick the color before the style. Independent visibility testing by water safety organizations consistently finds neon shades — hot pink, lime green, electric orange, bright yellow — spotted faster and from farther away than navy, black or dark green.
  • If it has a pattern, the dominant color must be the bright one. Bright accents on a dark base behave like a dark suit in the water.
  • Match the color to where your child actually swims. Avoid greens and blues in lakes and rivers, where they blend with the water; hot pink, orange and neon yellow contrast best with ocean blue. Neon hot pink and lime green are the safest choices if your child swims in more than one kind of water.
  • Check it in daylight, not shop lighting, which is closer to how it will look through moving water.
  • Make it the family default. A high-visibility suit shortens the search; it does not replace a Water Watcher, a life jacket, or a fence.

♻️ 6. Replace-By Signals

Gear rarely announces that it has failed. These are the signs that it already has

ItemReplace whenChecked on
GogglesThe gasket feels stiff or shows fine cracks, or the pair fails the suction test it used to pass. UV exposure hardens silicone, so a pair used for a full season can need replacing while still looking fine — retest the seal at the start of every season______
Swim capIt tears at the edge, no longer covers all the hair, or leaves marks it never used to leave______
Thermal wearThe cuffs stop reaching the wrists and ankles, the neck gaps, or your child has moved out of the weight range on the chart______
Ear plugsThey stop sealing, harden, or a molded pair no longer matches a growing ear canal______
SwimsuitThe color has faded toward dark or muddy, the fabric has gone thin or transparent, or it no longer stays put when your child pushes off a wall______
Life jacket (not optional gear)Any tear, missing strap or broken buckle — and any time your child moves out of the weight range printed on the label. Check it against the label, not against how it looks______

⭐ 7. The Rule That Outranks Every Row Above

  • Your child can submerge, hold their breath and open their eyes with no gear on. Test it, in a lesson or with you at arm’s length in shallow water.
  • Your child can perform their survival skills wearing nothing but a swimsuit — roll to the back, float, breathe, and get to the wall or the side.
  • Nobody in the family treats any item on this sheet as a flotation device. Goggles, caps, clips and warmth suits do nothing to keep a child at the surface.
  • The gear is comfort. The skills are the safety. A child who has never swum without goggles has a gap that no amount of well-fitting gear closes — because the fall into a pool never happens on lesson day, with the bag packed.
Where the real protection sits. Formal swim lessons are associated with a large reduction in drowning risk for young children, and drowning remains a leading cause of death for children ages 1–4. Fitting the goggles well is worth ten minutes. Being in the water every week is worth incomparably more.

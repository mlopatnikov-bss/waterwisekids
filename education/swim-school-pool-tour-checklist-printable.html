Swim School Pool Tour Card

Most swim school comparisons happen on a laptop — price, schedule, ratio, reviews. None of that tells you whether the pool itself suits your child. Water temperature, deck air, teaching depth, how a beginner gets in, and whether you can see anything from the parent seat are all decided by the building, and all of them are visible in a ten-minute walk-through. This card is what to check while you are standing there, and what to ask when the answer is not what you hoped.

📝 Start Here — How to Use This Card

  • Print one copy per school. The point of the card is the comparison, and memory is unreliable three days and two tours later.
  • Bring the child if you can, but the tour is for you. You are measuring the building. The teaching is a separate question with a separate checklist.
  • Ask for numbers, not adjectives. “Warm” is a feeling. “Eighty-eight degrees, held year round” is a fact somebody is accountable for.
  • The facility model does not decide the teaching quality. A strong instructor in a rented host pool beats a weak one behind a glass viewing wall every week. Check both, separately, and do not let a smart lobby answer a question about instruction.
  • Try to visit during a lesson, not an empty afternoon. Air handling, noise, sightlines and changing-room crush all look fine in an empty building.
Why the building matters at all. Two physical features shape a young child’s experience more than anything else on the tour: how warm the water is and how deep it is where they will be standing. A child who is cold or out of their depth spends the lesson managing that instead of learning, and thirty minutes is short enough already.

🌡️ 2. Water Temperature — Ask the Number

  • Ask what the pool is set to, not what it feels like. A school that is proud of the answer will give you the number without checking notes; some publish their water specification as a selling point.
  • Ask whether it is held year round. Outdoor and seasonally heated pools swing, and the pool you tour in August is not the pool your child swims in during March.
  • Ask whether the teaching pool is separate from the lap pool. Shared water usually means a compromise temperature, and the compromise is rarely made in the beginner’s favour.
  • Warmer is not automatically better. Water above 92°F becomes uncomfortable for an active swimmer, and above 90°F creates an environment where certain bacteria and fungi thrive.
  • Put your hand in if you are allowed to. Not a measurement — a sanity check against the number you were just given.
Three organisations, three slightly different ranges — all for the same thing. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends 86–90°F for young children’s swim lessons, the American Red Cross recommends 84–88°F for instructional programmes with young children, and the YMCA recommends 86–88°F for learn-to-swim. These are guidance ranges rather than a legal standard, and they overlap where it counts: mid-to-high eighties for a small child who is learning.
What pools actually run, which is a different number. Purpose-built teaching pools commonly operate at roughly 86–92°F, while rented host pools often sit cooler at roughly 80–84°F and competitive pools at 78–82°F. So a school renting time in a lap pool is not being careless when it quotes you 83°F — it is telling you honestly that the water was set for somebody else. That is worth knowing before you enrol a three-year-old, not after.
The gap that matters. If your five-year-old would be learning in 80°F water while the AAP range for that age is 86–90°F, that is a legitimate concern and a fair thing to raise. Ask what the school does about it: a warmer slot, a shorter session, a wetsuit or thermal rash top, or a different pool. A programme with a good answer will have heard the question before.

🌬️ 3. The Air — Breathe Before You Look

  • Stand on the deck and take three normal breaths. A well-ventilated pool smells only faintly of chlorine even when it is busy.
  • A strong, stinging odour is the warning sign, not the reassurance. That smell is chloramines — formed when chlorine reacts with sweat, urine, sunscreen and body oils — and it signals a ventilation or chemistry problem rather than a clean pool.
  • Check your own eyes after five minutes. Watering or stinging eyes on a short tour tells you what a thirty-minute lesson twice a week will be like.
  • Notice whether the air feels heavy or humid. Chloramine gas collects at the water surface, which is exactly where a small child’s face is.
  • Ask what the ventilation and treatment set-up is. Useful answers mention high air-turnover HVAC, UV secondary disinfection or regenerative media filtration. A blank look is itself an answer.
  • Ask how often water chemistry is tested. A school that is on top of it will tell you the cadence without looking it up.
Say it out loud if anyone in the family has asthma. The CDC links high chloramine exposure to eye irritation, respiratory symptoms and aggravated asthma. An indoor pool is not off the table — a well-ventilated one is usually fine — but this is the one item on the card where a bad answer should change the decision rather than start a negotiation.

📏 4. Depth Where Your Child Will Stand

Depth in the teaching area: _______
Where that lands on your child: ____
Can the instructor stand there?: ____
Depth of “the deep end”: __________
Is there a shallow shelf or ledge?: ___
How fast the floor drops away: _____
  • Ask where your child’s class actually swims, then look at that spot rather than at the pool in general.
  • A shallow teaching depth lets the instructor stand. That matters more than it sounds: an instructor who can stand can support a nervous beginner with their hands rather than with their whole body.
  • Chest-deep is confidence; chin-deep is management. A child who can put their feet down and be safe will spend the lesson trying things. A child who cannot will spend it holding on.
  • Watch how quickly the floor drops. Lap and municipal pools often reach standard depth fast, which can intimidate a new swimmer and forces more reliance on the instructor’s hold.
  • Ask what happens at the next level up. Deep-water skills need deep water, and a school with nowhere to progress to will hit a ceiling before your child does.
Do not be sold on size. A longer pool is not a better learning pool. For a beginner, warm water, a depth they can stand in and an instructor within reach do more than length ever will — a large competition pool is a feature for swim teams, not for a four-year-old learning to float.

🪜 5. Getting In and Out

  • Look at the entry. Steps, a ramp or a zero-depth beach entry suit beginners; a vertical ladder into open water is a hurdle before the lesson has started.
  • Handrails and a graspable edge all the way round. A child who can reach the wall and hold it has somewhere to go when something goes wrong.
  • Deck surface under bare wet feet. Textured and dry-ish, or glossy tile with standing water? Watch where staff walk and how they walk there.
  • Clutter on the deck. Loose kickboards, hoses, cones and bags in the walking route are a small thing that says something larger about how the space is run.
  • Ask about the main drain covers. They should be present, intact and anti-entrapment rated. It is a fair question and a quick one.
  • Ask who is watching the water besides the instructor — whether there is a dedicated lifeguard during lessons, and where they stand.

👀 6. The View From the Parent Seat

  • Sit down where parents actually sit and look for the teaching area. Do it before you decide anything else about the room.
  • Can you see your child’s face, or only the back of a group? Glass viewing walls, deck seating and a corridor window are three very different experiences.
  • Glare, fog and reflections. A viewing window that mists over on a busy evening is a window you cannot use.
  • Noise. If you cannot hear the instructor from the gallery, neither can a nervous child at the far end of the lane.
  • Is the viewing area climate-controlled? A humid pool deck for thirty minutes twice a week, in outdoor clothes, quietly wears families down and is a real reason enrolments lapse.
  • Where do siblings go? A toddler with nowhere to be turns every lesson into a logistics problem.
Sightlines are a safety item as well as a comfort one. Being able to see your own child in the water is the whole basis of parent supervision, and it is worth knowing on day one whether the room lets you do that or asks you to trust the room instead.

🧳 7. Changing, Drying and Getting Out the Door

  • Family or private changing space, or a shared locker room you will be navigating with a wet four-year-old and a bag.
  • Warm, draught-free changing. A cold changing room undoes a good part of what a warm pool bought you, in the ten minutes that matter most.
  • Somewhere to shower or rinse. A quick rinse before swimming removes the sweat and oils that create chloramines in the first place, so a school that encourages it is helping its own air.
  • Parking and the walk from the car. Count the minutes, in winter, with a wet child. This is the item that most often decides whether a family keeps coming.
  • Stroller and buggy access. Flat entries and a place to park it, or three steps and a fire door.
  • What else runs in the building at your slot? Rented and shared pools can mean a water-aerobics class beside your child’s lesson, or a swim meet that eats the car park.

🚩 8. Answers That Should Slow You Down

  • “It’s nice and warm.” Asked for a number, given a feeling. Ask again once; if there is still no number, assume nobody is watching it.
  • “That strong chlorine smell means it’s clean.” It means the opposite, and a programme that believes otherwise is not managing its air.
  • “You can’t see the pool, but we’ll send photos.” Photos are a nice extra. They are not a substitute for a sightline.
  • “We can’t show you the pool today.” Reasonable once, with a reason and an offer of another time. A pattern of it is a pattern.
  • “Depth doesn’t really matter, the instructor holds them.” For a first lesson, perhaps. As a philosophy, it describes a child who never gets to try anything alone.
  • Any answer that arrives with irritation. You are asking the questions a good programme wants asked. The reaction is data.
One bad answer is not a disqualification. Rented pools trade water temperature for being close to where families live, and that can be the right trade. What you are looking for is whether the school knows its own weak point and has a plan for it, or has never thought about it.

⭐ 10. The Lines That Matter Most

  • The building predicts comfort and logistics, not teaching. Water temperature, air quality, depth and parking are set by the facility. Whether your child learns is set by the instructor, the ratio and the curriculum — check those separately and do not let one stand in for the other.
  • Warm water is not a guarantee of a good programme. It removes an obstacle to learning. The learning still has to be taught.
  • Ask the questions out loud, even the awkward ones. A school that welcomes them is telling you something no brochure can.
  • No programme makes a child drown-proof. Lessons are one layer of protection alongside four-sided pool fencing, constant undistracted supervision within arm’s reach of a young or non-swimming child, properly fitted life jackets in open water, and an adult trained in CPR.
  • Do not let the perfect pool delay the first lesson. Enrol somewhere reasonable now and keep looking if you must. Time out of the water is the one cost this card cannot help you recover.
Why the tour is worth the ten minutes. Drowning remains a leading cause of death for children ages 1–4, and participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a large reduction in that risk. The programme you actually keep attending is the one that protects your child — which is why the mundane things on this card, the walk from the car and the temperature of the water, end up mattering as much as the curriculum does.

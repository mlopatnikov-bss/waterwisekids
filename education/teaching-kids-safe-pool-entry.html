Teaching Kids to Enter the Pool Safely

Getting into the water seems like the easy part of swimming — until a child runs off the edge into water over their head, lands on a sibling, or dives headfirst into a shallow end. Safe entry is a real, teachable skill, and it deserves the same attention as the strokes.

Quick Answer: Teach children to stop at the edge, check the depth and that an adult is watching, and enter feet first — never headfirst into shallow or unknown water. Start young children with the seated slide-in, progress to controlled feet-first jumps into confirmed-deep water, and always pair entry with getting back to the wall. The rule "feet first, every time, until you're told otherwise" prevents most pool entry injuries.

Why entry deserves real teaching

Walk past any pool on a summer day and you will see the most common entry: a running leap off the edge with no look at what is below. Most of the time nothing goes wrong. But the times it does go wrong are serious — a non-swimmer who jumps into the deep end, a child who lands on another swimmer, or, most gravely, a headfirst dive into water too shallow to absorb it.

Headfirst entries into shallow or unknown-depth water are a leading cause of serious neck and spinal injuries in recreational swimming. The good news is that nearly all of these are preventable with a simple, well-drilled habit. Entry is not just splashing in; it is a decision a child makes dozens of times a day, and we can teach them to make it safely.

Skills are a safety layer: The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) lists swimming competence among the layers of protection against drowning — and competence includes knowing how to get in and out of the water safely, not just how to swim across it. Entry and exit are foundational water-safety skills, not extras.

Step one: stop and check

Before any entry, teach your child a two-second pause at the edge with three quick checks: How deep is it? Is the water clear and free of other swimmers? Is an adult watching and ready? This pause is the most important habit of all, because it converts entry from an impulse into a decision. Practice it out loud at first — "Stop. Check. Okay" — until it becomes automatic.

The depth check is non-negotiable. Children should never assume a pool is deep enough to jump into or shallow enough to stand in; depths change, and an unfamiliar pool gives no clues. This is the same principle that makes a pool with cloudy water off-limits — if you cannot see and confirm the bottom, you do not get in, as we explain in cloudy pool water safety.

Step two: the seated slide-in for beginners

For toddlers and new swimmers, the safest entry is the seated slide-in, and it is usually the first entry taught in lessons. The child sits on the edge of the pool, places both hands flat on the deck to one side, then turns the body and lowers themselves into the water while facing the wall. Because they end up facing the edge, they can immediately grab the wall and hold on.

This entry is controlled, eliminates surprises about depth, and naturally connects to the most important survival skill of all: holding the wall and climbing out. Practice it with hands-on support at first, then let the child do it independently while you stay within arm's reach.

Step three: controlled feet-first entries

Once a child is comfortable and swimming a little, you can teach a feet-first step or jump — but only into water you have confirmed is deep enough, and with an adult positioned to help. Teach them to look before they leap, keep the body upright, and after surfacing, turn and move to the wall or steps.

Feet first is the safe default for children for one simple reason: it protects the head and neck. Even if the water turns out shallower than expected, a feet-first entry rarely causes the catastrophic injury a headfirst dive can. Make "feet first, every time" the family rule, with no exceptions until a child is an able swimmer being formally taught to dive.

The never-dive rule (and when it lifts)

Diving headfirst is a genuine skill with a genuine place — but it belongs only in clearly marked deep water, taught under adult instruction, to children who are already competent swimmers. Until then, the rule is absolute: no diving, no headfirst entries, ever. Most home and community pools are simply not deep enough for a child to dive safely, and the deceptively shallow water near the surface of a lake or the slope of a pool's shallow end is exactly where dive injuries happen.

Teach children that diving into water of unknown depth — a lake, a friend's pool, the shallow end — is one of the most dangerous things a swimmer can do. This connects directly to why pool skills don't transfer to open water, where murky water hides the bottom entirely.

Step four: tie entry to getting out

Safe entry is only half the skill. Teach children that every entry ends with getting back to safety — turning to the wall, finding the steps, and climbing out. Practicing entry and exit together builds the reflex that can save a life if a child ever falls in by accident: orient toward the edge, get to it, and climb out. We cover the climb-out itself in teaching kids to climb out of the pool, a survival skill many children never practice.

When entry, swimming, floating, and exit are all taught as one connected chain, a child has a complete picture of how to handle the water — not just how to play in it. That is the difference between a child who can swim and a child who is water-safe.

Family pool-entry rules

  • Stop and check depth, clearance, and supervision before every entry.
  • Feet first, every time, until a child is formally taught to dive.
  • No diving except into marked deep water with adult instruction.
  • One at a time — never enter where someone is already in the landing zone.
  • No running on the deck to the edge, the cause of many slips and falls.
  • Get to the wall after every entry.

Drill these until they are second nature. An entry habit a child carries to every pool, lake, and water park is one of the simplest, highest-value safety skills you can give them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is jumping into a pool dangerous for kids?

Jumping in without checking can land a child in water over their head, into another swimmer, or onto a shallow bottom. Headfirst entries into shallow or unknown water are a leading cause of serious neck and head injuries. Teaching children to stop, check depth, and enter feet first prevents most of these.

At what age can a child learn safe pool entry?

Toddlers can learn the seated slide-in with hands-on help, and most children learn controlled feet-first entries during preschool and early elementary years. The rule to stop and check depth develops alongside the physical skill, so it should be taught and reinforced every time, not assumed.

Should kids dive headfirst into a pool?

Only into clearly marked deep water, under adult instruction, once they are competent swimmers. Diving headfirst into shallow or unknown water risks catastrophic head and spine injury. The safe default for children is always feet first; diving is a separate skill taught later with strict depth rules.

What is the safest way for a young child to get into a pool?

The seated slide-in: sit on the edge, place both hands to one side, then turn and lower the body facing the wall so the child can immediately hold on. It keeps entry controlled, avoids depth surprises, and pairs naturally with learning to grab the wall and climb out.

Why should a child grab the wall after jumping in?

Returning to the wall or steps teaches that getting back to safety is part of every entry. It builds the reflex that saves lives if a child falls in unexpectedly: orient, get to the edge, climb out. Entry and exit should always be practiced together.

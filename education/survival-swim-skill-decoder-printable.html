Survival Swim Skill Decoder

None of these skill names are standardised — they are each program's vocabulary for the same small set of actions. Here is what your child is actually doing, what finished looks like, and what to ask before you enrol.

🔤 The Skill Names, Decoded

  • Roll to back float — also rollover, flip to float, roll to breathe. The child turns from face-down to face-up, clears the mouth and nose, and rests there breathing. Breathing is what runs out first; this is the skill that buys time.
  • Jump-turn-swim — also jump-push-turn-grab, turn and return. After an unexpected entry the child turns back toward where they fell in, travels the short distance to the wall, and holds on. The word “jump” describes how it is rehearsed, not the event it answers.
  • Swim-float-swim — the distance strategy. Swim face-down until out of air, roll up to breathe, roll back down, repeat until reaching an edge. Later and harder than the roll alone, because it requires stopping and restarting without panic.
  • Monkey crawl — also wall crawl, monkey walk. Hand-over-hand travel along the wall or gutter to a ladder, step, or shallow end. It is how a child covers ground once they have a grip.
  • Elbow, elbow, tummy, knee — the exit. The sequence a small body actually uses to get up over a pool edge. Reaching the wall is not the same as getting out, and programs that skip this leave the story unfinished.
  • Blowing bubbles — breath control. Exhaling through the nose underwater. Unglamorous, and the prerequisite for every other skill on this page.
  • Water competency — the umbrella term. The Red Cross framework term for the whole set: entering, surfacing, turning, floating, moving, and exiting. When a school uses it, ask which parts they actually teach.

✅ What Genuine Competency Looks Like — Tick What You Have Seen

  • Performs it without being placed into position. No instructor hands guiding the roll or the turn.
  • Performs it after an unexpected entry, not a countdown. Rehearsed on cue is a different skill from produced under surprise.
  • Performs it in water too deep to stand in. Standing up is not a recovery.
  • Performs it wearing clothes or a swim shirt. Fabric changes buoyancy and drag more than parents expect.
  • Performs it on a cold, tired, or unhappy day. The conditions of a real incident are never the conditions of a good lesson.
  • Still performs it a week later with no refresher. Skills that need a warm-up are not yet skills.

🚩 Red Flags in How a Program Talks About These Skills

  • Any language implying your child will be “safe” or “drown-proof.” There is no such state and no program can deliver it.
  • A skill guaranteed in a fixed number of lessons. Children are not on a schedule; a guarantee is a sales term, not a teaching one.
  • No answer to what happens if your child cannot do it. A program without a plan for that has one plan for every child.
  • Clothed testing you were not told about in advance. Testing in clothes can be defensible; doing it without consent is not.
  • Skills described as a substitute for fencing or supervision. This is the single most dangerous claim in the category.
  • Distress framed as a necessary part of learning. Ask what they do when a child is upset, and listen for a real answer.

❓ Ask the School These Six Questions

  • Which of these skills do you teach, and in what order? You are listening for a curriculum, not a brochure.
  • How do you decide my child has learned a skill rather than done it once? The answer should include repetition, deep water, and no assistance.
  • Do you test in clothes? What does it look like, and will I know first? Consent before the session, not an explanation after.
  • What happens if my child becomes distressed? Ask for the specific step, not the philosophy.
  • How quickly do these skills fade, and what is your policy if we take a break? Decay is normal; a school that pretends otherwise is not being straight.
  • What do you tell parents these skills do not do? The best programs answer this fastest.

⚠️ What These Skills Do Not Replace

  • Four-sided isolation fencing with a self-closing, self-latching gate. The barrier works when nobody is watching, which is when incidents happen.
  • Constant supervision within arm's reach for young children. A designated water watcher with no phone, rotating on a timer.
  • Coast Guard-approved life jackets in open water. A skill practised in a pool is not a plan for a lake or the ocean.
  • CPR training for caregivers — and for teenagers in the house. The minutes before help arrives belong to whoever is there.
  • Emptying and storing buckets, tubs, and inflatable pools. Small water is where the youngest children are lost.

The Vocabulary Matters Less Than the Teaching

Two schools can teach the same five actions and call them completely different things. What separates them is how they decide a skill is learned, what they do when a child struggles, and whether they are straight with you about what the skills do not cover. Formal swim lessons are associated with a reduction in drowning risk of up to 88% for children ages 1–4 — ask the six questions and pick the program that answers them plainly.

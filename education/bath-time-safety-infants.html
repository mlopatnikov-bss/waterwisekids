Baby Bath Safety: Bath Time Rules for Infants and Toddlers

Bath time should be enjoyable, not terrifying. Learn the expert-backed strategies to keep your infant or toddler safe while building water confidence and establishing lifelong healthy habits around water.

Quick Answer: Stay with your baby or toddler for the entire bath. Keep a hand on your baby, gather supplies before you begin, and take your child with you if you need to leave. Drowning can happen quickly and quietly in shallow water. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises avoiding infant bath seats and rings.
Critical Safety Rule
Drowning happens quickly and silently. Never leave your infant or toddler unattended in or near water, not even for a second—not to answer the door, grab a towel, or check your phone. Constant, hands-on supervision is non-negotiable.

Baby bath safety: the rules that actually prevent drowning

Infant bath safety comes down to one non-negotiable rule and five supporting ones. The non-negotiable: a baby or toddler is never alone in the bath, not for a moment, not for a ringing phone, not with an older sibling as the supervisor. Drowning can happen quickly and quietly in shallow water.

The five supporting rules:

  • Touch supervision. One hand on your child for the entire bath. Arm's reach is not close enough for an infant who cannot right themselves.
  • Use warm water. Mix and test it before your child gets in. The AAP recommends a maximum of 120°F (49°C) at the faucet to reduce scald risk.
  • Use shallow water. Keep your baby’s face and most of their body above the water.
  • Avoid infant bath seats and rings. They can tip or allow a child to slip. Set up an infant tub according to its instructions and keep a hand on your baby.
  • Gather everything first. Keep the towel, washcloth and clean clothes within reach. Take your child with you if you need to step away.

The sections below cover this by age, plus the specific hazards at each stage. If you want the print-and-stick version, our printable infant water safety checklist covers the bath alongside the rest of the home.

Why does bath time require extra vigilance for young children?

Bath time demands hands-on, uninterrupted supervision because infants and toddlers can drown quickly and quietly in shallow water. Bath time is one of the most frequent water exposures for infants and toddlers, which means it's also a significant injury risk if not managed properly. Unlike swimming pools or beaches where water safety is expected, parents sometimes let their guard down at home—a dangerous mistake. For a room-by-room routine, use our bathtub safety checklist, and review the added precautions for babies under one year old.

Young children have characteristics that make them uniquely vulnerable in water:

  • Immature coordination: Their bodies don't yet have the muscle control to right themselves if they slip
  • Disproportionate head size: A baby's head is roughly 25% of their body weight, making it harder to stay upright
  • No water instinct: Infants don't hold their breath automatically when water reaches their face—this reflex develops much later
  • Quick temperature loss: Babies lose heat rapidly in water, affecting coordination and consciousness
  • Limited communication: A young child may not be able to verbally communicate distress

The CDC identifies drowning as the leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 4 in the United States. Bathtubs are a particular drowning hazard for infants.

What do the drowning facts say about bathtub incidents?

Drowning can happen quickly and quietly, even in shallow bathwater. The American Academy of Pediatrics says most child drownings inside the home occur in bathtubs. Keep a hand on your baby throughout the bath. If you must leave, take your child out and bring them with you.

What are the age-specific bath safety rules?

At every age from newborn through age 4, children require hands-on, in-the-bathroom supervision. Adapt the setup as they grow. Bath safety changes as children grow and develop new capabilities. What's appropriate for a 6-month-old is different from a 2-year-old or 4-year-old. Here are age-specific guidelines:

0-6M Newborn to 6 Months

Supervision requirements:

  • One adult must maintain hands-on contact at all times—literally touching the baby
  • Never leave the bathroom, even briefly
  • Phone, door, and other distractions should be eliminated
  • Bathe only when you're fully alert and able to focus

Bath setup:

  • Use an infant tub that meets current safety standards and follow its instructions.
  • Always run cool water last to prevent hot water scalding if someone bumps the faucet

What newborns need:

  • Support for their head and neck with your hand and arm
  • Gentle handling to prevent slipping
  • Quick, efficient baths to prevent heat loss (5-10 minutes)
  • Immediate wrapping in a warm towel afterward
6-12M 6 Months to 1 Year

Supervision requirements:

  • Hands-on contact and presence in the bathroom at all times
  • Even though babies this age may sit up, they can still slip and fall forward
  • Keep your hands within immediate reach
  • Do not walk away, answer doors, check phones, or multitask

Bath setup:

  • Avoid using an infant bath seat or ring. Keep a hand on your child.
  • Use an infant tub only while your child fits the manufacturer’s size and developmental limits.

What to expect:

  • Increased interest in water play—splashing, reaching
  • Beginning attempts to move (crawling, pulling up)
  • No true self-righting ability yet—they cannot reliably recover if they slip
  • Still vulnerable to rapid heat loss
12-24M 1 to 2 Years

Supervision requirements:

  • Hands-on supervision is still absolutely required
  • Toddlers are mobile, curious, and unpredictable—they may try to climb out, lean over the side, or drink the bath water
  • Stay within arm's reach at all times
  • Keep the bathroom door locked when not in use to prevent unsupervised access

Bath setup:

  • Adult tub with non-slip mat for safety
  • Keep a hand on your child, even once they can sit up on their own.
  • Remove bath toys when not actively bathing to prevent choking hazards

What to watch for:

  • Toddlers may try to stand unassisted in the tub—slippery surfaces make falls common
  • They may attempt to drink bathwater
  • Curiosity about controls—keep drain covers in place to prevent finger entrapment
  • Increased independence but zero actual water safety knowledge
2-3Y 2 to 3 Years

Supervision requirements:

  • Still require constant supervision—this is not the age where they can "play alone in the tub"
  • Toddlers this age are fast, mobile, and may not listen to safety instructions reliably
  • Stay within arm's reach at all times
  • Lock the bathroom door from the inside to prevent escape

Bath setup:

  • Adult tub with non-slip mat and grab bars if available
  • Ensure faucet has a soft cover to prevent head bumps

Teaching moments:

  • Begin teaching basic safety language: "We stay sitting in the tub," "No running"
  • Model and practice water safety behaviors
  • Make it clear that the adult is in charge of water activities, not the child
3-4Y 3 to 4 Years

Supervision requirements:

  • Hands-on supervision is still required, though some children show better judgment
  • Some 3-4 year-olds can follow safety directions, but they still cannot be trusted with water safety independently
  • Stay in the bathroom with them—do not step away

Bath setup:

  • Adult tub with secure, non-slip mat

Building independence appropriately:

  • You can gradually reduce hands-on contact as their skills develop, but only in your clear sight line
  • Never step away, even briefly
  • Introduce concepts like "slow movements" and "sitting in the tub"

What bathing equipment makes infant baths safer?

Set up a stable infant tub and have your supplies within reach before you begin. The right equipment makes safe supervision easier. Here's what to use—and what to avoid:

Essential safety items:

  • Non-slip bath mat: Prevents sliding and provides texture for grip. Replace regularly as they become worn.
  • Infant tub: Choose one that meets current safety standards and use it only as directed by its manufacturer. Stay with your baby and keep a hand on them throughout the bath.
  • Bath thermometer: You can use one when checking the water before your child gets in. The water should feel warm.
  • Soft faucet cover: Prevents injuries if the child bumps their head on the faucet edge.
  • Washcloths and soft bath products: Gentle on delicate skin and easier to control around the face.
  • Towels: Use several warm towels, especially for younger infants who lose heat quickly.

Items to avoid or use cautiously:

  • Bath seats and rings: Avoid them. Seats can tip or let a child slip into the water.
  • Too many toys: Bath toys can obstruct your view of the child and present choking hazards. Keep the area clear.
  • Bubble bath in large quantities: While small amounts are generally fine, excessive bubbles can obscure your view of the child and irritate skin.

Why should you never leave a child alone in or near water?

This bears repeating because it is the single most important bath safety rule: Never leave your child unattended in the bathtub. Not for one second. Not to answer the door. Not to grab a towel you forgot. Not to quickly check your phone.

This is not exaggeration. Drowning in bathwater can happen quickly—often silently, with no splashing or call for help. A child who slips under the water and panics cannot reliably recover. Even children who have taken swim lessons and understand the concept of water safety cannot always apply those skills when surprised or frightened.

Practical strategies to ensure constant supervision:

  • Gather everything before the bath: Towels, clean clothes, diapers, wash items—have it all within arm's reach before you start.
  • Use a baby gate to block bathroom access: If you have other children, prevent them from bursting in and startling you.
  • Turn off your phone or put it in another room: Out of sight, out of mind. Resist the urge to check notifications.
  • Lock the bathroom door: Prevent unsupervised access by other household members or pets.
  • Have a plan if the doorbell rings or someone calls: Decide in advance that you'll ignore it. No delivery is worth leaving a child unattended in water.
  • Bathe during calm times: When you're not exhausted, stressed, or managing multiple crises, you're better able to stay focused.
  • Use a water-resistant speaker or timer: If you need a timer, put it where you can hear it without stepping away.
  • Assign one adult to supervise: In households with multiple caregivers, make it clear who is responsible. No assumptions or shared responsibility—one person is "on duty."

How Long Can You Safely Leave a Child Alone in the Bath?

Not for any length of time — even in shallow water. This question comes up constantly, including on childcare and early-childhood certification exams, and the correct answer is always the same: a child in or near bath water is never left unattended, for any reason, at any depth. If you must leave the room, the child leaves the room with you — wrapped in a towel and carried, or lifted out and set down somewhere safe.

Even shallow water can cover a child’s nose and mouth if they slip. Stay within reach and give the bath your full attention.

The practical version of the rule for households: gather everything you need before the water goes in — towel, washcloth, clean nappy, clothes, lotion — so that nothing can force you out of the room mid-bath. Let the doorbell ring. Let the phone go to voicemail. And drain the tub the moment the bath ends, so that a toddler who wanders back in later finds an empty one.

What Is National Bath Safety Month?

National Bath Safety Month is observed each January in the United States, and it exists to draw attention to the fact that the bathroom is one of the most hazardous rooms in the home for a young child — combining drowning risk, scald risk and fall risk in a single space. January is deliberate: it lands in the season when families are indoors, baths are longer and hotter, and water heaters are often turned up.

It is a useful annual prompt to check the three things that quietly drift out of adjustment over a year:

  • Hot tap water. The AAP recommends a maximum of 120°F (49°C) at the faucet to reduce the risk of scalds. Always check the mixed bathwater before putting a child in.
  • Non-slip surfaces. Bath mats lose their grip as the suction cups age, and a slip in the tub is a head-injury risk that can lead to submersion.
  • Bath equipment. Avoid bath seats and rings. A stable infant tub still requires hands-on supervision.

If you are marking the month at home, the most durable action is to write the household rules down rather than reciting them. What is the safe water temperature for an infant bath?

Use warm bathwater and check it before putting your baby in. Mix the water and test it with your wrist or elbow. Turn the faucet off before your baby enters the water. The AAP recommends a maximum hot-water temperature of 120°F (49°C) at the faucet to reduce scald risk. Bathwater should be much cooler than that.

When Can Children Bathe Independently?

Babies and young children need direct adult supervision throughout the bath. For older children, discuss readiness with their pediatrician if you are unsure, particularly if they have a condition that affects balance, awareness or their ability to get out of the tub. Stay in the room whenever your child still needs direct help or supervision.

What should be on a complete bath time safety checklist?

Before every bath, assign an alert adult, gather supplies, and check the water. Keep the bath shallow, the water warm, and the faucet off while your child is in the tub.

  • Supervision: One alert adult assigned to watch the child. Phone off. No distractions.
  • Preparations: All supplies (towel, clothes, soap, thermometer) gathered and within arm's reach.
  • Temperature: Mix and test the water before your child enters.
  • Water depth: Use shallow water and keep your baby’s face and most of their body above it.
  • Non-slip surface: Bath mat in place and secure.
  • Faucet safety: Soft cover in place, faucet turned off or adjusted to prevent accidental spills.
  • Bathroom security: Door locked to prevent unsupervised access by other children or pets.
  • Bath setup: Avoid bath seats and rings. If you use an infant tub, follow its instructions.
  • Toy selection: Only age-appropriate, non-choking-hazard toys present and minimal to maintain clear sightlines.
  • Drain covers: Secure to prevent finger entrapment.
  • Time awareness: Plan to keep bath short (10-15 minutes for infants, 15-20 for toddlers).
  • Post-bath plan: Warm towels ready, change of clothes accessible, next steps planned.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water do infants need for drowning?

Infants can drown in shallow water. If a baby slips, even a small amount of water can cover their nose and mouth. Stay with your baby and keep a hand on them throughout the bath.

What is the proper water temperature for infant baths?

Use warm bathwater, mix it well, and test it with your wrist or elbow before putting your baby in. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends a maximum of 120°F (49°C) at the faucet to reduce scald risk. That figure is for hot tap water. Bathwater should be much cooler.

Can I leave a toddler unattended in the bath for a moment?

Stay with your toddler throughout the bath. If you must leave the room, take your child out of the water and bring them with you.

What bath safety equipment is essential?

Have a towel, washcloth and supplies within reach before you start. If you use an infant tub, follow its instructions and keep a hand on your baby. Avoid infant bath seats and rings because they can tip or let a child slip into the water.

How do I prevent bathroom water hazards beyond the tub?

Close toilet lids and use locking mechanisms, empty buckets and basins immediately after use, drain standing water from sinks, keep bathroom doors closed when not in use, ensure non-slip surfaces, and store all water-related items securely. Toddlers can drown in toilets, buckets, and standing water as easily as in tubs.

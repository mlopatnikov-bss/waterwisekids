When to Get Out of the Water

Children are the worst available judges of when to stop swimming. They underestimate their own fatigue, they do not want the fun to end, and a young child’s ability to sense that they are getting cold does not fully mature until around age ten to twelve — so by the time they are visibly shivering, they are already cold. This card puts the exit decision back where it belongs: with the adult, on a schedule, using signs you can see rather than answers you have to trust.

📝 Start Here — How to Use This Card

  • The top half is what you check at the pool. The bottom half is what you fill in on the way out. Neither takes longer than fifteen seconds.
  • Do not ask “are you cold?” or “are you tired?” You will get “no” either way, and the child will believe it. Look at the lips, the strokes and the wall instead.
  • Breaks go on a timer, not on request. Set the rhythm before anyone gets in, so it is a rule rather than a negotiation and nobody has to be caught tired first.
  • Praise the child who says “I need a rest.” Reporting tiredness is a skill worth building, and it will not build itself if stopping is treated as a punishment.
  • Fill in the log for a few swims before you trust any number on this page. Every figure here is a starting point. Your child is the actual answer.
Why the water is different. A tired runner stops and stands still. Swimming needs continuous muscle work just to hold position and keep breathing, so when those muscles give out, the body sinks. A child who can swim can still get into trouble once they are past their endurance — which is why the exit decision cannot wait for them to ask.

The temperature question is a fair one to ask. Front desks and instructors can usually tell you what the pool is set to. The younger and leaner the child, the warmer the water needs to be and the shorter the session should run — and the same programme can feel completely different in an outdoor pool in May and an indoor teaching pool in August.

🥶 2. Cold — Look, Don’t Ask

  • Lips, fingernails or the skin around the mouth turning blue or purple. The clearest early sign, and the one worth checking every time you look up.
  • Shivering or fine trembling. Treat this as late, not early. In a young child, visible shivering means they are already quite cold.
  • Pale or mottled, blotchy skin.
  • Decreased activity, limpness or reduced responsiveness.
  • Crying or complaining that gets worse rather than settling. A grizzle that fades is a mood. A grizzle that escalates in the water is usually temperature.
At the first sign, out. Warm dry towel, wrapped snugly. A baby or small child held against your chest. Somewhere warm and out of the draught. If normal colour and normal activity have not come back within about ten minutes of warming, call your pediatrician.
Why children cool so much faster than you do. Less insulating fat, more surface area for their size, and water that carries heat away from the body roughly twenty-five times faster than air. A pool that feels pleasant to an adult standing at the side can be genuinely cold to a child swimming in it.

🔍 3. Tired — The Five Signs From the Deck

  • The stroke shortens. Arms dropping below the surface on the pull, shallower kicks, more head-lifting to breathe, stroke rate slowing for no reason. Not bad technique — the body taking shortcuts.
  • The body rides lower. A fresh swimmer lies fairly flat. A tired one sinks at the hips and goes more vertical, which adds drag and burns energy faster still.
  • The wall gets more attractive. More time resting at the edge or the steps, or a growing reluctance to swim away from it. That instinct is healthy. It is also telling you the reserve is low.
  • The noise stops. A child who was splashing and shouting twenty minutes ago and is now quiet, withdrawn, clingy or irritable is not just cooling down.
  • The breathing changes. Laboured breathing after easy swimming rather than after a sprint, or “I can’t catch my breath.” This one is not a “take a break” sign. It is an out-now sign.
Distance rule that belongs with these. A child should never swim farther from safety — the wall, the steps, a dock, a supervising adult — than they could reliably reach if they suddenly ran out of energy. A child who can comfortably manage one length should not be doing two without a rest.

🦵 4. Cramp — Six Steps, In Order

  • 1. Stop swimming. Do not try to swim through it. A cramp can worsen fast, and a leg that will not work in deep water is a serious problem.
  • 2. Signal for help. One arm up, call out to a lifeguard or a parent, make noise. Asking for help in the water is always the right answer.
  • 3. Move to safety with the limbs that still work — nearest wall, ladder or shallow end. Slow and controlled beats fast and panicked.
  • 4. Float if the wall is too far. Roll onto the back. Floating costs almost no muscle effort and keeps the face up while the cramp eases.
  • 5. Stretch once safe. Calf cramp: flex the foot up toward the shin. Foot cramp: pull the toes back. Hold thirty to sixty seconds, no bouncing.
  • 6. Get out, drink, rest. The muscle stays fatigued and is likely to cramp again straight away. Reassess before going back in at all.
Rehearse this on dry land. Say the six steps out loud with your child before they get in, the way you would rehearse a fire drill. A cramp is not the moment to be inventing a plan.
Prevention is mostly water. Eight to sixteen ounces in the one to two hours before swimming — hydrating in the car on the way is too late. A few sips every twenty minutes at the pool even when they say they are not thirsty, because the water masks the feeling of sweating and dehydration makes fatigue considerably worse.

🚫 5. Breath-Holding — The One Absolute Rule

  • No breath-holding contests. Ever. Not “maybe if supervised,” not “carefully.” This is the one line on the card with no judgement call attached.
  • No underwater distance races and no games that reward staying under longest.
  • No big deep breaths before going under. Hyperventilating first is the specific danger: it removes the carbon dioxide build-up that would otherwise force a breath.
  • Tell whoever else is supervising. Grandparents, the babysitter, the older cousin at the party. A rule only one adult knows is not a rule.
  • Any deliberate breath-hold training belongs with a certified instructor in a controlled setting with a spotter and a plan — never unsupervised, and never as a game.
Why this one is absolute. Loss of consciousness underwater after breath-holding usually comes with no struggle at all — the swimmer goes still and silent, which is exactly what a watching adult is least likely to notice. The National Drowning Prevention Alliance and the YMCA both prohibit prolonged underwater breath-holding activities in their aquatic programmes.

Take the number to the desk. “He gets cold quickly” is easy to nod at. “He starts shivering about twenty-five minutes into every Tuesday lesson but never on Saturdays” is a question somebody can actually answer.

🚨 9. Get Out Now — The Four Non-Negotiables

  • Blue, grey or purple lips, nail beds or skin around the mouth. Out, dry, warm, and watch for colour and normal activity to return.
  • Laboured breathing after easy swimming, or “I can’t catch my breath.” Not after a sprint — after ordinary swimming.
  • A swimmer who has gone suddenly still, quiet or uncoordinated. Any change from a child’s normal pattern in the water gets checked immediately, not after the length.
  • Any cramp. The session is over for that muscle, whatever the child says once the pain passes.
Drowning is quiet. It rarely looks like the splashing and shouting people expect — which is why “he went still and stopped playing” belongs on this list rather than in the wait-and-see column. If you are not sure, get them out. Nobody has ever regretted an unnecessary rest break.

⭐ 10. The Lines That Matter Most

  • No lesson and no number of lessons makes a child drown-proof. There is no such thing, at any age or in any programme. Lessons change what your child can do; they do not change the supervision your child needs.
  • Lessons are one layer of protection, never the whole plan. Four-sided pool fencing, constant undistracted supervision within arm’s reach of a young or non-swimming child, properly fitted life jackets in open water and an adult trained in CPR are the others.
  • A break is not a failure. If the only lesson your child takes from this card is that stopping is allowed and asking is welcome, that is the whole win.
  • Keep swimming while you sort the details out. The worst outcome of this card would be a family who stays out of the water while they think about it. Shorten the session, do not cancel it.
Why any of this is worth the fifteen seconds. Drowning remains a leading cause of death for young children, and participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a large reduction in that risk. The lessons do the heavy lifting. Knowing when your own child has had enough is what keeps the ordinary Saturday afternoon between lessons ordinary.

The Best Way to Extend the Session Is to Build the Swimmer.

Endurance is what moves the get-out line later, and it is built through regular structured practice rather than through longer unsupervised swims. Participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a large reduction in drowning risk for young children, and drowning remains a leading cause of death for children ages 1–4 — so the fastest route to a child who can safely stay in longer is a good programme, not a longer afternoon.

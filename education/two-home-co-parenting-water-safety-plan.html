🏡 Why Two Homes Need One Plan

Many children split their week, their weekends or their holidays between two homes: two parents, a parent and grandparents, or a blended family with new partners and step-siblings. Each home can have different water, from a backyard pool or hot tub at one house to a bathtub and a neighborhood pond at the other, and each can have its own habits about who watches the kids. Holiday parenting schedules add more: relatives’ houses, hotel pools and winter trips. Children do best with the same rules and the same picture of their swimming in every place they sleep. This plan is a practical water safety tool that both households can fill in together or separately. It is not a custody or legal document, and it works whether co-parents talk every day or mostly by text.

🏊 Share One Swim Profile

The most common gap between homes is an out-of-date idea of what the child can do in the water. Keep one swim profile that both homes share: the child’s current class or level, the skills they can do on their own (such as floating on their back, turning around and getting back to the wall, or climbing out without the steps), the skills they are still working on, whether they need a life jacket and the instructor’s latest notes. Update it after each progress report and send it to the other home. Our water competency skills checklist and swim level translator help both parents describe the same skills in the same words, and the swim lesson medical information form keeps health and comfort needs in one place.

📏 Agree on the Same Water Rules in Both Homes

Children test rules that change from house to house. Choose a short list both homes will use, in the same words. Always ask an adult before going near water, including the bathtub, a pool, a hot tub or a pond. An adult watches whenever the child is in or near water. Non-swimmers and beginners wear a fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket and stay within arm’s reach. No breath-holding contests or “who can stay under longest” games. Get out when an adult says so. Write the rules down, keep a copy in each home and go over them with any new partner, relative or sitter. Our family water safety pledge is an easy way for the child to practice the rules with each parent.

🔍 Check Each Home’s Water Hazards

Each household checks its own home and shares what it found, without judging the other. Look for any pool, and whether it has a four-sided fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate; any hot tub, and whether its safety cover locks; bathtubs, buckets, coolers with melted ice and kiddie pools that should be emptied; and water nearby, such as a neighbor’s pool, a retention pond or a creek. Note where life jackets are kept and whether they still fit. Our room-by-room home water safety checklist, pool fence and gate inspection checklist and layers of protection checklist walk through each layer. If one home has just moved, the new home water safety checklist helps.

👀 Hand-Offs, Visits and Who Is Watching

Busy moments are when supervision slips: a hand-off in the driveway, a holiday gathering where both families are present, or a weekend at a relative’s house where everyone assumes someone else is watching. Each time the child is in or near water, name one adult as the Water Watcher and say it out loud. The Water Watcher is not drinking, not on a phone and close enough to see the child, and trades the role in about 15-minute shifts with a clear hand-off. Never assume the other household, a lifeguard or an older sibling is watching. Tell new partners, grandparents and sitters the child’s swim level and the house rules before they are left in charge. Our Water Watcher card, grandparent water safety checklist and babysitter water safety checklist make the hand-off easier.

📅 Keep Swim Lessons Steady Across Two Schedules

Swim skills grow with steady weekly practice, and a child who misses every other week can stall. Pick a lesson day and time that works in both parenting schedules, put it on a shared calendar and agree who drives each week. Keep one swim bag that travels with the child so the suit, goggles, towel and any life jacket do not get left at the wrong house; our swim bag checklist helps. Ask the swim school whether both parents can be listed for progress updates and schedule reminders, and whether both can be authorized for pickup. Agree in advance how missed classes will be made up; the missed class makeup tracker and authorized pickup plan keep everyone on the same page.

🎄 Holiday Visits and Trips Near Water

Thanksgiving, winter break and long weekends often mean a different house, a hotel or a trip. Before the child leaves, the traveling parent tells the other home where the child is going and what water will be there: a hotel pool, a relative’s pool or hot tub, a lake, a beach or a cruise ship. Share who will be the Water Watcher, whether the child needs a life jacket and the latest swim profile, and agree on any rules that stay the same while traveling. Our holiday gathering water safety checklist, vacation rental pool safety checklist and winter break swim lesson plan cover the details.

🎽 Swim Lessons Give Both Homes a Shared Goal

Swim lessons give co-parents something simple to agree on and track together: the same instructor notes, the same next skill and the same weekly routine. Indoor lessons keep skills strong through the winter, when holiday schedules move children between homes. You can browse swim lessons near you or read our guide to winter swim lessons. Families in New Jersey and Pennsylvania can see our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia, which link to the official location pages. Skills lower risk but never replace close adult supervision.

🆘 Prepare Both Homes for an Emergency

Both homes should be ready for the same emergency. Post the home address, emergency numbers and the child’s key medical information where a sitter or relative can find them. Keep a phone near the pool or hot tub when the child is in the water. If a child is missing, check the water first: the pool, hot tub, bathtub and any nearby water. If a child is in trouble in the water, get them out if you can do so safely, call 911 or have someone call, and follow the dispatcher’s instructions, including sending someone for an AED if one is nearby. Encourage every adult who regularly cares for the child in either home to take a CPR class. Our water emergency action plan and CPR basics for parents help you prepare.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do co-parents keep water safety rules the same in two homes?

Agree on a short list of rules and use the same words in both houses: always ask an adult before going near water, an adult watches whenever the child is in or near water, non-swimmers and beginners wear a fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket and stay within arm’s reach, and no breath-holding games. Write the rules down, keep a copy in each home and review them when a new partner, relative or sitter starts helping.

What should both homes know about a child’s swimming?

Both homes should have the same up-to-date swim profile: the child’s current class or level, which skills they can and cannot do yet, whether they need a life jacket, their instructor’s latest notes and any health or comfort needs the swim school knows about. Update it after each progress report so neither home relies on an old picture of what the child can do.

Who should watch the child around water during exchanges and visits?

Name one adult as the Water Watcher every time the child is in or near water, and say it out loud at hand-offs, holiday gatherings and visits with relatives. A Water Watcher is not drinking, not on a phone and close enough to see the child, and trades the role in about 15-minute shifts with a clear hand-off. Never assume the other household, a lifeguard or an older sibling is watching.

How can we keep swim lessons going when a child splits time between two homes?

Pick a lesson day and time that works in both schedules, put it on a shared calendar and agree who drives each week. Keep one swim bag that travels with the child, ask the swim school whether both parents can receive progress updates and schedule reminders, and agree in advance how missed classes will be made up so practice stays steady.

Does each home need the same water safety equipment?

Each home should have the layers that fit its own water: a four-sided fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate around any pool, a locked safety cover on any hot tub, empty buckets and kiddie pools, and supervision at bath time. The child also needs a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket for any trip near open water, either one in each home or one that travels in the swim bag.

What should we share before a holiday trip near water?

Tell the other parent where the child is going and what water will be there, such as a hotel pool, a relative’s pool or hot tub, a lake, beach or cruise ship. Share who will be the Water Watcher, whether the child needs a life jacket and the latest swim profile, and agree on any rules that should stay the same while traveling.

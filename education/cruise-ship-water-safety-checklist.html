Cruise Ship Water Safety Checklist for Families (Free Printable)

Winter break and spring break are busy cruise seasons for families, and a ship packs pools, slides, hot tubs, balconies and beach days into one trip. Many cruise pools have no lifeguard, and the busiest pool days are often the days everyone is most relaxed. This checklist walks you through the plan from packing to the last shore excursion, with a free one-page printable to keep in your cabin.

Quick Answer: Before you sail, check the cruise line’s pool, swim diaper and kids’ club water rules, and pack properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard approved life jackets for children who cannot swim well. In the first hour on board, find every pool, check whether a lifeguard is on duty, check the cabin life jackets fit your kids, lock the balcony and pick a family meeting spot. At the pool, an adult water watcher stays within arm’s reach of young and weak swimmers, with no phone. Children never climb or lean on railings. On shore days, choose lifeguarded beaches and use life jackets for snorkeling and boats. Our free printable puts it all on one page.

🚢 Why a Cruise Needs Its Own Water Plan

A cruise ship is a floating resort with water everywhere: pools, splash areas, water slides, hot tubs and the ocean on every side. Some cruise lines now station lifeguards at certain pools, but many ship pools have none and rely on swim-at-your-own-risk signs. Ship pools are often deep with no shallow end, the water can slosh as the ship moves, and sea days pack the pool deck. Our full guide to cruise ship water safety for families explains each of these risks. Drowning is quick and usually silent, so the plan below is built around one idea: a named adult is always watching. Our guide to why lifeguards don’t replace parent supervision explains why this matters even when a guard is on duty.

🧳 Before You Sail

Check your cruise line’s rules before you pack. Ask whether each pool has a lifeguard, what the age and height rules are for slides, whether children in diapers or swim diapers may use the pools (many lines do not allow it outside a baby splash area) and how the kids’ club handles water play. For children who cannot swim well, pack a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard approved life jacket rather than relying on loaners or inflatable toys; our life jacket sizing guide helps with fit, and our floaties and puddle jumpers checklist explains why arm floaties are not safety devices. A few weeks of lessons before the trip help too; our winter break swim lesson plan and swim lessons while traveling guide show how to keep skills going. Use our water competency skills checklist to write down what each child can really do, and pack sun protection with our sun-safe swim day checklist.

🕐 The First Hour on Board

Before anyone swims, walk the ship together. Find every pool, splash area, slide and hot tub, note how deep each pool is and check whether a lifeguard is posted. In your cabin, look at the life jackets and check that there are sizes that fit your children; ask your cabin steward or guest services if not. Find the ship’s emergency number on the safety card or phone in your cabin and save it on the printable. Attend the safety drill with the whole family so children know their muster station. Lock the balcony door and pick a family meeting spot that is not on a pool deck, so a child who gets separated knows where to go.

🏊 At the Pool Deck

Treat every cruise pool as if no lifeguard is there. Name one adult as the water watcher, with no phone, book or drink in hand, and switch every 15 to 30 minutes so nobody’s attention fades; our water watcher card makes the hand-off clear, and our multi-child supervision plan helps when you have several swimmers. Young children and weak swimmers stay within arm’s reach, and non-swimmers wear their life jacket. Crowded pools make it hard to see a child in trouble, so learn the warning signs of drowning. Follow posted rules for slides and splash zones with our water slide safety checklist and splash pad checklist. Hot tubs are often off-limits to young children; our hot tub safety checklist explains why. If your child joins the kids’ club, ask how they supervise any water play. Our when to get kids out of the water checklist helps with cold, tired or overexcited swimmers.

🛑 Railings, Balconies and Open Decks

Railings are never for climbing, sitting, leaning or photos. Never lift a child onto or over a rail, and hold small children’s hands on open decks. On the balcony, keep the door locked when an adult is not out there, and move chairs, tables and luggage away from the railing so they cannot be used as a step. Remind older children that the rules stay the same when friends are around. These simple rules prevent the rare but serious falls that families worry about most.

🏝️ Shore Excursions and Beaches

Port days bring open water, and open water is different from a pool. Choose beaches with lifeguards when you can, swim near the guard stand and learn the warning flags; our beach day checklist and rip current checklist cover what to look for. On snorkel trips, boats and water toys, children wear properly fitted life jackets, and you ask the tour operator about life jackets in kids’ sizes before you book; see our guide to kids’ snorkeling safety and our open water safety checklist. Keep the same water watcher habit on shore that you use on the ship.

🆘 If Something Goes Wrong

If a child is missing, check the water first. Shout for help, alert the nearest crew member right away and call the ship’s emergency number. If a child is in trouble in the water, use reach, throw, don’t go so you do not become a second victim, and get crew help immediately. Taking a CPR class before your trip gives you the skills to help while waiting; our CPR basics for parents guide and water emergency action plan help you prepare. Any child who had trouble in the water should be checked by the ship’s medical team, even if they seem fine.

🏊 Build Swim Skills Before You Go

Lessons in the weeks before a cruise help children feel at home in the water and practice getting back to the edge. For families in Pennsylvania and New Jersey, our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia point you to the official location pages, where you can ask about classes before your trip. Frequently Asked Questions

Do cruise ship pools have lifeguards?

It depends on the cruise line and the ship. Some lines station lifeguards at certain pools, while many ship pools have none and post swim-at-your-own-risk signs. Check each pool when you board, and plan to watch your own children closely either way.

Should my child wear a life jacket in a cruise ship pool?

If your child is a non-swimmer or a weak swimmer, a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard approved life jacket is a smart choice around any pool without a lifeguard. It does not replace an adult watching within arm’s reach. Puddle jumpers and inflatable floaties are not a substitute.

Are there life jackets for kids in our cruise cabin?

Ships carry life jackets for every passenger, including children and infants. When you reach your cabin, check that jackets in the right sizes for your children are there, and ask your cabin steward or guest services if they are not.

Can babies and toddlers in swim diapers use cruise ship pools?

Many cruise lines do not allow children in diapers or swim diapers in the main pools and hot tubs, and some ships have a separate baby splash area instead. Check your cruise line’s policy before you pack, and never leave a baby or toddler alone near any water.

How do I keep my child safe on a cruise ship balcony?

Keep the balcony door locked when you are not out there with your child, keep chairs and tables away from the railing so they cannot be used to climb, and never let children sit on, lean over or climb a railing anywhere on the ship.

What should we do if a child goes missing near the water on a cruise?

Check the water first. Shout for help and alert the nearest crew member right away; use the ship’s emergency number from the safety card in your cabin. Agree on a family meeting spot on day one so children know where to go if they get separated.

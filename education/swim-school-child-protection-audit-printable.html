Swim School Child Protection Policy Audit

Competitive clubs registered with USA Swimming must follow a written abuse-prevention policy. Most learn-to-swim schools — where nearly every child has a first lesson — are not covered by it and write their own rules, or none. This audit borrows that policy’s structure and turns it into five questions you can ask on a tour, with what a strong answer sounds like next to what a weak one sounds like.

📝 Start Here — How to Use This Audit

  • Ask for the written policy first. Every question below is really one question: is this written down, and can I read it? A school with a document hands it over. A school without one answers with reassurance.
  • These questions are normal. Good programs are asked them and are glad to be. If asking feels rude, that feeling is worth noticing — it is exactly the discomfort that keeps most parents from ever asking.
  • Fill in two schools. One completed audit tells you little. Two side by side show you which school has thought about this and which is improvising an answer at the front desk.
  • A gap is information, not a verdict. A small independent school may have no formal document and still supervise beautifully. Score the gaps, weigh them against the alternatives near you, and ask again in six months.
  • Policy is the floor, not the ceiling. Written rules do not protect a child on their own. Use the culture check at the end — it is the part a policy cannot fake.

👁️ 1. Adults Alone With Children

The single most protective rule in child safeguarding: no unobservable one-on-one contact

AskA strong answer sounds likeA weak answer sounds likeSchool ASchool B
“What is your written rule on a staff member being alone with a child?”One-on-one contact is not permitted in any space that cannot be observed, and the rule is in the handbook. Private lessons happen in open water, in sight of the deck and the viewing area“That would never happen here” — a statement about people rather than about a rule____________
“Can parents watch every lesson, start to finish?”Yes, from a viewing area with a clear line of sight to the whole pool, with no session parents are asked to sit outObservation is discouraged, limited to certain weeks, or the viewing glass looks away from part of the water____________
“Who else is on deck during a private lesson?”A named second adult — a deck supervisor, another instructor, or a lifeguard — is present and can see the lesson“Just the instructor”, especially for an early-morning or after-hours slot____________
“What happens if a child needs the bathroom mid-lesson?”A specific answer: the parent is called, or two staff go, or the child is walked to the door and the parent takes it from thereThe instructor takes the child, alone, and no one has thought about it before you asked____________
Why this row matters most. Nearly all abuse in youth programs requires time alone with a child in a place no one else can see. A program that eliminates unobservable one-on-one contact has removed the opportunity, which is more protective than any amount of screening applied afterwards.

🚪 2. Locker Rooms, Changing Areas and Bathrooms

The one place at a swim school where children are undressed — and the policy most often missing

AskA strong answer sounds likeA weak answer sounds likeSchool ASchool B
“Is there a written locker room and changing area policy?”Yes, and it is posted or in the parent handbook. It says who may be in there and under what conditionsThere is no policy because “it is just a changing room”____________
“May a staff member be alone in a changing area with a child?”No — never one adult and one child. If help is needed, either the parent does it or two adults are present“Only if the parent asks us to”, with no second adult and nothing in writing____________
“Who helps a small child change if I am not there?”A named role, with written parent consent on file, and never behind a closed door alone“Whoever is free”____________
“Are phones allowed in the changing areas?”No devices with cameras, for staff or for other parents, and signage says soNo rule at all, or a rule for staff but not for anyone else____________
Watch for the shrug. Of all five sections, this is the one where a school with no written policy is most likely to say the question has never come up. That answer is itself the finding — note it and move on rather than pressing.

📱 3. Messages, Social Media and Photographs

Private contact between an adult and a child is where grooming usually starts, and it leaves a record

AskA strong answer sounds likeA weak answer sounds likeSchool ASchool B
“May instructors text or message a child directly?”No. Adult-to-child contact routes through a parent or a monitored school account, and a parent is copied on anything sent“We trust our staff”, or private messaging is allowed for older kids____________
“May staff connect with students on personal social accounts?”No, and the rule is in the employment handbook rather than left to judgementDiscouraged but not prohibited, or handled case by case____________
“Who may photograph or film in the pool area, and where do images go?”Only designated staff, only with signed consent, stored on school accounts and never on a personal phone. Other parents are asked not to film the waterAnyone may film, or staff use personal phones for “progress videos”____________
“Can I decline photography and still enroll?”Yes, opting out is a checkbox and changes nothing about the lessonsConsent is bundled into the enrollment agreement with no way to decline____________
Progress video is worth its own question. Filmed feedback can genuinely help a swimmer, and plenty of good schools use it. The safeguard is not banning the camera — it is that images live on school accounts, consent was asked for separately, and no image is ever on an instructor’s personal phone.

🔎 4. Screening and Training

Who gets checked, how recently, and what they were actually taught to recognise

AskA strong answer sounds likeA weak answer sounds likeSchool ASchool B
“Who gets a background check, and how often is it repeated?”Every adult with access to children — instructors, deck staff, front desk, contractors — through a named third-party vendor, re-run on a stated schedule. Record the interval they give you: ______“All our instructors” with no vendor named, no interval, and no mention of anyone but instructors____________
“Does the check include the sex offender registry?”Yes, plus a criminal search beyond the single county the school sits in“I believe so”____________
“What abuse-prevention training do staff complete, and how often?”A named program — U.S. Center for SafeSport, Praesidium, MinistrySafe or an internal curriculum — repeated on a schedule, covering grooming behaviour and reporting duties“It is covered in onboarding”, once, with no name attached____________
“What is checked before a new instructor is alone on deck?”References contacted, a supervised probation period, and a specific number of co-taught lessonsStart date and a polo shirt____________
Ask for the date, not the fact. A background check describes one moment in the past. “We background check everyone” and “every employee was re-checked within the last two years” are very different sentences. This is the same discipline the credential decoder applies to certifications — the date is the evidence.

📢 5. How a Concern Actually Travels

A reporting process only exists if a parent can describe it before they need it

AskA strong answer sounds likeA weak answer sounds likeSchool ASchool B
“If I had a concern about a staff member, who exactly would I tell?”A named person and a named backup, so a concern about one of them does not have to go through the other“Just tell us”, or a single owner who is also the only route____________
“What happens in the first 24 hours after I report?”A described sequence with a timeline, including when the staff member is stood down and when outside authorities are contacted“We would look into it”____________
“Do your staff know they are mandatory reporters?”Yes, it is in the handbook and covered in training, with the state duty namedUncertainty about what the term means____________
“Can I report without my child being identified to the staff member?”Yes, with a stated confidentiality practice and a no-retaliation commitmentNo, or the question is treated as odd____________
You never have to go through the school. Whatever any program’s internal process says, you can always report a specific concern directly to local law enforcement or your state child protective services. If the program is connected to organised sport, the U.S. Center for SafeSport takes reports as well. The contacts are at the bottom of this sheet.

⭐ The Culture Check — What a Policy Cannot Tell You

Do this part with your eyes while you are on the tour
Watch forWhat it tells youSchool ASchool B
Is any adult ever out of sight of another adult?Open sightlines are a design decision. A deck where nobody can be hidden is behaviourally safer than one where a policy says they should not be____________
Are parents in the viewing area, or pushed to a lobby?Free parental observation is a safeguard no written rule can replicate____________
Ask a front-desk or junior staff member how they would raise a worry about a colleagueComfort or hesitation in that answer is more informative than the handbook____________
How long have the instructors been there?Programs that treat staff well generally treat children well; constant churn erodes every policy above____________
How did they react to being asked all of this?Interest and specifics are a good sign. Defensiveness is data____________
Passing this audit does not guarantee a child is safe. It is a floor. Some of the most serious failures in youth sport happened at programs with policies on paper, because nobody enforced them. The culture rows above are where a written policy either becomes real or does not.

Ask the Questions. Then Get in the Water.

This audit takes ten minutes and it helps you choose well — but the lessons are the thing that protects your child. Participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a large reduction in drowning risk for young children, and drowning remains a leading cause of death for children ages 1–4.

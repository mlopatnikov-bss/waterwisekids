Parent & Me Swim Class Card: Holds, Cues and What to Do in the Water

In a parent-and-child swim class you’re not watching from a bench. You’re in the water, holding your baby, and doing most of the teaching yourself while the instructor coaches you. Here’s what to do: how to get in safely, the three holds, the cue routine that means your baby never gets surprise water, how to read your baby’s signals, and what a baby class can and can’t do. There’s also a free printable card with an eight-class log.

Quick Summary: In a parent-and-me class, the instructor teaches you and you teach your baby. Before class: finish the feed a while before you leave, put on a swim diaper that meets your pool’s rules (the CDC says they are not leak-proof), and pack your own quick change. Getting in: never hand a baby down from the deck. Get in first, then take your baby. In the water: learn three holds (face-to-face, back float support, tummy support). Use the same verbal cue before any water on the face, and only put your baby underwater when the instructor leads it. Watch your baby: some crying in the first classes is normal. Shivering, blue lips or floppiness means get out and warm up. Remember what a baby class is for: the American Academy of Pediatrics says there is no evidence that lessons under age one reduce drowning. It recommends starting lessons after the first birthday and keeping touch supervision in place for babies. A baby class builds comfort and teaches you how to handle your baby in the water. Formal lessons come next.

🤲 Why your hands matter more than anything else in a baby class

New parents often arrive at their first baby swim class expecting to hand their child to an instructor. In a parent-and-child class that almost never happens. The instructor stands in the middle, shows each activity with a doll or their own arms, and explains what it’s for. Then you do it with your baby. You’re the one holding them, reading their face and deciding whether to try again or have a cuddle.

Babies learn best from the adults they trust, so that setup works. It also means the class goes as well as you’re prepared for it. A parent who knows the holds, uses the same cue every time and can tell “this is new” from “this is too much” usually has a baby who looks forward to the water by week four. A parent who is guessing often has a baby who cries at the pool entrance. Our longer guide to parent-and-me swim lessons explains how these classes are structured. This page, and the printable card, turn that into a checklist you can use on the day.

👜 Before class: feeding, diapers and your own change

Time the feed. A hungry baby won’t settle, and a baby who has just had a full feed may bring some of it back when you start bobbing. Most families finish a feed a while before leaving home and bring a bottle or snack for afterwards, when a tired baby is often ready to feed and sleep.

Get the swim diaper right. Many pools ask for a reusable swim diaper, sometimes over a disposable one. Check your program’s rule before the first class. It’s worth knowing what swim diapers can’t do: the CDC notes that swim diapers and swim pants are not leak-proof. They may hold in solid stool for a while, but they don’t stop diarrhea germs getting into the water. So the CDC’s advice is to check them about every hour, change them in a changing area rather than by the pool, wash your hands afterwards, and keep babies with diarrhea out of the water altogether. Our swim diaper guide covers types and fit.

Pack for two people getting changed. Pack the baby’s hooded towel, a clean diaper and warm clothes where you can reach them first. Pack something you can pull on yourself with one hand. After class, the baby gets dried and dressed first while you’re still in your wet swimsuit. Take off earrings and necklaces and tie your hair back; small hands grab everything. Aim to arrive with ten quiet minutes to spare. Babies pick up on how you feel, and a rushed parent is hard to relax with.

🪜 Getting in and out of the pool safely

The rule that matters most is simple: never hand a baby down from the deck to someone in the water, and never climb down the steps with a baby held out in front of you. Get in first while the instructor or another adult holds your baby on the deck, then take your baby once you’re standing steady. If the pool has a sloped “zero-entry” end, you can walk in together.

On steps, hold the rail with one hand and keep your baby close to your chest with the other arm. Go one step at a time and stop partway down. Even a warm teaching pool feels cooler than a baby’s skin, and a few seconds to adjust at waist height makes the rest of the way in calmer. To get out, reverse it: hand your baby to someone on the deck, climb out, then take your baby back and wrap them in the hooded towel straight away. Walk, don’t hurry. Wet tiles and a wriggly baby in your arms are a bad combination.

🤍 The three holds you’ll use

Most parent-and-child programs build on three positions. Your instructor’s version comes first, since programs teach them slightly differently. It’s worth asking them to check your hands in the first class.

  • Face-to-face hold. Your baby faces you with your hands under their armpits and your thumbs resting on the front of their chest, shoulders in the water. This is the home base: you can see their face, they can see yours, and it’s where most songs, bobbing and the cue routine happen. Keep your baby close, and avoid lifting them in and out of the water quickly.
  • Back float support. Your baby lies on their back with the back of their head resting on your shoulder or in your palm, your other hand under the lower back, ears at the water line. Babies need reasonable head control for this one. Many babies dislike being on their back at first. If your baby arches, stiffens or panics, bring them upright into a cuddle and try again in a later class. Don’t push through it.
  • Tummy (swimming) support. Your baby faces down with one of your hands under the chest and the other under the hips. Walk slowly backwards so they move towards you, kicking and reaching. Keep the chin up and the face clear of the water unless the instructor is running a cued activity. Watch for tiring: legs that stop kicking or a head that starts to drop mean it’s time to switch back to face-to-face.

Whatever the hold, one rule doesn’t change: your hands stay on your baby. The American Academy of Pediatrics calls this touch supervision, meaning an adult who can swim stays within arm’s reach of an infant or toddler in or near water. In a class your hands are closer than that.

🗣️ The cue routine: no surprise water

Babies handle water on the face much better when they know it’s coming. That’s why nearly every program teaches parents a verbal cue: often the baby’s name, then “Ready… go”, then the water. The words matter less than using them the same way every single time. A consistent cue means your baby has a moment to prepare. Surprise water teaches a baby to be wary of the water, and that can take weeks to undo.

The usual progression starts well away from the face: a little water poured over the back of the head, then the crown, then the forehead, and only later the face. Some babies go through it in a class or two. Others take a month or more, and both are normal. If your program includes brief underwater moments, they should only ever happen when the instructor leads them, with a settled baby, after the cue. If your baby coughs or splutters, lift them up, hold them upright against your chest and give them time before anything else. Never repeat an underwater activity with a baby who is upset.

You can keep the cue going at bath time: say it before pouring water over your baby’s head, so the routine stays familiar between classes. Stay within arm’s reach the whole time. Our bath time safety guide covers the rest.

👀 Reading your baby: new, too much, or stop

Crying is common in the first two or three classes. The pool is loud and echoey, the water feels different, being weightless is strange, and there are other babies and splashing. Most of that crying means “this is new”, not “I’m frightened”. It’s useful to sort your baby’s signals into three groups:

  • Going well: a relaxed body, looking around, kicking, reaching for toys or for you, smiling or babbling.
  • Too much right now: turning away, a stiff body, clinging, whimpering. Bring your baby into a close face-to-face cuddle, move away from the splashing, talk quietly, and skip the next activity if you need to. Staying calm and in the water often works better than getting out straight away.
  • Take a break: crying that doesn’t settle after a couple of minutes of calm cuddling. Step out to the side, wrap your baby up, and go back in if they settle. A break is not the same as quitting, and a good instructor will support it.

Some signs mean the class is over for today. Take your baby out and warm them up if they’re shivering, their lips look blue-tinged, they seem unusually floppy or quiet, or they’ve coughed a lot after swallowing water. Call your pediatrician or get urgent care if breathing seems hard, fast or noisy afterwards, or your baby is unusually sleepy. Call 911 for any baby who stops breathing or needed rescue breaths. Our after-a-water-scare symptom watch log explains what to watch for in the hours afterwards.

🤒 When to skip a class

Keep your baby home with a fever, diarrhea or vomiting, an ear infection, or a rash that could be contagious, and check your program’s rules on when they can come back. Diarrhea matters most, because it can spread illness to every other baby in the pool. The CDC advises that anyone with diarrhea stays out of the water, swim diaper or not. A mild runny nose in a baby who is otherwise bright and feeding well is more of a judgement call. Our sick-day decision guide walks through the common cases. It’s also worth checking with your pediatrician before starting if your baby was born early, has a heart or breathing condition, or has had repeated ear infections.

🛡️ What a baby swim class does, and what it doesn’t

Parents should hear this plainly. In its 2026 updated policy statement on drowning prevention, the American Academy of Pediatrics says there is no evidence that swim lessons for babies under one year old reduce drowning, and recommends starting swim lessons after a child’s first birthday. Babies that young can’t yet coordinate the movements needed to keep themselves safe in the water.

That doesn’t make a baby class a waste of time. It’s time in the water together. Your baby gets used to the water early, so the first “real” lesson at one or two isn’t a shock. And you come out knowing how to hold, support and handle a baby in a pool, which you’ll use every time you’re in the water together for years. What a baby class doesn’t do is make a baby water-safe. That job still belongs to supervision and barriers: touch supervision whenever your baby is in or near water, an empty bath and empty buckets, a shut bathroom door and a latched pool gate. Our infant water safety checklist lists the home rules.

🛁 Practising between classes

One class a week goes further if the ideas carry into ordinary life. Bath time is the obvious place: use your cue before pouring water over your baby’s head, let them kick against your hand, and sing the songs from class. Keep bath time about play and comfort. Never practise putting your baby’s face underwater at home, and never step away from a baby in the bath, even for a moment.

The printable card’s eight-class log helps here. After each class, jot down your baby’s mood going in, the favourite activity, the hard moment, and one tip from the instructor. The last column is “try at bath time”: one small thing to repeat that week. After a few weeks you’ll see whether the hard moments are shrinking, and you’ll have something specific to ask the instructor about if they’re not.

🏊 What comes after the baby class

Around the first birthday, ask your program what the next step is. Many move families into toddler classes where a parent is still in the water. Children then move on to classes where they begin to work with the instructor on their own, usually somewhere between two and four depending on the child. This is the stage the AAP describes as a layer of protection against drowning. Children start learning to float on their back, turn around, and get back to the wall or steps.

When you compare programs, look for a warm teaching pool, small class sizes, instructors who explain what they’re doing and why, a clear policy on illness and swim diapers, and a clear path from baby classes to independent swimming. Frequently Asked Questions

What do parents do in a parent and me swim class?

You are in the water holding your baby for the whole class. The instructor demonstrates each activity and explains its purpose, and you carry it out with your child: holding them in the right position, using a consistent verbal cue before any water on the face, singing, supporting floats and kicks, and reading your baby’s signals. In effect the instructor teaches you and you teach your baby.

What are the main holds in a baby swim class?

Most programs build on three holds. The face-to-face hold, with your hands under your baby’s armpits and your thumbs on the chest, is the base for songs and bobbing. The back float support has your baby’s head resting on your shoulder or hand and your other hand under the lower back, with the ears at the water line. The tummy or swimming support has one hand under the chest and one under the hips while you walk slowly backwards. Always follow the version your instructor teaches.

How do I get into the pool safely with my baby?

Never hand a baby down from the deck to someone in the water. Get in first using the steps while an instructor or another adult holds your baby, then take your baby once you are standing steady. If the pool has a sloped zero-entry end, you can walk in together. On steps, hold the rail with one hand and keep your baby against your chest with the other arm.

Should my baby go underwater in a parent and child swim class?

Only if your program includes it, only when the instructor leads it, and only with a settled baby after a consistent verbal cue. Many programs start with pouring water over the back of the head and progress slowly toward the face. Never force it, never repeat it with an upset baby, and if your baby coughs or splutters, hold them upright against your chest and give them time.

Is it normal for my baby to cry in swim class?

Yes, especially in the first two or three classes. The pool is loud, the water feels different and weightlessness is new, so most early crying reflects novelty rather than fear. Hold your baby close in a face-to-face cuddle, move away from splashing and talk quietly. If crying does not settle after a couple of minutes, take a short break at the side and go back in if your baby calms.

Do infant swim lessons prevent drowning?

No. The American Academy of Pediatrics says there is no evidence that swim lessons for babies under one year old reduce drowning, and recommends starting swim lessons after a child’s first birthday. Baby classes build comfort in the water and teach parents safe handling, but infants still need touch supervision from an adult within arm’s reach whenever they are in or near water.

What should my baby wear to a swim class?

A swim diaper that meets your pool’s rules, which is often a reusable swim diaper, sometimes over a disposable one. The CDC notes that swim diapers are not leak-proof, so check them about every hour, change them in a changing area away from the poolside, and keep your baby out of the water if they have diarrhea. Bring a hooded towel for afterwards.

When should my baby skip swim class?

Keep your baby home with a fever, diarrhea or vomiting, an ear infection, or a rash that could be contagious, and follow your program’s rules on returning. Check with your pediatrician before starting classes if your baby was born early, has a heart or breathing condition, or has had repeated ear infections.

This guide is general family information, not medical advice. Follow your instructor and your swim program’s rules, and talk to your pediatrician about any health concern.

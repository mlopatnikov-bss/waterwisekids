Questions to Ask Your Child's Swim Instructor: A Free Printable Checklist

Most parents watch swim lessons from the bleachers for months without asking a single question — and then quietly wonder whether their child is actually progressing. Two minutes at the edge of the pool answers that. This free printable gives you the exact questions to ask about progress, level advancement, survival skills, and what to practice at home.

💬 Why should I talk to my child's swim instructor?

Because swim lessons are the one activity parents watch every week and understand the least — and a two-minute conversation converts twenty weeks of guessing into a clear picture of where your child actually stands. From the bleachers, a good lesson often looks like play. Children bob, splash, chase toys, and jump off the wall, and a parent watching without context can easily conclude that not much is happening. Meanwhile the instructor is tracking breath control, body position, and comfort — the things that have to come before a stroke ever looks like a stroke.

That gap between what a lesson looks like and what a lesson is doing is the number one reason families quietly stop coming. Parents rarely quit because a program is bad; they quit because they cannot see progress and no one has explained what progress should look like. Asking is the fix, and it costs nothing. Our guide to measuring your child's real swim progress covers what to watch for between conversations.

🗓️ When is the best time to ask?

Ask at the end of class, not the beginning — and if you need more than a minute or two, ask the front desk to schedule a short progress check-in. Before a lesson, the instructor is setting up equipment, greeting children, and — most importantly — watching the water. Pulling their attention away from the pool at that moment is exactly what you do not want. At the end of class, they have just spent thirty minutes with your child and can speak specifically to what happened that day.

Two more things make the conversation land. Keep it to two or three questions, so each gets a real answer instead of a rushed one. And lead with curiosity rather than complaint — “help me understand what you're working on” opens a door that “why isn't she moving up yet?” slams shut. Then write the answer down, because you will not remember it by the drive home.

📈 What questions should I ask about progress?

Ask what one skill your child is working on right now, what mastering it looks like in observable terms, what has improved since last month, and what is holding your child back most. The first question is the most revealing one on the whole list. A good instructor can name the current focus skill in a single sentence. If the answer is vague — “we're just building confidence” — follow up: confidence doing what, exactly?

Then ask what mastery looks like. You want the observable version: how many seconds of independent back float, how many feet swum unassisted, how many times in a row, how consistently across different days. That specificity is what lets you see progress yourself the following week. And ask what is holding your child back most — the honest answer is almost always breath control, fear, or body position, and each of those has a completely different fix. Our guide to the 4 C's of progress explains how good programs sequence those building blocks.

🪜 What should I ask about moving up a level?

Ask for the written advancement criteria, ask honestly how close your child is in weeks or months, and ask whether the school advances on mastery or on session length. Level advancement is where most parent frustration lives, and most of it comes from a standard that was never explained. Ask what exactly your child must demonstrate to move up, and ask to see it written down if the school has criteria on paper. Then ask which items your child can already do and which remain.

The mastery-versus-session question matters more than parents realize. A mastery-based program moves a child when the skill is genuinely there; a session-based program moves whole classes when the calendar turns over. Neither is automatically wrong, but they produce very different timelines and very different levels of retained skill — our comparison of mastery-based versus session advancement breaks down the trade-offs. It is also entirely fair to ask whether a different class time, level, or instructor would serve your child better. Good schools welcome that question. If your child has been stuck for a while, read what to do when your child hits a swim plateau.

🛟 What safety questions matter most?

Ask what would happen if your child fell into the water fully clothed today — then ask whether your child can roll over, float, and breathe unassisted, and get back to the wall alone. This is the single most useful question a swim parent can ask, and it reframes the entire conversation away from strokes and toward survival. Floating on the back, recovering a breath, and returning to the edge are not advanced skills; they are the skills that decide outcomes in the seconds after an unexpected fall.

Those abilities — entering the water, turning around, floating, breathing, swimming a distance, and exiting — are what the American Red Cross describes as water competency. Ask plainly what your child cannot yet do safely, because that answer tells you exactly where your supervision still has to be constant. And keep the frame right: no lesson makes a child drown-proof. Swim skill is one layer of protection, alongside barriers, life jackets, and eyes-on supervision — our water competency skills checklist lays out the full picture.

🏡 What should we practice between lessons?

Ask for one thing to practice — and just as importantly, ask what to avoid, because well-meaning parent coaching can undo an instructor's work. Most instructors will offer something small and repeatable: pouring water over the face in the bathtub, blowing bubbles, practicing a supported back float, or simply getting comfortable putting ears in the water. One specific thing done often beats a long list done once.

The “what should I avoid” half of that question is the one parents skip. Breath-holding games and inflatable floaties are the usual culprits — floaties in particular train a vertical body position that instructors then have to untrain. Ask for the instructor's exact script for when your child is scared or refuses, and use those same words at home so the message stays consistent. Finally, ask how much unstructured open swim time helps; relaxed pool time often builds comfort faster than extra drilling ever will. Our swimming progress tracker guide shows how to log all of it week to week.

🚩 What if the answers are vague?

A vague answer is itself an answer — if an instructor cannot name the current focus skill, describe mastery, or tell you what your child could do in an emergency, that is information about the program, not just the person. Give the benefit of the doubt first: instructors are often mid-shift, managing a deck full of children, and a rushed reply is not the same as an uninformed one. Ask again at a calmer moment, or request a scheduled check-in.

But if you consistently get nothing concrete — no criteria, no timeline, no honest account of what your child cannot yet do — then evaluate the program rather than the instructor. Ratios, instructor training and turnover, and whether progress is assessed at all are the things that make specific answers possible in the first place. Frequently Asked Questions

What should I ask my child's swim instructor?

Start with four questions. First, what one skill are you working on with my child right now? Second, what does mastering that skill look like, described in observable terms like seconds floating or feet swum unassisted? Third, what exactly does my child need to demonstrate to move up a level? Fourth, if my child fell into the water fully clothed today, what would happen? Those four cover progress, standards, advancement, and safety. Keep the conversation to two or three questions at a time so the instructor can answer them properly, and write the answers down before you leave the pool deck.

When is the best time to talk to a swim instructor?

Ask at the end of class rather than the start. Before a lesson the instructor is setting up, greeting children, and watching the water, and a conversation then pulls attention away from supervision. At the end of class the instructor has just watched your child swim and can speak to what happened that day. If you need more than a minute or two, ask the front desk to schedule a short progress check-in. Most quality swim schools offer these and will not treat the request as a complaint.

Why is my child not moving up a swim level?

Usually because a specific skill has not yet been mastered to the school's standard, and that standard is rarely explained to parents. Ask for the written advancement criteria for the next level, then ask which of those items your child can already do and which remain. Also ask whether the school advances on mastery or on session length, because those two models produce very different timelines. A plateau is normal, especially around breath control, back floating, and independent breathing, and comfort in the water often improves for weeks before a level changes.

How do I know if my child can actually save themselves in the water?

Ask the instructor directly what would happen if your child fell into the water unexpectedly and fully clothed. Then ask three specific follow-ups: can my child roll over, float, and breathe without help; can my child get back to the wall or edge on their own; and what can my child not yet do safely. Those skills, along with entering the water and turning around, are what the American Red Cross describes as water competency. No lesson makes a child drown-proof, so swim skill belongs alongside barriers, life jackets, and constant eyes-on supervision.

What should we practice between swim lessons at home?

Ask the instructor for one specific thing you can practice, and just as importantly, ask what you should avoid. Parent coaching can unintentionally undo an instructor's work, particularly around breath-holding games and inflatable floaties that teach a vertical body position. Many instructors will suggest simple bathtub or shallow-water practice like pouring water over the face, blowing bubbles, or practicing a back float with support. Ask how much unstructured open swim time helps too, since relaxed pool time often builds comfort faster than extra drilling.

Indoor Pool Safety Checklist

A roof does not make a pool safe — it changes the hazards. Indoor pools bring chloramine-heavy air, slick decks, echo that hides the quiet signs of drowning, and very often no lifeguard. Run this two-minute walkthrough at any hotel, gym, apartment, or community indoor pool before your child gets in.

🔎 The Two-Minute Arrival Walkthrough

  • Can you clearly see the bottom at the deepest point? If the water is cloudy, do not swim — a swimmer on the bottom would be invisible.
  • Read the depth markings and find where the floor drops off; small indoor pools often slope faster than they look.
  • Note the no-diving areas. Most indoor pools are too shallow for diving anywhere.
  • Locate the rescue ring or reaching pole, the first aid kit, and the nearest phone before anyone gets in.
  • Is a lifeguard on duty? If not, an adult must take the Water Watcher role for the whole swim.

🌬️ Indoor Air & Hygiene

  • Does the air smell clean? A strong "chlorine" smell means chloramines are building up — a ventilation or chemistry problem, not too much chlorine.
  • Everyone showers before getting in — it removes the sweat and body oils that create chloramines.
  • Bathroom breaks on a schedule; nobody pees in the pool. Swim diapers for babies and toddlers, changed in the changing room, never on deck.
  • Asthma or reactive airways? Keep the rescue inhaler in the bag on deck, not in a locker, and tell the instructor before the lesson.
  • Stinging eyes, coughing, or a scratchy throat? Take a break in fresh air and rethink the facility if it happens every visit.

🚷 Deck, Drains & Physical Hazards

  • Walking feet only — wet indoor tile is the slipperiest surface most kids meet all year, with a hard edge a few feet away.
  • Drain and suction covers intact and securely fastened on the floor and walls; a loose or missing cover is an entrapment hazard — do not swim.
  • Long hair tied back or capped and no loose jewelry near drains or grates.
  • Gate or door to the pool room self-closes and latches so a young child cannot wander in alone.
  • Clear the walkways — bags, toys, and towels on a wet deck are trip hazards.

👀 Your Supervision Plan

  • One named Water Watcher at a time — phone away, eyes on the water, hand the role off out loud. Twenty-minute shifts.
  • Weak and young swimmers stay within arm's reach — close enough to touch, every second.
  • Watch for silence, not splashing. A drowning child goes vertical, head back, arms pressing down — no shouting, no waving.
  • Echo hides distress. Hard walls turn happy noise into a wall of sound — you will see trouble before you hear it.
  • Coast Guard–approved life jackets only for non-swimmers — never water wings, floaties, or puddle jumpers.

🚨 If Something Goes Wrong

  • Reach or throw — don't go. Use the pole or ring from the deck; a panicking swimmer can pull an untrained rescuer under.
  • Call 911 and start CPR. Have one adult call while another begins rescue breaths and compressions.
  • Missing child? Check the water first — every second counts, and the pool is the most dangerous place they could be.

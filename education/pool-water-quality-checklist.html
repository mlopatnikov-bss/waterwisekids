Is This Pool Safe to Swim In? A Free Printable Water Quality Checklist

Parents are told to watch the water. Almost nobody is told how to read it. Clear is not the same as clean, a strong chlorine smell means the opposite of what most people assume, and the pool that closes for an hour is usually the one being run properly. This free printable turns all of it into a sixty-second check you can run at any pool.

🔍 How can I tell if pool water is safe just by looking?

Stand at the deep end and find the main drain on the bottom — if you cannot see it clearly, do not let anyone swim. This one test does double duty, and that is why it belongs first. Cloudy water tells you the disinfectant or the filtration is losing ground, which is a germ problem. It also means a child face-down on the bottom would be invisible from the deck, which is a drowning problem. Lifeguards are trained to close a pool they cannot see the bottom of, and a parent can apply exactly the same standard without any equipment at all.

Four more things take about thirty seconds together. Check that the pump is running and the skimmers are actually pulling water, because chemistry means very little in water that is not circulating. Run a hand along the tile at the waterline — smooth is fine, slimy is biofilm, and biofilm shelters germs from chlorine. Look for film, foam, or debris sitting undisturbed on the surface, which tells you how recently anyone paid attention. And look at the water colour itself: milky, green, or tinted all mean the same thing, which is that you are not swimming today. Our guide to what cloudy pool water is really signalling goes deeper on why clarity is the single most useful thing a parent can judge. If the pool is your own, the shelf the chemicals came off deserves the same thirty seconds — the pool chemical storage and handling audit is the companion pass.

👎 Does a strong chlorine smell mean the pool is clean?

No — it almost always means the opposite, and this is the most useful thing most parents have never been told. Fresh chlorine dissolved in water is close to odourless. The sharp, eye-watering smell people call “pool smell” is chloramines: compounds that form when chlorine reacts with the sweat, urine, body oils, and cosmetics that swimmers carry in with them. So the smell is not evidence of vigorous disinfection. It is evidence of a heavy contamination load, and often of a chlorine level being consumed faster than staff are replacing it.

Indoors this matters more. Chloramine gas is heavier than air and collects in a layer just above the water surface — precisely where a child's face is. If you walk into an indoor pool enclosure and your eyes sting within a minute, that is a ventilation failure, not a strong pool. The fix on the swimmer's side is almost comically simple: a one-minute shower before getting in removes most of the sweat and oils that make chloramines possible. Our pool chemistry basics for parents explains how free chlorine, combined chlorine, and pH fit together without requiring a certification to follow.

🧪 What chlorine and pH levels should a pool have?

The CDC recommends at least 1 part per million of free chlorine in pools, at least 3 ppm in hot tubs, and a pH between 7.0 and 7.8. Free chlorine is the number that matters, because it is the fraction still available to kill germs — total chlorine includes the spent chloramines that are causing the smell. Hot tubs need triple the pool target for a straightforward reason: heat and aeration strip disinfectant out of the water far faster, which is why hot tubs punch so far above their weight in outbreak statistics.

pH is the quieter half of the pair. Push it outside 7.0–7.8 and chlorine becomes measurably less effective at the same concentration, so a pool can hit its chlorine target and still be under-disinfected. Water that has drifted is also the usual culprit behind stinging eyes and irritated skin that parents blame on “too much chlorine.” You do not need to test a public pool yourself — you need to ask. Any staffed facility should be able to tell you the last readings and show you the log, and a front desk that cannot has answered a different and more important question. If you run a pool at home, the CDC advises a DPD test kit over test strips, which work but are noticeably less accurate. For backyard pools specifically, our pool chemical safety guide covers storing and handling those chemicals without creating a second hazard.

💩 What is a “code brown,” and how long does the pool close?

A code brown is a fecal incident in the water, and the closure length depends entirely on whether it was formed stool or diarrhea — roughly 30 minutes for the first, hours for the second. For formed poop, CDC guidance has staff close the pool, remove it with a net or bucket rather than vacuuming it, then raise the free chlorine to 2 ppm at a pH of 7.5 or less and hold those levels for 30 minutes while confirming the filtration system is operating. That is a specific disinfection requirement with a clock attached, not a courtesy delay that a polite request can shorten.

Diarrhea is treated as a different category of event. It is presumed to be a high-risk Cryptosporidium contamination, and a public pool operator responds by raising free chlorine very high for hours. A contaminated hot tub gets the most drastic treatment of all: drained, scrubbed, refilled, and brought to pH 7.0–7.8 with at least 3 ppm free chlorine for 30 minutes. Our full explainer on why pools close for a code brown walks through what staff are doing behind the rope, and why an hour on a deck chair is the system working rather than failing.

🦠 Why can't chlorine just kill everything instantly?

Because chlorine is fast against nearly every germ and remarkably slow against one. The CDC publishes the inactivation times at 1 ppm free chlorine, pH 7.5 or less, and water at 77°F or warmer, with no cyanuric acid in the water. E. coli O157:H7 takes under one minute. Hepatitis A virus takes about 16 minutes. Giardia takes about 45 minutes. Cryptosporidium takes about 15,300 minutes — roughly 10.6 days.

That single outlier explains most of what looks strange about pool rules. Crypto has a tough outer shell that shrugs off normal chlorine levels, which is why it drives most treated-water outbreaks, why diarrheal incidents trigger hours of hyperchlorination instead of a half-hour wait, and why swallowing pool water is the specific habit worth drilling out of a child. Note the fine print too: those times assume no cyanuric acid, and outdoor pools using stabiliser take longer still. Our guide to preventing recreational water illness covers the full range of what circulates in treated water, from Crypto to the causes of swimmer's ear.

🚽 What habits keep my family from being the problem?

Nobody swims with diarrhea — and nobody swims for two full weeks after it stops. That second half is the part that surprises people. Both the CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics give this guidance, because someone recovering from Crypto can keep shedding the parasite for up to two weeks after symptoms end, and because Crypto survives properly chlorinated water for days. One swimmer who feels fine can contaminate a pool that is being run correctly. It is an inconvenient rule and it is the single most protective one on the list.

The rest are small and habitual. Take a bathroom break every 60 minutes whether or not anyone asks — set a timer, because children will not volunteer. Check swim diapers every 30 to 60 minutes and change them well away from the water, remembering that swim diapers slow leakage rather than sealing it. Teach “don't swallow the water” the way you teach any other pool rule, since nearly every recreational water illness begins with a mouthful. And shower before and after: before protects the water for everyone else, after protects your own child's skin and ears. These pair naturally with the venue-specific habits in our public pool safety checklist and hotel pool safety checklist, which cover supervision, barriers, and rescue equipment rather than water chemistry.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if pool water is safe to swim in?

Start with a sixty-second look. Stand at the deep end and find the main drain on the bottom. If you cannot see it clearly, do not let anyone swim, because cloudy water both signals a disinfection or filtration problem and hides a child who is in trouble on the bottom. Then check that the pump is running and the skimmers are pulling water, that the tiles and steps are smooth rather than slimy, and that there is no film or foam sitting on the surface. Finally, ask the lifeguard or front desk for the most recent free chlorine and pH readings. The CDC recommends at least 1 part per million of free chlorine in pools, at least 3 ppm in hot tubs, and a pH between 7.0 and 7.8. A facility that cannot produce those numbers is not monitoring the water.

Does a strong chlorine smell mean a pool is clean?

No, it usually means the opposite. Fresh chlorine in water is close to odorless. The sharp smell people call pool smell is actually chloramines, which form when chlorine reacts with sweat, urine, body oils, and other things swimmers bring into the water. A strong smell therefore indicates a high contamination load, and often a chlorine level that is being consumed faster than it is being replaced. Indoors, that same smell combined with stinging eyes points to inadequate ventilation, because chloramine gas settles at the water surface where children are breathing. A well-run pool barely smells at all. Showering for one minute before getting in removes the sweat and oils that create chloramines in the first place.

What chlorine and pH levels should a swimming pool have?

The CDC recommends a free chlorine concentration of at least 1 part per million in pools and at least 3 ppm in hot tubs, with pH held between 7.0 and 7.8. Free chlorine is the portion still available to kill germs, which is why it matters more than total chlorine. Hot tubs need a higher target because heat and aeration burn off disinfectant much faster. pH matters because chlorine loses effectiveness outside that band, and because water that drifts too far from it starts to irritate eyes and skin. Home pool and hot tub owners should test with a DPD test kit rather than test strips, since strips work but are noticeably less accurate.

How long does a pool stay closed after a code brown?

It depends entirely on whether the incident was formed stool or diarrhea. For formed poop, CDC guidance has staff close the pool, remove it with a net or bucket rather than vacuuming, then raise the free chlorine to 2 parts per million at a pH of 7.5 or less and hold those levels for 30 minutes while confirming the filtration system is running. A diarrheal incident is treated as a high-risk Cryptosporidium event, and a public pool operator raises the free chlorine very high for hours rather than minutes. A contaminated hot tub is drained, scrubbed, refilled, and brought to pH 7.0 to 7.8 with at least 3 ppm free chlorine for 30 minutes. Asking staff to reopen early is asking them to skip a disinfection requirement.

Why does chlorine not kill all pool germs instantly?

Chlorine is fast against most germs and very slow against one. At 1 part per million of free chlorine, at a pH of 7.5 or less and a water temperature of at least 77 degrees Fahrenheit, the CDC puts the inactivation time for E. coli O157:H7 at under one minute, hepatitis A virus at about 16 minutes, and Giardia at about 45 minutes. Cryptosporidium takes roughly 15,300 minutes, which is about 10.6 days. Crypto has a hard outer shell that shrugs off normal chlorine levels, which is why it causes most treated-water outbreaks and why a diarrheal incident triggers hours of hyperchlorination rather than a half-hour wait.

How long should you stay out of the pool after diarrhea?

Two full weeks after the diarrhea has completely stopped. Both the CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics give this guidance, and it surprises most parents, who assume that feeling better is the all-clear. Someone recovering from a Cryptosporidium infection can keep shedding the parasite for up to two weeks after symptoms end, and because Crypto survives properly chlorinated water for days, a single swimmer can contaminate a pool that is being run correctly. The same rule protects your own family in reverse: it is the reason a well-managed facility posts a no-swimming-with-diarrhea policy and takes it seriously.

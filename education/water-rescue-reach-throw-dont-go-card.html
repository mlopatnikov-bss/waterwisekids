Reach, Throw, Don't Go: The Water Rescue Rule Every Parent Should Know

When someone is drowning, your instinct is to jump in. That instinct kills would-be rescuers every summer. There is a safer order that saves both lives — Reach, Throw, Don't Go. Learn it, teach it to your kids, and print the free wallet card below for the pool bag, beach tote, and boat.

🛟 What does "Reach, Throw, Don't Go" mean?

Reach, Throw, Don't Go is the order of safe water rescue for anyone who is not a trained lifeguard: reach the person from land first, throw them something that floats second, and never swim out unless you are trained and carrying flotation. It is taught by the American Red Cross, the YMCA, and water safety educators worldwide because it lets an ordinary bystander save a life without adding a second victim to the emergency. There is exactly one situation where the reaching step is dangerous rather than safe, and the electric shock drowning risk card explains why the power has to come off first.

Knowing how to help without becoming a second victim matters because drowning emergencies unfold in seconds. See how the risk shifts by age and location nationwide in our drowning statistics dashboard.

The logic is simple. The closer you stay to solid ground, the safer you are — and most people in trouble are close enough to reach or throw to. Only when both of those fail do you consider entering the water, and only then if you have the training and equipment to do it safely. The order is not a suggestion; it is a priority list you run top to bottom, fast.

This one rule pairs with the wider system of drowning prevention in our complete drowning prevention guide. Rescue is the last line of defense — but when it's needed, doing it in the right order is everything.

🤚 Step 1: How Do You Reach Someone From Safety?

Reaching means extending an object or your arm to the person while you stay braced on land, so you can pull them in without any risk of being pulled off the edge. Lie down or crouch low on the deck, dock, or shoreline and lower your center of gravity. Then extend the longest thing within reach.

Good reaching tools are everywhere once you look: a shepherd's-crook pool pole, an oar or paddle, a sturdy branch, a pool noodle, a kickboard, a towel, a shirt, or simply your own arm. Keep your weight back and anchored — if the person is heavy or panicking, you want to be able to let go rather than get dragged in. Once they grip the object, pull steadily toward the edge and talk to them the whole time.

At a properly equipped pool, the reaching pole and ring buoy hang on the fence for exactly this moment. Knowing where they are before anyone needs them is part of every good family pool safety checklist.

🎯 Step 2: What Should You Throw to Someone in the Water?

If the person is beyond arm-and-pole range, throw them anything buoyant — a ring buoy, life jacket, boogie board, cooler lid, empty jug, or beach ball — so they have something to hold above water while help comes. The goal of a throw is not to pull them in immediately; it is to give a struggling swimmer flotation so they stop sinking and start breathing.

If the object has a rope or line attached, aim your throw just past and beyond the person, then pull the line so the float drags into their hands. Throw downwind or downcurrent so the object drifts toward them, not away. If your first throw misses, retrieve and throw again — keep talking, keep them focused on you.

This is why throwable flotation belongs on the deck, in the beach bag, and unstowed on every boat. Our open water safety checklist and rip current safety checklist both build "keep flotation within throwing distance" into the plan.

⛔ Step 3: When Is It Ever Safe to Go In After Someone?

Don't Go means you do not swim out to a drowning person unless you are trained in water rescue and carrying a flotation device, because a panicking victim will climb over an untrained rescuer and push them under. This is the step people ignore, and it is the step that gets bystanders killed.

A drowning person is not thinking — they are in a survival reflex. The instinctive drowning response makes them silent, vertical, and unable to control their arms except to press down on anything solid, including you. That is why aquatic rescue deaths so often come in pairs: a child struggles, a parent swims out, and both are lost. It is one of the most heartbreaking patterns in drowning data.

If you truly must enter — a small child, no other option, and you can swim — take flotation with you and push it ahead of you, keeping it between your body and the victim. Never let them grab you directly. Trained rescuers always put an object between themselves and a panicking swimmer for exactly this reason.

🛶 Is it “Reach, Throw, Row, Go” or “Reach, Throw, Don't Go”?

Both are real. “Reach, Throw, Row, Go” is the four-step lifeguard and boating version, where “Row” means take a boat, board, or paddlecraft out to the person. “Reach, Throw, Don't Go” is the three-step version taught to the public and to children, because “Go” is the step untrained rescuers should never take.

The confusion is common and worth clearing up, because the two versions are aimed at different people. The full ladder runs in order of increasing risk to the rescuer:

  • Reach — extend a pole, paddle, branch, or your arm from a stable position on land or the deck. Lowest risk, highest success rate for near-shore incidents.
  • Throw — send out a ring buoy, life jacket, cooler lid, or anything buoyant, ideally with a line attached.
  • Row — use a boat, kayak, paddleboard, or rescue board to bring flotation to the person while you stay out of the water. This step assumes you can handle the craft in the conditions; in a rip current or surf, an inexperienced paddler becomes a second casualty.
  • Go — enter the water and swim to the person. This is a trained-lifeguard step, performed with a rescue tube or buoy held between rescuer and victim, never empty-handed.

Public safety campaigns compress this to three steps for a simple reason: the fourth step kills bystanders. The American Red Cross and the U.S. Coast Guard both report that would-be rescuers — frequently parents going in after a child — make up a meaningful share of open-water drowning deaths every year. Teaching “Don't Go” removes the step most likely to turn one emergency into two.

So use whichever version fits the audience. If you own a boat or paddle regularly, know all four and know that “Row” is available to you. If you are teaching children, a swim class, or a neighborhood group, teach three steps and stop at “Don't Go.”

📞 When Do You Call 911 and Start CPR After a Water Rescue?

The moment the person is out of the water or someone is free to do it, call 911, and begin CPR immediately if the person is unresponsive and you are trained. Water rescue does not end when the person reaches the edge — a swimmer who was submerged can still be in serious danger.

Send a specific person to call for help ("You in the red shirt — call 911 now"), so the job doesn't fall through the cracks. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR right away; drowning is primarily a breathing emergency, so rescue breaths matter. Have any submerged child evaluated by a medical professional even if they seem fine afterward. Our guide to what to do in a drowning emergency walks through the full sequence step by step — read it before swim season, not during it.

🧒 How Do You Teach Kids to Reach or Throw and Never Go?

Children should be taught to reach or throw from land and then yell for an adult — and never, under any circumstances, to swim out to rescue someone. A child who jumps in to save a friend or sibling can quickly become a second victim, because they lack the size, strength, and training to break free from a panicking grip.

Make the rule a rhyme they can remember under stress: "Reach or throw, don't go — then yell for a grown-up." Practice it at the pool the way you'd practice a fire drill. Point out the reaching pole and the ring buoy. Let them rehearse throwing a rescue tube to a target. This turns a scary idea into a confident, automatic action — and it reinforces that getting a capable adult is always step one.

🛡️ How Do You Prevent Ever Needing a Water Rescue?

Reach and throw skills are your emergency backup — but layered prevention is what keeps the emergency from happening at all. Water rescue sits at the very end of the five layers of drowning protection: barriers, supervision, water competency, life jackets, and emergency readiness. Rescue readiness is that last layer. The earlier layers are the ones that do the heavy lifting.

Active supervision closes the gap where most drownings begin — the simplest system for it is a designated Water Watcher. And the layer that travels with your child everywhere is water competency. According to research cited by the American Academy of Pediatrics, formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1–4. Survival skills like back-floating and rolling to breathe are exactly what buy time before any rescuer can reach the water.

If your child isn't enrolled yet, summer is when the gap is widest and practice time is greatest. Quality, safety-first swim programs teach survival skills from as early as a few months old, year-round. For how to evaluate a program, see our guide to choosing swim lessons for kids.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why shouldn't I just swim out to save a drowning person?

Because untrained rescuers frequently become victims themselves. A drowning person is in a panic state and will instinctively climb on top of anything that reaches them, including a rescuer, pushing that person underwater. Every year parents and bystanders die attempting well-meaning water rescues, especially in rip currents, cold water, and open water. Reaching or throwing from safety lets you help without putting a second life at risk. Enter the water only if you are trained and have a flotation device to keep between you and the victim.

What can I use to reach or throw to someone in the water?

Use whatever is within arm's reach. To reach: a shepherd's-crook pool pole, an oar or paddle, a branch, a towel, a pool noodle, or your own arm while lying down. To throw: a ring buoy, a life jacket, a boogie or kickboard, a cooler or its lid, an empty jug, a beach ball, or any buoyant object. If a floating object has a rope attached, throw it past the person and pull the line toward them. If nothing floats, form a human chain from shore with people holding wrists while the lead person stays in shallow water.

What should children be taught about water rescue?

Teach children the rule 'Reach or throw, don't go — then yell for a grown-up.' A child's only job in a water emergency is to get an adult and, if they can do it safely from land, to reach or throw a floating object. Children should never enter the water to rescue anyone, because they lack the strength and training and can easily be pulled under. Practicing the words and the actions ahead of time helps kids act correctly under stress.

Does knowing water rescue replace swim lessons?

No. Reach and throw skills help you respond to an emergency, but they do not prevent one. Formal swim lessons build the water competency that stops most emergencies from happening in the first place. According to research cited by the American Academy of Pediatrics, participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a reduction in drowning risk of up to 88% for children ages 1 to 4. Rescue readiness and swim skills work together: one is your backup layer, the other is your child's everyday protection.

Is the water rescue rule Reach, Throw, Row, Go or Reach, Throw, Don't Go?

Both are real versions of the same ladder. Reach, Throw, Row, Go is the four-step lifeguard and boating sequence, where Row means taking a boat, kayak, or rescue board out to the person while staying out of the water, and Go means swimming to them with a rescue tube. Reach, Throw, Don't Go is the three-step public and children's version, which deliberately removes the final step because untrained rescuers who enter the water frequently become the second victim.

Winter Swim Lessons Without the Shivers: The Pool-to-Car Routine

Nobody quits winter swim lessons because of the lesson. The water is warm, the instructor is kind, and the child is usually having a good time. Families quit because of the fifteen minutes afterwards — wet hair, a cold changing room, a fight over a car seat and a child who cries all the way home. Here is the routine that fixes those fifteen minutes, and a free printable card to keep it going through to spring.

Quick Summary: Winter swim lessons are won or lost in the fifteen minutes between the pool and the car. Before you leave, dress your child for the exit — easy clothes, slip-on shoes, hooded towel and hat packed on top. At the edge, have the towel open and waiting, hood up, walk. In the changing room, run a ten-minute sequence: a quick warm rinse, hair and ears first, dry the body fully, dress the core first, then hat on. In the car, no puffy coat under the car seat harness — the American Academy of Pediatrics advises thin layers under the straps and the coat on backwards or a blanket over the top. And drop the old worry: wet hair does not cause colds; viruses do. The routine is about keeping your child comfortable enough to want to come back next week.

❄️ Why the fifteen minutes after the lesson decide everything

Ask a swim school when families drop off and many will tell you the same thing: after the holidays, when the weather turns properly cold. It is rarely the lesson that goes wrong. Indoor teaching pools are kept warm precisely so that children can concentrate on learning rather than on shivering, and most children come out of a winter lesson flushed and pleased with themselves.

Then they step out of warm water into cooler air. They stand dripping while a parent looks for the towel. The changing room is busy and chilly. Their clothes stick to damp skin, their hair drips down their neck, and by the time they reach the car they are cold, hungry and cross. Do that a few weeks in a row and a child who loved the pool starts saying they do not want to go, and a tired parent starts wondering whether it would be simpler to stop until summer.

That would be a shame, because winter swim lessons are some of the most valuable a child takes. The fix is not heroic. It is a routine, the same every week, that makes the exit quick and warm enough that the good feeling from the lesson survives the trip home.

🏠 Before you leave home: dress for the exit, not the entrance

Most families dress children for the walk into the pool. The outfit that matters is the one they put on wet. Choose clothes that go on easily over damp skin: elastic waists instead of buttons, a zip-up top rather than something tight over the head, loose socks, and shoes your child can slip on without sitting down to do laces. Every fiddly fastener is a minute more standing cold.

Pack the bag in reverse order of need. The hooded towel or towel poncho, a hat and a dry fleece go in last so that they come out first; goggles, shampoo and the wet bag go underneath. Our swim bag checklist covers the full packing list — this routine is about the order you use it in.

A light snack before the lesson helps more than parents expect. Swimming uses a lot of energy, and a hungry child both feels the cold more and runs out of patience sooner. Save something warm to drink for the car. Visit the bathroom before changing, and aim to arrive five to ten minutes early: enough time to settle without rushing, but not so long that your child sits on a cool deck in a swimsuit. Wait in warm clothes and strip down at the last moment.

🧺 The poolside exit: the most important two minutes

The single biggest drop in warmth happens between climbing out of the water and reaching the changing room. So have the towel open and ready at the edge, and wrap your child the moment they are out. A hooded towel or poncho is worth buying for winter alone; the hood goes up straight away, because a wet head in cool air is where most of the chill comes from.

Then walk. Pool decks are slippery, and a hurried child tangled in a long towel is a fall waiting to happen. Sandals or pool shoes for the walk keep feet warmer and off wet floors. If the instructor gives feedback at the end of the lesson, take it — wrapped up — and ask what your child could practise at home this week. It gives you something positive to talk about on the way to the changing room, which helps more than it sounds.

⏱️ The ten-minute changing room sequence

A predictable order is what makes the changing room quick. Children cope with cold much better when they know exactly what comes next and how long it lasts. This is the sequence on the printable card:

  • Minutes 0–2: a quick warm rinse. A short shower takes pool water off skin and hair and warms a chilled child up. Keep it warm rather than hot, and keep it short; long hot showers dry out skin that winter air is already drying.
  • Minutes 2–4: hair and ears first. Squeeze and blot the hair rather than rubbing. Tilt the head to each side to drain the ears, then dry the outer ear with a corner of the towel.
  • Minutes 4–6: dry the whole body, feet last. Damp skin under dry clothes still feels cold. Between the toes counts. If your child’s skin is dry or prone to eczema, moisturise now while the skin is still slightly damp.
  • Minutes 6–8: dress the core first. A vest or T-shirt and a warm top before pants and socks. A warm chest takes the edge off everything else.
  • Minutes 8–10: hair and hat. A few minutes under a dryer if one is available, then the hat goes on and stays on until you are home.

Aim for water to car in about fifteen minutes. The printable has a log for writing down how long it actually takes. Most families find the first week runs long and the third week is dramatically shorter, simply because everyone knows the order.

💇 Wet hair, long hair and the hair dryer question

Hair is where winter routines usually stall, especially for children with long or thick hair. A few things help. Blotting and squeezing with a towel removes more water and causes fewer tangles than rubbing. A microfiber towel or hair wrap speeds this up noticeably. If the changing room has a hair dryer, use a warm rather than hot setting, hold it well away from the scalp, and aim for “damp” rather than “dry” — the hat does the rest.

For children who swim every week, chlorine is the longer-term issue. Wetting hair with clean water before the lesson, a swim cap, and a rinse and conditioner afterwards all help. Our guide to hair care for kids who swim goes through it, and post-swim care for kids covers skin and eyes as well.

👂 Ears and skin in the colder months

Water left in the ear canal is what sets up swimmer’s ear, so drain and dry after every lesson: tilt the head each way, then dry the outer ear gently. Nothing goes inside the ear — cotton swabs push wax and water deeper and can scratch the canal. The CDC’s swimmer’s ear advice is to keep ears as dry as possible and to see a healthcare provider for ear pain. Children who get repeated ear problems may do better with well-fitting ear plugs or a cap; our swimmer’s ear prevention guide has the details.

Winter air is dry and indoor heating makes it drier, so skin that coped fine with summer lessons may start to itch. A short warm rinse rather than a long hot shower, patting rather than rubbing dry, and a fragrance-free moisturiser straight afterwards usually settle it. If your child has eczema, swimming with eczema explains how to keep lessons going comfortably.

🚗 The winter coat and car seat rule

This is the one part of the routine that is about safety rather than comfort, and it is easy to get wrong when a child is cold and you are in a hurry. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against bulky coats and snowsuits under car seat harness straps. In a crash, the padding compresses and leaves the harness too loose to hold a child properly.

The answer is to keep your child warm in a different way. Dress them in thin layers, such as a long-sleeved top and a fleece, buckle them in and tighten the harness, and then put their coat on backwards over the straps or tuck a blanket over the top. If you are unsure whether a particular coat is too thick, the AAP describes a simple check: buckle your child in wearing the coat and tighten the harness, take them out without loosening it, remove the coat and buckle them back in. If you can now pinch slack in the strap at the shoulder, that coat is too bulky to wear under the harness.

If you can safely warm the car for a few minutes before you get in, do. A cold car seat against a damp back can undo a good changing room in minutes.

🧠 Does swimming in winter make kids sick?

This is the question behind a lot of winter drop-outs, so it is worth answering plainly. Going outside with wet hair does not, by itself, give a child a cold. The CDC is clear that colds are caused by viruses, and that children catch them at any time of year. Many respiratory viruses do circulate more in winter, which is why winter feels like cold season — but the virus has to come from somewhere, and it comes from other people, their hands and the surfaces they touch.

So the routine is not about warding off illness. It is about comfort. A child who is chilled and miserable after every lesson will start to dread the next one, and that dread is what ends winter lessons. The illness habits that actually help are ordinary ones: wash hands after the changing room and before the snack, don’t share towels or water bottles, and keep your child home when they are unwell.

🤒 When to skip a lesson

Keep your child home with a fever, vomiting or diarrhea, ear pain, an open wound, or a rash that could be contagious, and check your program’s policy on when they can return. Diarrhea in particular matters for everyone else in the pool. A mild runny nose in a child who is otherwise bright and energetic is more of a judgement call. Our sick-day swim lesson decision guide walks through the common situations, and swimming when kids are sick explains the reasoning. Ear pain that doesn’t settle, or pain when the outer ear is touched, is a reason to call your pediatrician.

👶 Babies, toddlers and parent-and-child classes

Babies and toddlers lose heat faster than older children, so the routine matters even more for them. Keep the time between leaving the water and getting dressed as short as possible, cover the head first, and dress the baby before yourself. In parent-and-child classes, where you are in the water too, put a change of clothes for yourself somewhere you can reach it one-handed, and think about who holds the baby while you get dressed. A warm feed or bottle afterwards is often the moment a tired baby settles.

If your child starts shivering hard, has blue-tinged lips or is unusually quiet during a lesson, it is fine to take them out early and warm them up. A good instructor will be watching for the same signs.

🏊 Why keeping lessons going through winter pays off

Swimming skills are like any other physical skill: they fade when they are not used. A child who stops in September and restarts in June often spends the first weeks of summer relearning floating, breath control and getting to an edge — the very skills that matter most for safety. Our guide to year-round versus seasonal lessons sets out the case, and the fall skill retention checklist helps you see what your child has held on to since summer.

Winter is also often a calmer time to learn. There is less competition for lesson slots, children are in a weekly rhythm with school, and progress made in January shows up on the first warm day of the year. If your child isn’t enrolled yet, look for a program with a warm indoor pool, a clean, reasonably warm changing area and a clear illness policy. You can find swim lessons near you, and the fall swim schedule planner helps you fit lessons around the rest of the week.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can kids get sick from swimming in winter?

Swimming in winter does not by itself make children sick. The CDC notes that colds are caused by viruses, and children catch them at any time of year. Indoor lessons in a warm pool are not the risk; what spreads winter illness is contact with germs on hands and shared surfaces. Washing hands after the changing room and keeping children home when they are unwell matter far more than whether hair is fully dry.

Does going outside with wet hair cause a cold?

No. Wet hair in cold air can make a child feel chilly and uncomfortable, but a cold is a viral infection and needs a virus to cause it. That said, a child who is cold and miserable after every lesson can quickly start to dread swimming, so drying hair and putting on a hat is worth doing for comfort and for keeping lessons enjoyable.

How do I dry my child’s hair after a winter swim lesson?

Squeeze and blot the hair with a towel rather than rubbing it, which reduces tangles and breakage. If the changing room has a hair dryer, a few minutes on a warm, not hot, setting held well away from the scalp will take long hair from dripping to damp. Then put a hat on and leave it on until you are home. For very long or thick hair, a microfiber towel or hair wrap speeds things up.

Should my child wear a winter coat in the car seat after swim lessons?

Not under the harness. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against bulky coats and snowsuits under car seat straps, because the padding compresses in a crash and leaves the harness too loose. Dress your child in thin warm layers such as a fleece, buckle and tighten the harness, and then put the coat on backwards over the straps or tuck a blanket over the top.

How long should it take to get from the pool to the car in winter?

About fifteen minutes is a realistic target once the routine is familiar: a couple of minutes to wrap up and walk to the changing room, around ten minutes to rinse, dry, dress and put on a hat, and a few minutes to the car. Most families find the first week takes much longer and the time drops steadily as the routine becomes automatic.

How do I stop water in my child’s ears after swimming?

Have your child tilt their head to each side to let water drain out, then gently dry the outer ear with the corner of a towel. Do not put cotton swabs or anything else inside the ear canal. Children who get swimmer’s ear often may benefit from well-fitting ear plugs or a swim cap, and ear pain, or pain when the outer ear is touched, is a reason to call your pediatrician.

When should my child skip a winter swim lesson?

Keep your child home with a fever, vomiting or diarrhea, ear pain, an open wound, or a rash that might be contagious, and follow your swim program’s return policy. A mild runny nose in a child who is otherwise energetic and happy is a judgement call, and a sick-day decision guide can help you decide.

Are winter swim lessons worth it?

For most families, yes. Swimming skills fade quickly during months without practice, so a child who keeps a weekly lesson going through winter usually starts the summer where they finished the previous one instead of relearning the basics. The practical challenge is keeping the post-lesson routine comfortable enough that your child wants to keep going.

Indoor Pool Birthday Party Safety Checklist (Free Printable)

A birthday party at an indoor pool is a winter favorite: warm water, a party room and no weather to worry about. It also means a crowd of excited children, some of whom you have never seen swim, a lifeguard who may be watching the whole pool and a lot of adults who each assume someone else is watching. This checklist covers what to ask the venue, how to plan the guest list, Water Watchers, life jackets, the rules to say out loud, headcounts and what to do in an emergency, with a free one-page printable.

🗺️ Before You Book: Questions for the Venue

Winter is indoor pool party season, and rec centers, hotel pools, community pools and some swim schools rent out pool time for birthdays. Ask the venue a few questions before you pay a deposit. Is a certified lifeguard on duty for the whole party, and is the guard assigned only to your group or watching the whole pool? Will the pool, or at least the deep end, be roped off for your party, or will you share it with the public? Do children take a swim test before they can go into deeper water? Does the venue have U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jackets in children’s sizes? How many guests can be in the water at once, and for how long? Where is the party room, and is it separate from the pool deck? Finally, ask about the emergency plan and where the AED is kept. Our indoor pool safety checklist and guide to community pool rules have more to look for.

💌 Invitations: Ask About Every Child’s Swimming

At a party you are responsible for children whose swimming you may never have seen. Add one line to the invitation asking each family how well their child swims, and whether the child needs a life jacket. For young children and anyone who is not a strong swimmer, ask a parent or another trusted adult to stay for the party instead of planning a drop-off. Keep a guest list with each child’s name, swimming level and the adult responsible for them; you will use it for headcounts. Our pool playdate safety checklist and multi-child water supervision plan help when you are watching other people’s children.

👀 Name Water Watchers, Even With a Lifeguard

Parties are loud and busy, and drowning is usually quiet. With many adults around, each one can assume someone else is watching. A lifeguard watches the whole pool, often with many swimmers at once; your Water Watcher watches your group. Name one adult at a time who is not drinking, not on a phone, not serving cake and close enough to see every child. Trade the role in about 15-minute shifts with a clear hand-off, and hand the next watcher a Water Watcher card or tag so everyone knows who is on duty. Weak swimmers and non-swimmers stay within arm’s reach of an adult in the water. Our guide to why lifeguards don’t replace supervision explains the difference.

🦺 Life Jackets for Weaker Swimmers

A child who cannot swim, or who is still a beginner, wears a U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket that fits, and still has an adult within arm’s reach. Inflatable arm floaties, rings, noodles and pool toys are not life jackets and can slip off or deflate. Check the label and fit before the child gets in: the jacket should be snug and should not ride up past the chin or ears when you lift it at the shoulders. Ask the venue whether it has approved children’s jackets, or ask families to bring their own. Our life jacket guide and floaties and puddle jumpers checklist cover sizing.

📣 Pool Rules to Say Out Loud

Before anyone gets in, gather the children on the deck for one minute and say the rules out loud. Walk on the deck. Stay in the area for our party. Feet first unless the lifeguard says diving is allowed. No breath-holding contests or “who can stay under longest” games, and no pushing or dunking. Get out when the lifeguard or a Water Watcher calls. Have a swim test or a quick check in shallow water before any child goes deeper, and keep children who have not passed it in the shallow area. Our underwater games safety card explains why breath-holding games are dangerous even for strong swimmers.

🔢 Headcounts, Locker Rooms and the Party Room

Most mix-ups happen when the group is moving. Count every child against your guest list when they get in the water, at every break, when they leave the pool for cake and presents, and before anyone leaves the building. Clear everyone out of the water for food and gifts, and keep the party room door between the children and the pool if you can. Send younger children to the locker room or restroom with an adult, and older children with a buddy, following the venue’s rules. If the party goes back in the water after cake, restart the Water Watcher rotation. Our locker room safety checklist has more ideas.

🎁 If Your Child Is the Guest

If your child is invited to a pool party, tell the host honestly how well your child swims. Stay for the party, or name another trusted adult who will. Pack a swimsuit, towel, goggles if your child uses them and, for a weaker swimmer, a fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket. Remind your child of your family rules on the drive over: stay where the adults can see you, walk on the deck and get out when you are called. Our family water safety pledge is an easy way to practice the rules.

🎽 Swim Skills Make Parties Safer

Pool parties are a good reminder that children meet water all year, not only in summer. Swim lessons build floating, breath control and turning back to the wall, and indoor lessons let children keep those skills strong through the winter. You can browse swim lessons near you or read our guide to winter swim lessons. Families in New Jersey and Pennsylvania can see our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia, which link to the official location pages. Skills lower risk but never replace close adult supervision.

🆘 If Something Goes Wrong

Before the party starts, find the lifeguard station, the emergency phone and the AED, and save the venue address in your phone. If a child is missing, check the water first and tell the lifeguard right away so the pool can be cleared and searched, then check the locker rooms, restrooms and party room. If a child is in trouble in the water, alert the lifeguard, call 911 or have a named person call, and follow the dispatcher’s and lifeguard’s instructions. If there is no lifeguard, reach or throw something from the edge rather than going in after them. Our water emergency action plan, reach, throw, don’t go card and CPR basics for parents help you prepare.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a lifeguard enough supervision at a pool birthday party?

No. A lifeguard is an important layer, but at a party pool the guard is usually scanning many swimmers at once, sometimes including other groups. Name one adult Water Watcher who is not drinking, not on a phone and close enough to see every child in your group, and trade the role in about 15-minute shifts with a clear hand-off. Ask the venue whether a guard is assigned only to your party or watching the whole pool.

How many adults should watch a pool birthday party?

Enough that every weak swimmer or non-swimmer has an adult within arm’s reach in the water, plus one Water Watcher on the deck who is doing nothing else. For young children, ask a parent or another trusted adult to stay for each child rather than planning a drop-off party.

Should kids who can’t swim wear life jackets at a pool party?

Yes. A child who cannot swim, or who is still a beginner, should wear a U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket that fits, and still have an adult within arm’s reach. Inflatable arm floaties, rings and pool toys are not life jackets. Ask the venue whether it has approved children’s life jackets, or ask families to bring their own.

What should I ask the venue before booking an indoor pool party?

Ask whether a certified lifeguard is on duty for the whole party and whether the guard is assigned only to your group, whether the pool or deep end will be roped off or shared with the public, whether children take a swim test, whether approved children’s life jackets are available, how many guests are allowed in the water, where the party room is and what the emergency plan is, including where the AED is kept.

Should parents stay at a child’s pool party?

For young children and for any child who is not a strong swimmer, yes, or they should name another trusted adult to stay in their place. Tell the host how well your child swims so the host can plan, and pack a fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket if your child needs one.

What should we do if a child goes missing at a pool party?

Check the water first. Tell the lifeguard right away so the pool can be cleared and searched, then check the locker rooms, restrooms and party room. If a child is found in the water, get them out, call 911 or have someone call, and follow the dispatcher’s and lifeguard’s instructions.

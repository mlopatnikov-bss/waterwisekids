Swim Level Translator Chart

Your old branch called her a Polliwog. The new one says Stage 3. The school across town says Pike. All three describe roughly the same child. This chart lines the naming systems up against the skills they actually mean, so you can walk into a new program knowing where your child belongs.

❓ Why the Names Never Match

  • The YMCA is a federation, not a franchise. Roughly 2,700 independent local YMCAs each set their own programming, and none are required to use the national names.
  • Legacy names stuck. Many branches still use the labels they adopted in the 1970s, 1980s, or 1990s and have never updated them.
  • Private schools invent their own. Commercial swim schools use proprietary level names and level counts that map to no external standard.
  • Level counts differ too. A six-level program and a twelve-level program can teach exactly the same skills at different granularity.
  • So a level name alone tells you almost nothing. Two children in “Level 3” at different schools may be a year apart in ability.

📋 Before You Switch — Do These Five Things

  • Ask the old school for the progress card. Most programs issue one at the end of a session, and it is the single most useful thing to hand the new school.
  • Write down what your child can do, in seconds and lengths. “Back floats ten seconds unassisted” travels; “she was a Guppy” does not.
  • Ask the new school for its written advancement criteria. Then check which items your child already meets.
  • Expect a placement assessment in the first lesson. Most schools run one; treat the first placement as provisional.
  • Ask for a re-evaluation early if the fit is wrong. Too high erodes confidence, too low erodes interest — both stall progress.
  • Do not let a level label change discourage your child. Moving from “Minnow” to “Level 2” is a relabel, not a demotion.

🏊 Skill Transfer Card — Tick What Your Child Can Do Today

  • Puts face in the water willingly and blows bubbles
  • Front floats without assistance
  • Back floats independently — note how many seconds: ______
  • Rolls from front to back and recovers a breath
  • Pushes off the wall and glides
  • Moves forward independently — note how many feet: ______
  • Swims freestyle with rotary side breathing
  • Swims a recognizable backstroke
  • Swims breaststroke or butterfly
  • Enters, turns around, returns to the wall, and exits unassisted
  • Jumps in over the head, surfaces, and returns to the edge
  • Treads water — note how many seconds: ______
Hand this ticked list to the new instructor. It places a child faster and more accurately than any level name, and it is the same vocabulary the American Red Cross uses to describe water competency.

The Right Level Is the One That Matches the Skill

A good program places a child on what they can actually do, explains the criteria to move up, and reassesses without being asked. Formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1–4 — what matters is that the lessons keep going.

Is Your Child Ready for Swim Team? Score the Four Gates Yourself

A novice swim team tryout is not a speed test. It checks four things — stroke proficiency, endurance away from the wall, coachability in a shared lane, and whose idea this is — and a parent can score every one of them at an ordinary open-swim visit weeks before tryout day. Here is what each gate looks like in the water, why Gate 4 outranks the other three, what to do when the honest answer is not yet, and a free printable scorecard that marks all four in one pass.

Quick Summary: A novice swim team tryout tests four things, and you can score every one of them yourself at an ordinary open-swim visit weeks beforehand. Gate 1 is stroke proficiency — freestyle with the face in the water and side breathing, plus a recognizable backstroke. Gate 2 is continuous swimming without standing up or hanging on the wall, twice. Gate 3 is coachability: taking a correction, waiting a turn, being on deck without a parent. Gate 4 is whose idea this is. A child can pass three gates and still have a miserable season because of the fourth, and Gate 4 outranks the rest. When a gate comes back not yet, the fix is almost always more taught swimming rather than more pool time — and the re-score is weeks away, not years.

🎯 What does a swim team tryout actually test?

Parents tend to arrive at this question with a speed frame: is my child fast enough? For a novice or developmental group, that is usually the wrong frame. A coach filling a beginner lane is not looking for times. They are looking for a swimmer who can be run in a lane for an hour without needing rescuing, individual supervision, or a parent on the deck.

That resolves into four gates. They are independent — being strong at one says almost nothing about the others — and three of the four have nothing to do with how fast your child swims.

Gate 1, stroke proficiency. Can they swim the strokes the tryout asks for in a form a coach can recognize and correct? Gate 2, endurance without the wall. Can they keep going for a continuous distance without standing up, grabbing the lane rope, or hanging on the wall to recover? Gate 3, coachability. Can they take an instruction, wait a turn, and manage themselves on deck? Gate 4, motivation. Do they want this, and does the family schedule genuinely carry it?

Our full guide to swim team readiness works through each of these in depth. What this page adds is the measurement: how a parent scores all four without a stopwatch, without a coach, and without telling the child they are being assessed.

🏊 Why score it at open swim instead of at tryouts?

Because a child being watched for a verdict does not swim like themselves. Tryout day compresses a first impression, an unfamiliar pool, an unfamiliar adult and a parent radiating expectation into twenty minutes — and a swimmer who would be fine in week three of practice can look unready in that window.

An ordinary pool visit removes all of it. You get to watch a child swim the way they actually swim, repeatedly, across half an hour, under real fatigue rather than performance nerves. And crucially you get the result weeks early, while there is still time to do something about a gap.

The method is simple and deliberately low-key: go to open swim, bring the scorecard, and say nothing about scoring. Watch rather than quiz — asking “can you swim freestyle?” returns an answer about confidence, not ability. Score each gate Yes, Almost, or Not Yet, and date it, because the whole point is to run it again in two or three months.

📏 Gate 1: which strokes does your child actually need?

Less than most families fear. For a novice group, the realistic expectation is freestyle and backstroke that a coach can work with. Butterfly and breaststroke are typically taught on the team rather than required to join one — though this varies by program, which is why the first question on the scorecard is a question for the coach, not for you.

Within freestyle, one detail matters more than any other: breathing to the side with the face in the water, rather than swimming head-up with the chin forward. Head-up freestyle is the most common single reason a swimmer is placed in a pre-team group. It is also a technique fault rather than a fitness one, which is good news — it responds to instruction quickly and does not respond to more laps at all.

The other two things to watch are a kick that actually moves the swimmer (legs near the surface, small and fast, rather than a bicycle kick with the hips sinking) and the ability to start at the wall and finish at the wall without a push or a stop. Our guide to swim strokes for kids covers what each stroke should look like at this stage.

💨 Gate 2: how much endurance is enough?

Ask the team for the number. Required tryout distances differ between programs, and guessing is how families over-prepare for one team and under-prepare for another. What is consistent is the shape of the requirement: continuous, unassisted, and repeatable.

Continuous means no standing up, no lane rope, no mid-length wall rest. Unassisted means they get themselves there. Repeatable is the one parents skip and the one that predicts practice — have them do it, rest a minute, and do it again. The second effort tells you what an hour of practice will feel like; the first tells you very little.

Watch how they finish, too. Tired and grinning is ready. Coughing, panicky, or climbing straight out is a clear not yet, and it is a meaningful signal rather than a discouraging one.

One hard line: never test or train endurance through breath-holding or underwater distance swimming. The American Red Cross, USA Swimming and the YMCA all prohibit prolonged breath-holding and hypoxic training, including as a game, because it can cause a blackout in a strong swimmer with no warning and no visible struggle. If a program you are considering runs breath-holding contests, treat that as information about the program.

🧠 Gate 3: the gate nobody tells parents about

This is where genuinely capable swimmers most often come up short, and it has nothing to do with the water. Practice is a continuous stream of instructions and corrections delivered to a group, in a shared lane, by an adult who is not your child’s parent.

The observable signals: can they hear an instruction on the wall and visibly attempt it on the next length? Can they wait and go when sent rather than pushing off on the heels of the swimmer ahead? Can they handle their own goggles, cap and water bottle? Can they be corrected without shutting down? And can they be at the pool without you in sight — because most teams do not want parents on deck?

Here is the part worth knowing: this gate is what group lessons quietly build. Taking turns, following a set, being corrected by an instructor — that is the same skill a coach needs on day one. A child who has only ever swum at family pool days can be a strong swimmer and still score Not Yet here, purely for lack of exposure. The transition is covered in more depth in our guide to moving from rec swim to swim team.

❤️ Gate 4: whose idea is this?

Gate 4 outranks the other three. A ready swimmer who did not choose this tends to quit within a couple of months, and often stops swimming altogether — which is a worse outcome than never having tried out.

The test is whether your child can say, in their own words, what they want out of it. Friends, racing, getting better, a particular stroke — any answer counts. “I don’t know, you signed me up” does not. They should also know what they are agreeing to: how many practices a week, that practice is mostly laps, and that a meet is a long day containing a very short swim.

The most common false positive is a child who loves swimming and hates practicing. These are genuinely different preferences, and thirty minutes on a bench watching a real practice separates them better than any conversation. Most programs will let you do exactly that; a program that will not is telling you something.

Gate 4 is also a family gate. The drive, the cost, the weekend meets and the effect on a sibling’s calendar are part of the honest answer. So is an agreed exit — “we will finish this season, then decide” is a promise a child can hold on a hard Tuesday. And because competitive training brings its own considerations around load, rest and burnout, our guide to competitive swimming safety is worth reading before the first season rather than during it.

🚩 What if the answer is not yet?

Then you have learned something useful weeks before tryout day, which was the entire point. Not yet is a timing verdict with a shelf life measured in weeks, not a talent verdict — write the re-score date on the scorecard before you leave the pool.

What closes the gap depends on which gate failed, and the answer is more consistent than families expect. Gaps at Gates 1 and 2 are usually technique problems wearing a fitness costume: head-up freestyle, a sinking kick, a breathing pattern that runs the swimmer out of air. Those respond to instruction. They do not respond to more unsupervised laps — if anything, repetition grooves the fault deeper. Gate 3 gaps close fastest in a structured group class, which is simply a lower-stakes version of the same environment.

Which means the honest version of the answer, most of the time, is that a child who is not ready for team needs more taught swimming: a progressive class with an instructor correcting stroke under mild fatigue. That is what a year-round lesson program does. British Swim School and similar progressive programs are built around exactly this — stroke development past the survival stage, in warm water, on a schedule that keeps the skill from going stale between seasons.

Two things not to do. Do not run tryout training at home — timed sprints and endurance drills from a parent on the deck raise anxiety and change nothing a coach will see. And do not leave your child to infer the verdict from your silence; tell them the plan in their own words, with a date attached.

🗣️ What should you ask the coach before tryout day?

Six questions, and the first one is worth more than the rest combined.

“Which strokes and what distance does the tryout actually ask for?” Get the specific number. “Is there a pre-team or developmental group?” Many programs have one, it is where most not-quite-ready swimmers belong, and it is often not advertised anywhere. “How many practices a week is the novice group, and is partial attendance allowed?” Ask before committing the family calendar. “What does a season cost, all in?” Dues, suit, registration, meet fees and travel — the total, not the monthly figure. “Can we watch a practice first?” “What is your policy on breath-holding and underwater work?” You want a coach who answers that one immediately and correctly.

If cost is the constraint rather than readiness, that is a solvable and separate problem — see our guide to swim lesson scholarships and free programs.

One thing sits underneath all of it and does not change with the verdict. No program, at any level, makes a child drown-proof — and that stays true for a competitive swimmer. Swim team changes what a child can do; it does not remove the need for four-sided pool fencing with a self-closing and self-latching gate, undistracted supervision, properly fitted life jackets in open water, and an adult trained in CPR.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my child is ready for swim team?

Score four gates rather than looking at age or speed. Gate 1 is stroke proficiency: freestyle with the face in the water and side breathing, plus a recognizable backstroke. Gate 2 is endurance without the wall: a continuous swim with no standing up or hanging on the wall, done twice. Gate 3 is coachability: taking a correction, waiting a turn, and managing themselves on deck without a parent. Gate 4 is the child's own motivation, plus whether the family schedule genuinely carries the commitment. You can score all four yourself at an ordinary open-swim visit, weeks before a tryout.

What strokes does my child need before swim team tryouts?

For a novice or developmental group, usually freestyle and backstroke in a form a coach can recognize and correct. Butterfly and breaststroke are typically taught on the team rather than required to join it, though this varies by program. The detail that matters most is freestyle with the face in the water and breathing to the side; head-up freestyle is the most common single reason a swimmer is placed in a pre-team group instead of the team.

How far should my child be able to swim before joining a swim team?

Ask the specific team, because required tryout distances differ between programs and guessing leads families to over-prepare for one and under-prepare for another. What is consistent is the shape of the requirement: the swim must be continuous, with no standing up, no grabbing the lane rope and no mid-length wall rest, and your child should be able to repeat it after a short rest. The second effort is what predicts practice; the first tells you very little.

Should my child practice holding their breath to build swim endurance?

No. The American Red Cross, USA Swimming and the YMCA all prohibit prolonged breath-holding and hypoxic training, including as a game or a contest. It can cause a blackout in a strong swimmer with no warning and no visible struggle. Build endurance with continuous swimming that includes normal breathing, and treat a program that runs breath-holding contests as a warning about that program.

What does it mean if my child is not ready for swim team yet?

It is a timing verdict with a shelf life of weeks, not a talent verdict, and what closes the gap depends on which gate came back short. Gaps in stroke proficiency and endurance are usually technique problems rather than fitness problems, so they respond to instruction rather than to more unsupervised laps. Gaps in coachability close fastest in a structured group class. Set a re-score date eight to twelve weeks out and tell your child the plan in plain words rather than letting them infer it from your silence.

Will more pool time get my child ready for swim team?

Usually not on its own. Head-up freestyle, a sinking kick and a breathing pattern that runs a swimmer out of air are technique faults, and unsupervised repetition tends to groove them deeper rather than correct them. A progressive lesson program with an instructor correcting stroke under mild fatigue closes those gaps far faster, and it also builds the coachability gate at the same time, because a group class is a lower-stakes version of the same shared-lane environment.

My child loves swimming but might not want swim team. How do I tell?

Loving the water and wanting to swim laps for an hour are different preferences, and the gap between them is the most common false positive in readiness. The reliable test is to watch a real practice together for about thirty minutes; most programs allow it. Also check that your child can say in their own words what they want out of the team and that they know what they are agreeing to, including how many practices a week and that a meet is a long day containing a very short swim.

What should I ask a swim coach before tryouts?

Ask which strokes and what distance the tryout actually requires, whether there is a pre-team or developmental group (many programs have one and do not advertise it), how many practices a week the novice group trains and whether partial attendance is allowed, what a season costs all in including dues, suit, registration, meet fees and travel, whether you can watch a practice first, and what the coach's policy is on breath-holding and underwater work.

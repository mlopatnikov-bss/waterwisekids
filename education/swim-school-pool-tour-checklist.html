Swim School Pool Tour: Water Temperature, Air and Depth Checks

You compared the prices, read the reviews, asked about the ratio — and then you walked into the building and the whole thing came down to a smell, a number nobody could give you, and a viewing window you could not see through. The pool itself decides more of your child’s lesson than the brochure does, and almost all of it is visible in ten minutes. Here is what to check while you are standing there, with a free printable tour card for the visit.

Most of the advice about choosing a swim school is about the programme: certifications, ratios, curriculum, make-up policy, price. All of it matters, and all of it can be settled from a laptop or a phone call. What cannot be settled that way is the pool, and the pool is where your child will actually spend the thirty minutes.

Two physical features shape a young child’s experience more than anything else in the building: how warm the water is, and how deep it is where they will be standing. A child who is cold, or out of their depth, spends the lesson managing that instead of learning — and half an hour is short enough already. Everything else on this page is a variation on those two, plus the mundane logistics that quietly decide whether a family is still attending three months later.

🌡️ What water temperature should a lesson pool be?

Warmer than most adults expect, and warmer the younger the child. Three organisations publish guidance and they overlap where it counts. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends 86–90°F for young children learning to swim. The American Red Cross recommends 84–88°F for instructional programmes with young children. The YMCA recommends 86–88°F for learn-to-swim programmes. Broken down by age, the AAP guidance runs 86–92°F for ages 0–3 with 90°F ideal, 86–90°F for ages 3–6, 84–88°F for ages 6–10, 82–86°F for ages 10–12, and 78–82°F becoming acceptable for teens. These are recommendations, not a legal standard.

Those are the guidance ranges. What pools actually operate at is a separate number, and confusing the two leads parents to hear a perfectly honest answer as a bad one. Purpose-built teaching pools commonly run roughly 86–92°F. Rented host pools — a school buying time in somebody else’s facility — often sit cooler at roughly 80–84°F, because the water was set for lap swimmers. Competitive pools run 78–82°F. A school that tells you 83°F is not being careless; it is telling you the water belongs to someone else. That is worth knowing before you enrol a three-year-old rather than after.

So ask three questions rather than one. What is the pool set to? Is that held year round, or does it swing with the season? And is the teaching pool separate from the lap pool, because shared water usually means a compromise temperature and the compromise is rarely made in the beginner’s favour. A programme that is proud of its answer will give you the number without checking notes. Some publish the water specification as a feature.

Warmer is not automatically better, either. Water above 92°F becomes uncomfortable for an active swimmer, and above 90°F creates an environment where certain bacteria and fungi thrive. The useful band for a small child who is learning sits in the mid-to-high eighties. Our full guide to pool temperature for swim lessons covers the research behind those ranges, and whether a baby needs a wetsuit covers what to do when the water is colder than you would like.

🫁 What does the air on the pool deck tell you?

Stand on the deck before you look at anything and take three normal breaths. A well-ventilated indoor pool smells only faintly of chlorine, even when it is busy. A strong, stinging odour is the opposite of a good sign: that smell is chloramines, the compounds formed when chlorine reacts with sweat, urine, sunscreen and body oils, and it points at a ventilation or chemistry problem rather than at a clean pool.

Then check your own eyes after five minutes. Watering or stinging eyes on a short tour tells you exactly what a thirty-minute lesson twice a week is going to be like. Notice whether the air feels heavy or humid, because chloramine gas collects at the water surface — which is precisely where a small child’s face is.

Ask what the ventilation and treatment set-up is. Useful answers mention high air-turnover HVAC, UV secondary disinfection or regenerative media filtration; a blank look is also an answer. Ask how often water chemistry is tested, too. A school on top of it will tell you the cadence without looking it up.

This is the one item on the tour where a bad answer should change the decision rather than start a negotiation, and it is worth saying so out loud if anyone in the family has asthma. The CDC links high chloramine exposure to eye irritation, respiratory symptoms and aggravated asthma. An indoor pool is not off the table — a well-ventilated one is usually fine, and in lightning-prone or very hot regions it is the safer option overall — but a poorly ventilated one is a real problem for a child with reactive airways. Our guide to indoor versus outdoor swim lessons works through that trade-off in detail, and the pool water quality checklist covers what is happening in the water itself.

📏 How deep is the water where your child will actually stand?

Write your child’s standing height down before you go. Every depth question on a tour is really a question about your child, and “three feet six” means nothing until you know whether it lands at their chest or their chin.

Ask where your child’s class actually swims, then look at that spot rather than at the pool in general. A shallow teaching depth lets the instructor stand, which matters more than it sounds: an instructor who can stand can support a nervous beginner with their hands rather than with their whole body. And a child who can put their feet down and be safe will spend the lesson trying things. A child who cannot will spend it holding on.

Watch how quickly the floor drops away. Lap and municipal pools often reach standard depth fast, which can intimidate a new swimmer and forces more reliance on the instructor’s hold. Then ask what happens at the next level up, because deep-water skills genuinely do need deep water, and a school with nowhere to progress to will hit a ceiling before your child does. Our guide to deep-water readiness covers what that transition should look like.

One thing not to be sold on: size. A longer pool is not a better learning pool. For a beginner, warm water, a depth they can stand in and an instructor within reach do more than length ever will — which is the argument in our piece on what “Olympic-sized pool” actually means. A large competition pool is a feature for swim teams, not for a four-year-old learning to float.

🪜 How do children get in and out of the pool?

Look at the entry. Steps, a ramp or a zero-depth beach entry suit beginners; a vertical ladder into open water is a hurdle placed before the lesson has even started. Check that there are handrails and a graspable edge all the way round, because a child who can reach the wall and hold it has somewhere to go when something goes wrong.

Then look down. The deck surface under bare wet feet should be textured rather than glossy tile with standing water on it — and a useful shortcut is to watch where the staff walk and how they walk there. Loose kickboards, hoses, cones and bags in the walking route are a small thing that says something larger about how the space is run.

Two questions belong here. Ask about the main drain covers: they should be present, intact and anti-entrapment rated, and it is a quick, fair thing to ask. Our pool drain safety checklist explains why. And ask who is watching the water besides the instructor — whether there is a dedicated lifeguard during lessons, and where they stand.

👀 Can you see your child from where you will be sitting?

Sit down where parents actually sit, and look for the teaching area. Do this before you decide anything else about the room, because it is the only test that cannot be answered from the lobby. Can you see your child’s face, or only the back of a group? Glass viewing walls, deck seating and a corridor window are three very different experiences.

Check for glare, fog and reflections while you are there. A viewing window that mists over on a busy evening is a window you cannot use. Listen, too: if you cannot hear the instructor from the gallery, neither can a nervous child at the far end of the lane.

Being able to see your own child in the water is the whole basis of parent supervision, so this is a safety item as well as a comfort one. It is worth knowing on day one whether the room lets you do that, or asks you to trust the room instead.

🧳 What does the changing and drying routine involve?

This is the least glamorous section on the page and the one most likely to end an enrolment. Look for a family or private changing space rather than a shared locker room you will be navigating with a wet four-year-old and a bag. Check that the changing area is warm and draught-free — a cold changing room undoes a good part of what a warm pool bought you, in the ten minutes that matter most.

Find the showers. A quick rinse before swimming removes the sweat and oils that create chloramines in the first place, so a school that encourages it is helping its own air quality as well as everyone’s eyes.

Then count the walk from the car, in minutes, imagining February and a wet child. Check stroller access, check where a sibling is meant to sit, and ask what else runs in the building during your slot — rented and shared pools can mean a water-aerobics class beside your child’s lesson, or a swim meet that eats the car park. Our guides to the three swim school facility models and to strip-mall swim schools cover how these logistics differ by building type.

⏱️ What should you watch in the ten minutes before your tour ends?

Some answers should slow you down. “It’s nice and warm” is a feeling offered where a number was asked for; ask once more, and if there is still no number, assume nobody is watching it. “That strong chlorine smell means it’s clean” is the opposite of true, and a programme that believes it is not managing its air. “You can’t see the pool, but we’ll send photos” is a nice extra rather than a substitute for a sightline. “Depth doesn’t really matter, the instructor holds them” is fine for a first lesson and worrying as a philosophy. And any answer that arrives with irritation is data: you are asking the questions a good programme wants asked.

One bad answer is not a disqualification, though. Rented pools trade water temperature for being close to where families live, and for many families that is the right trade. What you are listening for is whether the school knows its own weak point and has a plan for it, or has never thought about it.

Keep the two questions separate as you leave. The building predicts comfort and logistics; the instructor, the ratio and the curriculum predict whether your child learns. A strong instructor in a rented host pool beats a weak one behind a glass viewing wall every single week — so pair this tour with our questions to ask your child’s swim instructor and the swim lesson quality checklist, and do not let a smart lobby answer a question about teaching.

One last thing worth saying plainly, because it sits underneath all of this: no programme and no number of lessons makes a child drown-proof. Lessons change what a child can do; they do not change the supervision that child needs. Swim lessons are one layer of protection alongside four-sided pool fencing, constant undistracted supervision within arm’s reach of a young or non-swimming child, properly fitted life jackets in open water, and an adult trained in CPR. And do not let the search for a perfect pool delay the first lesson — time out of the water is the one cost this checklist cannot help you recover.

Frequently Asked Questions

What temperature should a swim lesson pool be?

Warmer than most people expect, and warmer the younger the child. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends 86–90°F for young children’s swim lessons, the American Red Cross recommends 84–88°F for instructional programmes with young children, and the YMCA recommends 86–88°F for learn-to-swim. Broken down by age, the AAP guidance runs 86–92°F for ages 0–3 with 90°F ideal, 86–90°F for ages 3–6, 84–88°F for ages 6–10 with 86–88°F preferable for structured lessons, 82–86°F for ages 10–12, and 78–82°F becoming acceptable for teens and particularly for competitive swimmers. These are guidance ranges rather than a legal standard. Warmer is not automatically better either: water above 92°F becomes uncomfortable for an active swimmer, and above 90°F creates an environment where certain bacteria and fungi thrive.

Which air-quality problems show up on a pool deck tour?

Two, and you can detect both with no equipment at all. The first is chloramine buildup. A strong, stinging chlorine odour and eyes that water within a few minutes are the signal, and they mean weak ventilation or a chemistry problem rather than a clean pool — chloramines form when chlorine reacts with sweat, urine, sunscreen and body oils, and the gas collects at the water surface, which is exactly where a small child’s face is. A well-ventilated pool smells only faintly of chlorine even when it is busy. The second is humidity: air that feels heavy on the deck usually means the ventilation is not keeping up, and it is worth asking what the set-up is. Useful answers mention high air-turnover HVAC, UV secondary disinfection or regenerative media filtration. The CDC links high chloramine exposure to eye irritation, respiratory symptoms and aggravated asthma, so this is the item worth pressing on if anyone in the family has asthma.

How deep should the water be for a beginner swim lesson?

Shallow enough that the instructor can stand, and shallow enough that your child can put their feet down. That second part is why you should write your child’s standing height down before the tour — a stated depth means nothing until you know where it lands on them. Chest-deep water lets a child try things; chin-deep water means they spend the lesson holding on and the instructor spends it supporting them. Ask specifically where your child’s class swims rather than looking at the pool in general, and watch how quickly the floor drops away: lap and municipal pools often reach standard depth fast, which can intimidate a new swimmer and forces more reliance on the instructor’s hold. Then ask what happens at the next level, because deep-water skills do need deep water and a school with nowhere to progress to will hit a ceiling before your child does.

Which pool features should a parent verify in person before enrolling?

Seven, and none of them takes more than a minute. The water temperature as a number rather than an adjective, and whether it is held year round. The air on the deck, judged by three normal breaths and by whether your eyes sting after five minutes. The depth in the teaching area, measured against your own child. The entry design, because steps, a ramp or a zero-depth beach entry suit a beginner in a way a vertical ladder does not. The sightline from the seat parents actually sit in, tested by sitting in it. The changing, drying and parking route, counted in minutes as it would be in February with a wet child. And who is watching the water besides the instructor. Ask the questions out loud, including the awkward ones — a school that welcomes them is telling you something no brochure can.

Does a purpose-built pool teach better than a rented one?

No. The building predicts comfort and logistics; it does not predict teaching. Purpose-built lesson pools are designed for young learners and commonly operate at roughly 86–92°F with a shallow teaching depth and large viewing windows, while rented host pools often sit cooler at roughly 80–84°F because the water was set for somebody else, and competitive pools at 78–82°F. That is a real difference and worth knowing before you enrol a three-year-old. But a strong instructor in a rented host pool beats a weak one behind a glass viewing wall every single week, and the two things are independent, which is why you check both separately. What you are really listening for on a tour is whether the school knows its own weak point and has a plan for it — a warmer slot, a shorter session, a thermal top — or has never thought about it at all.

What if the pool is colder than the recommended range?

Raise it, and listen to the answer rather than to the temperature. A five-year-old learning in 80°F water when the AAP range for that age is 86–90°F is a legitimate concern and a fair thing to bring up, and a programme with a good answer will have heard the question before. The usual remedies are a warmer time slot, a shorter session, a wetsuit or thermal rash top, or a different pool within the same programme. Children lose heat in water far faster than adults do — less insulating fat, more surface area for their size, and water that carries heat away from the body roughly twenty-five times faster than air — so cold water shortens the useful part of a lesson even when nobody is complaining. And do not let the search for a perfect pool delay the first lesson: enrol somewhere reasonable now and keep looking if you need to, because time out of the water is the one cost you cannot recover.

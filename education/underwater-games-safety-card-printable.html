Underwater Games Safety Card

Children invent pool games faster than any list can name them, so a list of banned games is out of date by the second hour of the party. This card sorts a game you have never seen before. Three ingredients make an underwater game dangerous; one ingredient means set a limit, two or more means stop the game. The hazard here is not the weak swimmer — it is the confident one, going quietly still on the bottom while everyone watching the surface sees nothing at all.

🧪 The Three Ingredients — Score Any Game in Ten Seconds

  • Ingredient 1 — the game rewards staying under longer or going farther. Anything scored by duration or distance: who lasts longest, how many widths, one more than last time, beat your record. The reward is the danger; a child chasing a number pushes past the urge to breathe.
  • Ingredient 2 — the game has a breathing ritual before it starts. Big breaths, deep breaths, “breathe up”, panting, or any coaching about how to prepare. Deliberate over-breathing lowers carbon dioxide, and carbon dioxide is what triggers the urge to breathe — remove it and a swimmer can lose consciousness before their body ever tells them to surface.
  • Ingredient 3 — the game makes a still child on the bottom look normal. Hide-and-seek underwater, sinking-toy hunts, statues, dead man’s float, pretending to be a shark. This is the ingredient adults never count, and it is the worst one: it disables the rescue, because nobody looks twice at the thing the game told them to expect.
  • Score it: one ingredient → allow it with a stated limit. Say the limit out loud, to the children, before play starts — one trip, one object, surface between every attempt, no counting.
  • Two or more ingredients → the game is over. Not “be careful”, not “only the big kids”. Name a replacement game in the same breath, because a banned game with nothing to replace it comes back in ten minutes.
Why sort by ingredient rather than by game name. A banned-games list only works on games somebody has already thought of, and children are extremely good at inventing new ones — usually by combining two old ones. The ingredients survive the invention. If a game your child made up scores two, it is as dangerous as the version with a name.

🔴 Red — Stop These, At Every Age and Every Skill Level

  • Breath-holding contests — who can stay under longest, in or out of the water. Ingredients 1 and 2. The strongest swimmer usually wins, which is exactly why the strongest swimmer is the one at risk.
  • Underwater distance races and “how many widths” — including repeated no-breath laps done as training. Ingredients 1 and 2, with fatigue on top.
  • Any game with a deliberate breathe-up — even a single trip, even in the shallow end. Hyperventilating is the specific mechanism, not the depth and not the distance.
  • Dunking and holding a child under, however playful. It teaches that another person controls your next breath, and it can put water in the airway in seconds.
  • Underwater hide-and-seek and any game where a child is supposed to stay hidden and motionless below the surface. Ingredients 1 and 3 together — the version that has killed children whose friends were an arm’s length away.
  • Weighted objects held or carried underwater — bricks, dive bricks, anything that makes surfacing slower or harder.
This is not a house rule — it is the position of the organisations that run American aquatics. The American Red Cross, USA Swimming and the YMCA of the USA issued a joint statement calling on aquatic programmes and facilities to prevent hyperventilation and extended breath-holding. It was first issued in 2015 and updated in 2022. The condition it describes is hypoxic blackout; the older phrase “shallow water blackout” is still widely used but is less accurate, because it happens in water of any depth.

🟡 Yellow — Fine With a Limit You Say Out Loud

  • Retrieving a sinking dive toy — one object per trip, surface after each one, nobody counting or timing. Remove the scoring and Ingredient 1 goes with it.
  • Swimming one width underwater — once, then a full rest at the surface. Never back-to-back, and never with an adult calling the number.
  • Somersaults, handstands and swimming through a hoop or legs — short, one at a time, in water where the child can stand or reach the side.
  • Opening eyes, blowing bubbles and bobbing — the skills a lesson actually builds. Keep them untimed and they stay green.
  • Diving-toy hunts with several toys down at once — only with one child down at a time and an adult whose whole job is that child. Otherwise the pattern reads as Ingredient 3.
Say the limit to the children, not to the other adults. “One toy, then up” works. “Be careful down there” does not — it names no behaviour, so nothing changes. A limit a child can repeat back is a limit they will police on each other for the rest of the afternoon.

👂 Six Phrases From the Deck That Mean Stop Now

  • “Take a big breath first.” The single most dangerous sentence said at a pool, and it is almost always said kindly, by an adult who is helping.
  • “Let’s see who can stay under longest.” Ingredient 1, stated aloud.
  • “One more than last time.” A record is being kept, which means the limit is now a moving target.
  • “Count for me.” A stopwatch, a phone timer or a child counting out loud all do the same thing to the game.
  • “He’s just hiding.” Ingredient 3 in five words. Check anyway, every time, and do not apologise for it.
  • “She’s a really strong swimmer.” Offered as a reason to relax, it is the opposite. Strong swimmers are over-represented in these deaths precisely because they are trusted to stay under.

👁️ The Bottom Sweep — Why the Surface Scan Misses This One

  • Everything you have been taught about spotting drowning is a surface skill — the vertical body, the head tipped back, the arms pressing down, the silence. All of it happens at the surface.
  • A hypoxic blackout does not reach the surface. The swimmer is doing something they have done a hundred times, then simply stops. No thrash, no call, no splash. They settle quietly to the bottom.
  • So add one movement to every scan: count heads, then sweep the bottom. Eyes along the floor of the pool, end to end, every single pass. It takes about two seconds and it is the only thing that finds this.
  • Any child on the bottom gets checked, every time, with no benefit of the doubt. The cost of being wrong is one embarrassed child. The cost of assuming is not recoverable.
  • A lifeguard on duty does not remove this job from you. Guards scan a whole pool; you scan your child. Both of you can miss a still body that the game explained away.
This is the gap the usual water-watcher habit leaves open. A named watcher with no phone is the right foundation and it is still not enough here, because the watcher is scanning for a struggle that is never going to happen. The bottom sweep is the extra movement. Teach it to whoever takes the next watcher shift.

🚨 If a Child Is Found Still Underwater

  • Get them out and face-up immediately. Shout for a second adult as you go in — do not leave to find one.
  • Call 911, or have a named person call while you work. Point at one adult and say their name; “someone call 911” is how nobody calls.
  • If they are not breathing normally, start CPR and keep going until they respond or help takes over. Rescue breaths matter in drowning — the problem started as a lack of oxygen.
  • Any child who surfaces dizzy, tingling, confused, or who blacked out even briefly, is seen by a doctor that day — even if they seem completely fine five minutes later.
  • Then end swimming for the day, for everyone. The adults will not be scanning properly afterwards, and the children will not be following limits.
The rule that survives the party. Nobody holds their breath as a contest, and nobody prepares to go under with big breaths — ever, at any pool, with any adult, at any age. Say it as one sentence, say it to every child in the water, and say it again to the next family your child swims with. This rule only works if it travels.

The Skill That Actually Protects a Child Is Not Breath-Holding

Breath-hold endurance is the most misread signal at any pool — it looks like competence and it is the trait most associated with these deaths. What actually protects a child is a taught sequence: getting to the surface, rolling to the back, floating, breathing, and reaching an edge. That is built in lessons, not in the deep end on a dare.

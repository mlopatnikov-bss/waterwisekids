Heat Exhaustion vs. Heatstroke at the Pool: Warning Signs for Parents

A day at the pool feels like the perfect way to beat the heat — but the cool water can hide just how hot your child is actually getting. Knowing the difference between heat exhaustion and heatstroke, and acting fast, can keep a fun afternoon from turning into an emergency.

Quick Answer: Children can overheat even at the pool. Heat exhaustion is the warning stage — heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, weakness, headache, nausea, and dizziness — and is treated by moving to a cool place, resting, and sipping water. Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency — very high body temperature, hot skin, confusion, slurred speech, or fainting — and requires calling 911 and aggressive cooling right away. Prevent both by offering water often, taking shade breaks, and avoiding peak midday heat.

Why do kids overheat even at the pool?

It seems backwards: how can a child overheat while splashing in cool water? But the pool environment hides several heat traps. The sun reflects off the water and the deck, hot concrete can radiate intense heat, and the cooling sensation of the water can mask a rising core temperature. Kids run, jump, and play hard, generating body heat, then dry off in direct sun between swims.

According to the CDC, heat-related illness is entirely preventable, and infants and young children are among the groups at highest risk because they heat up faster and depend on adults to hydrate and cool them. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises frequent fluids, shade breaks, and light clothing on hot days, and treats a child with hot skin and altered behavior as a medical emergency.

Children are also more vulnerable to heat than adults. They produce more heat relative to their body size, sweat less efficiently, and often don't recognize or report early symptoms because they are having too much fun to stop. Younger children and babies are at the highest risk. That is why parents need to watch for heat illness even on a "refreshing" pool day.

104°FAccording to the CDC, a body temperature of around 104 degrees Fahrenheit (40 degrees Celsius) or higher, combined with confusion or altered behavior, signals heatstroke — a medical emergency that requires calling 911 immediately.

What are the signs of heat exhaustion in children?

Heat exhaustion is the body's warning that it is struggling to cool itself. It is serious, but if you catch it here and act, you can usually prevent it from progressing. Watch for:

Heavy sweating and skin that feels cool, pale, and clammy.

Tiredness, weakness, or crankiness — your normally energetic child suddenly wants to lie down or seems "off."

Headache, dizziness, or lightheadedness.

Nausea, stomachache, or vomiting.

A fast, weak pulse and muscle cramps.

A child with heat exhaustion is usually still alert and able to talk to you, even if they feel miserable. That alertness is an important clue that it has not yet become heatstroke.

What are the signs of heatstroke, and when is it an emergency?

Heatstroke happens when the body's cooling system is overwhelmed and the core temperature climbs dangerously high. It can damage the brain and organs within minutes and is a true 911 emergency. The warning signs are different from heat exhaustion in critical ways:

Very high body temperature (around 104°F / 40°C or higher).

Skin that is hot — it may be dry or still sweaty, but it feels hot to the touch.

Confusion, agitation, slurred speech, or strange behavior. This change in mental state is the single most important red flag.

A fast, strong pulse, rapid breathing, a throbbing headache.

Seizures or loss of consciousness.

If you ever see confusion or altered behavior alongside overheating, do not wait to see if it improves. Treat it as heatstroke.

What should you do if a child has heat exhaustion?

For heat exhaustion: Move your child to a cool, shaded, or air-conditioned spot. Have them lie down and remove extra clothing. Give cool water to sip if they are alert and not vomiting. Apply cool, wet cloths to the skin, or have them sit in cool (not ice-cold) water. They should start to feel better within about 30 minutes. If they don't improve, get worse, or can't keep fluids down, seek medical care.

For heatstroke: Call 911 immediately. While you wait, move the child to a cool place and cool them as fast as you can — remove outer clothing, place cool wet cloths or ice packs on the neck, armpits, and groin, fan them, and if safe, immerse them in cool water. Do not give fluids to a child who is confused or not fully awake, because of the choking risk. Rapid cooling in the first minutes is what saves lives.

How do you prevent heat illness on hot pool days?

Most heat illness is preventable with a few simple habits:

Hydrate early and often. Offer water before kids feel thirsty — thirst is already a sign of mild dehydration. Our guide to nutrition and hydration for young swimmers has more.

Build in shade and rest breaks. Set a timer to call everyone out for a drink and a few minutes in the shade, even when they protest.

Avoid peak heat. When possible, swim in the morning or late afternoon rather than the hottest midday hours.

Dress smart. Light-colored, loose clothing or a sun shirt, a wide-brimmed hat for deck time, and sunscreen reduce heat load. See our sun safety at the pool guide.

Mind the hot deck. Scorching concrete can burn little feet and add to heat stress — our pool deck safety tips help.

Never leave a child in a parked car, not even for a minute. Car interiors heat to deadly temperatures astonishingly fast.

Let kids acclimate. Bodies adjust to heat over several days, so ease into long, hot outdoor days early in the season.

The bottom line for parents

The pool is one of summer's great joys, but cool water can disguise a child's rising temperature. Learn the two-stage picture: heat exhaustion brings sweating, weakness, and clammy skin and is handled with cooling and rest, while heatstroke brings a very high temperature, hot skin, and confusion and demands a 911 call and immediate cooling. The change in a child's mental state is your most important warning sign. Stay ahead of it with frequent water, shade breaks, and smart timing, and you'll keep your family's pool days as safe as they are fun. For the decision that comes before any of this — whether the heat index makes today an afternoon to shorten, move indoors or skip — see our pool weather rules guide and its printable go/no-go card. This article is general education and not a substitute for medical advice — when in doubt, call your pediatrician or seek care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a child get heat exhaustion at the pool?

Yes. Splashing in cool water can mask how hot a child's body is becoming. Sun, high temperatures, hot pool decks, and physical activity can all push a child toward overheating, even though they feel refreshed in the water. Children overheat faster than adults, so the pool is not a guaranteed protection against heat illness.

What is the difference between heat exhaustion and heatstroke?

Heat exhaustion is the warning stage, with heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, weakness, headache, nausea, and dizziness. Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency where the body can no longer cool itself, with a very high temperature, hot skin, confusion, slurred speech, or loss of consciousness. Heatstroke requires calling 911 immediately.

What should I do if my child has heat exhaustion?

Move them to a cool, shaded, or air-conditioned spot, have them lie down, remove extra clothing, and give cool water to sip if they are alert. Apply cool wet cloths or have them rest in cool water. They should feel better within 30 minutes. If symptoms worsen or do not improve, seek medical care.

When is overheating a 911 emergency?

Call 911 immediately if a child has signs of heatstroke: confusion, agitation, slurred speech, a body temperature around 104 degrees Fahrenheit or higher, very hot skin, vomiting, seizures, or loss of consciousness. While waiting for help, move them to a cool place and cool them aggressively with water and fanning.

How can I prevent heat illness on a hot pool day?

Offer water frequently before kids feel thirsty, schedule shade and rest breaks, avoid the hottest hours of midday, use hats and light clothing, never leave a child in a parked car, and watch younger children closely. Acclimating gradually to the heat over several days also lowers the risk.

Water Safety Games That Teach Kids Life-Saving Skills

Try ten water-safety activities for kids, with dry-land practice and simple shallow-water games for children who are ready. Keep an adult close and skip contests.

Quick Answer: Good water safety games are noncompetitive and supervised: a supported back float, walking games in shallow water, "catch the wall" practice, practicing calm breathing on land, and deck games for life jackets and reach or throw, don't go. Skip breath-holding contests, underwater distance games, eyes-closed games and dunking. Keep an adult within arm's reach of young children and weak swimmers, and actively watching everyone else.

🎮 How can games help kids practice water safety?

Games let children rehearse habits such as asking permission before getting in, spotting the exit, and getting an adult when someone needs help. Many activities work at home or away from the water. Teach one habit at a time, then ask the child to explain it back to you.

📊 Free curriculum: The American Red Cross publishes Longfellow's WHALE Tales, a free water safety program for children with videos, activity sheets and lesson plans. Its lessons include "Swim as a Pair with a Lifeguard There", "Don't Just Pack It, Wear Your Life Jacket" and "Reach or Throw, Don't Go", and several games below rehearse those same rules.

⭐ What Games Build Floating Skills?

Floating Stars and Starfish and Stand practice a relaxed back float in shallow water, with an adult within arm's reach and no timing or contest.

1. Floating Stars

In water where the child can stand, an adult stays within arm's reach and offers a hand under the shoulders while the child leans back into a back float with arms and legs wide like a star. The child stands up whenever they want. There is no clock and no winner: the goal is a calm face-up float, repeated a few times.

Safety skill built: A relaxed face-up float, which lets a swimmer rest and breathe.

2. Starfish and Stand

Same setup, same shallow water. The adult says "starfish" and the child floats on their back; the adult says "stand" and the child tucks their knees and puts their feet down. Practicing the stand-up matters as much as the float, because it shows the child how to get back to their feet.

Safety skill built: Recovering from a back float to standing.

🌊 What Shallow-Water Games Build Calm Movement?

Waiter Walk and Red Light, Green Light keep feet on the bottom in shallow water and reward slow, controlled movement over speed. Treading water and deep-water skills belong in swim lessons with an instructor, not in a backyard contest.

3. Waiter Walk

In waist-deep water or shallower, each child walks from one side to the other balancing a ping pong ball on a plastic plate. Splashy, rushed steps tip the ball; smooth steps keep it. Nobody is eliminated: children simply try again.

Safety skill built: Balance and calm, deliberate movement in water.

4. Red Light, Green Light

Children walk through shallow water toward the adult on "green light" and freeze on "red light". Add "yellow light" for slow-motion walking. It is a playful way to rehearse stopping immediately on an adult's signal.

Safety skill built: Listening for and obeying a stop signal in the water.

🏊 What Games Build Getting-to-Safety Skills?

Catch the Wall and Find the Exit teach children to locate and reach the nearest wall, steps or ladder, with eyes open and in water where they can stand.

5. Catch the Wall

Children spread out in water where they can stand. On a clap or the word "wall", everyone walks or swims to the nearest wall and holds on. Repeat from different spots. It is not a race: the adult praises choosing the closest wall and holding on with both hands.

Safety skill built: Knowing where the nearest edge is and getting to it.

6. Find the Exit

Play this on the deck before anyone gets in, then again in shallow water. The adult names a spot ("you are by the slide") and children point to the nearest steps, ladder or wall and say how they would get there. Eyes stay open the whole time.

Safety skill built: Looking around and planning a way out before playing.

What Games Teach Calm Breathing?

Pinwheel Breathing is a dry-land activity, and Thumbs Up, Thumbs Down is a deck game about safe choices. Breath-holding contests and underwater distance games are never part of play.

7. Pinwheel Breathing

On dry land, ask children to blow gently to turn a pinwheel, then breathe normally. Use this as a short break between activities. Skip any timing or competition.

Safety skill built: Practicing a calm, gentle exhale on land.

8. Thumbs Up, Thumbs Down

On the deck or indoors, the adult describes a choice and children show thumbs up or thumbs down: asking a grown-up before getting in, swimming with a buddy near a lifeguard, seeing who can hold their breath longest, pushing a friend under. Talk through each answer. Breath-holding and dunking games are always thumbs down.

Safety skill built: Recognizing safe and unsafe choices before getting wet.

What Games Work for Mixed Ages and Skill Levels?

Life Jacket Check and Reach or Throw, Don't Go are dry-land games, so swimmers and non-swimmers of any age can play together.

9. Life Jacket Check

On the deck, children pick a U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket, buckle every strap, and ask a partner to check that it is snug. An adult does the final check. Play for a careful fit, not for speed. Our life jacket guide explains sizing.

Safety skill built: Choosing and fastening a life jacket correctly.

10. Reach or Throw, Don't Go

On grass or the deck, well back from the edge, one child stands on a towel "island". Partners lie flat and reach with a pool noodle, or toss a ring buoy or foam ball toward a chalk target, while calling for an adult. Everyone says the rule out loud: reach or throw, don't go. No one enters the water. See our reach-and-throw guide.

Safety skill built: Getting help and helping from land without entering the water.

How Do You Keep Pool Games Safe?

Keep an adult as a dedicated water watcher throughout, set clear no-dunking and no-breath-holding rules, and teach a STOP signal that sends everyone to the wall. The American Academy of Pediatrics emphasizes constant, close supervision whenever children are in the water. Even fun pool games require consistent safety practices. An adult must remain as the designated water watcher throughout any pool game involving children who are not fully independent swimmers. Pool games often involve splashing, grabbing, and unexpected movements that can cause distress even in confident swimmers. A game that appears to be all laughter can transition to a real struggling moment very quickly.

The Red Cross WHALE Tales activity cards warn against breath-holding games and contests.

Establish clear rules before any game begins: no dunking or holding underwater, no breath-holding contests, no jumping on other players, and a clear signal (raised fist, a specific word like "STOP") that ends all game activity immediately and requires everyone to the nearest wall. Practice the STOP signal before playing so it's understood and automatic.

How Do You Use These Games for Drowning Prevention Week?

Pair one skill game with one talking point per session, and anchor the week to a recognized date — May is National Water Safety Month and July 25 is World Drowning Prevention Day. Both give swim schools, camps, and classrooms a natural hook for a themed week of drowning prevention activities without inventing a curriculum from scratch.

The structure that works best is simple: each session opens with a two-minute talking point, moves into a game that rehearses that exact skill, and closes with a question that makes the child say the rule out loud. A five-day drowning prevention week plan built entirely from the games above looks like this:

  • Day 1 — Float to rest. Talking point: tell an adult and take a break as soon as you feel tired. Game: Floating Stars.
  • Day 2 — Move calmly. Talking point: slow and steady beats splashing. Game: Waiter Walk.
  • Day 3 — Get to the wall. Talking point: always know which way the nearest edge is. Game: Catch the Wall.
  • Day 4 — Breathe and stay calm. Talking point: breathe out gently, and holding your breath is never a game. Game: Pinwheel Breathing and Thumbs Up, Thumbs Down.
  • Day 5 — Reach, throw, don't go. Talking point: children never swim out to a struggling person. Game: Reach or Throw, Don't Go, played on dry land.

For a classroom rather than a pool, our water safety activities for schools guide covers the same week in a K–5 format with pledges, coloring pages, and assembly plans.

Where can you get free drowning prevention materials?

You do not need to pay for a curriculum. Every organization below publishes drowning prevention materials — handouts, posters, lesson outlines, and water safety pledges — that are free to download and reproduce for community and classroom use:

  • CDC Drowning Prevention — national data and plain-language prevention fact sheets.
  • American Red Cross Water Safety — downloadable handouts and the Circle of Drowning Prevention.
  • National Drowning Prevention Alliance — campaign toolkits and layers-of-protection graphics.
  • USA Swimming Foundation — water competency resources and lesson-access programs.

We also publish a free water safety resource library and printable checklists, and our U.S. child drowning statistics report gives you the current national numbers to open any drowning prevention presentation with. Frequently Asked Questions

What are good drowning prevention activities for kids?

The most effective drowning prevention activities rehearse a survival skill rather than just talking about it. A supported back float teaches a child to rest and breathe, “catch the wall” practice builds the habit of getting to the nearest edge, a life jacket fit check builds a habit for boats and open water, and dry-land reach-or-throw games teach children to get help without entering the water. Pair each activity with one spoken rule so the child can repeat it back.

What pool games teach water safety skills?

Games like 'Floating Stars' (a supported back float), 'Waiter Walk' (calm movement in shallow water), 'Catch the Wall' (getting to the nearest edge) and 'Pinwheel Breathing' (practicing calm breathing on land) build water safety skills while children play. Keep games noncompetitive, in water where children can stand, with an adult within arm's reach of young children and weak swimmers. Avoid breath-holding contests, underwater distance games and eyes-closed games.

Can pool games replace swim lessons for teaching water safety?

No. Pool games are an excellent supplement to structured swim lessons, not a replacement. Games build confidence, enjoyment, and reinforce skills learned in lessons. But they don't provide the systematic, progression-based instruction and safety monitoring of professional swim lessons. Children need both — structured skill development and positive play experiences in the water.

What water safety games are appropriate for toddlers?

For toddlers, games should focus on water comfort and basic safety concepts: 'Ring Around the Rosie' in the shallow end, blowing bubbles at the surface, gentle splashing games, and 'Floating on Mommy's Tummy' for a relaxed back float experience. Always keep toddlers within arm's reach during water play.

How can I use games to help a child who is afraid of water?

For water-anxious children, start with games that keep the face out of the water: water squirt toys in shallow water, splashing games at the pool steps, bubble blowing from the edge. Add new games only as comfort grows, and let the child set the pace. The playful context reduces anxiety better than direct instruction because the focus is on fun rather than skill performance. Never force an anxious child into deeper water through game rules.

What should adults do while children play water safety games?

An adult must be actively supervising — watching the children, not participating in conversations or using a phone. Even 'safe' pool games can involve moments of distress if a child slips, panics, or ends up in deeper water than intended. A designated 'water watcher' should be maintaining visual contact with all children in the water at all times, and an adult should stay within arm's reach of infants, toddlers and weak swimmers.

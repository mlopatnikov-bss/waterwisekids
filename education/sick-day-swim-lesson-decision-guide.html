Swim or Skip? A Sick-Day Decision Guide for Swim Lessons

The lesson is at four o’clock and your child woke up with a cough. Most sick-day calls are made in about ninety seconds, from memory, at a moment when nobody is thinking clearly. This free printable card puts the symptom-by-symptom answer on one page — what means skip, what is usually fine, and how long to wait before going back in.

🌡️ When can a child swim with a cold, and when should they stay home?

A mild cold with no fever is usually fine; a fever, diarrhea, vomiting, pink eye or an open wound all mean staying out. The reason this call feels hard at seven in the morning is that it is really three separate questions wearing one coat, and they have to be asked in order.

The first is contagion: is anything here going to travel through the water to other children? The second is water makes it worse: is this a condition that swimming itself aggravates, like swimmer’s ear or an open wound? Only if both answers are no does the third question matter — how does my child actually feel? Most parents start at question three, which is why a runny-nosed but cheerful child sometimes goes to a lesson they should have missed, and a tired child with nothing contagious sometimes stays home unnecessarily. Our full guide to whether your child can swim when sick works through every symptom in detail; this page turns it into a card you can hold.

🛑 Which symptoms mean skipping swim lessons entirely?

Fever, diarrhea, vomiting, pink eye, swimmer’s ear, a ruptured eardrum, and contagious skin infections or open wounds. These are not judgement calls, and none of them improve because the child says they feel ready.

A fever is the clearest of them. It signals that the body is fighting an infection and needs energy for recovery rather than exertion, and cool water can make a feverish child uncomfortable and may mask chills. Wait until the fever has been gone for a full day and your child is back to normal. Vomiting and stomach upset mean staying out until fully recovered. Pink eye is contagious and means keeping out until cleared, and open wounds, rashes and skin infections mean keeping out to protect your child and everybody else in the water. Children with asthma are a separate case worth planning ahead for — cold water and vigorous swimming can trigger symptoms when they are already congested, which our guide to swimming with asthma covers.

💧 Why is diarrhea the one absolute rule?

Because some germs survive chlorine, and one accident can sicken an entire pool. The CDC advises that anyone with diarrhea should not enter the water, and for certain diarrheal illnesses recommends waiting two weeks after the diarrhea stops before swimming.

Chlorine is not instant and it is not magic. Cryptosporidium in particular can survive in properly chlorinated pools long enough to spread illness to everyone swimming, which is why the response to a diarrheal accident is a long closure rather than a quick skim — the arithmetic behind that is in our explainer on why pools close after a fecal accident. This is the rule where keeping your child out protects other families as much as your own, the same shared-responsibility idea behind preventing recreational water illness and everyday public pool hygiene. For babies and toddlers still in swim nappies, the double-layer rule exists for exactly this reason, as our guide to swim diapers for baby swim class explains.

👂 Ears: which infections keep a child out, and for how long?

It depends on which ear problem you are dealing with, and the three common ones give three different answers. This is the question families ask most, and the reason the answers conflict online is that people are describing different conditions.

A middle ear infection with an intact eardrum is generally not made worse by swimming, because the eardrum keeps water out of the middle ear; many doctors allow swimming if the child feels well enough, but check first. Swimmer’s ear is an infection of the ear canal that water actively aggravates, so keep your child out until it heals, and prevent the next one with our swimmer’s ear prevention guide. Ear tubes or a ruptured eardrum are different again: water can pass through into the middle ear, though the American Academy of Otolaryngology’s updated guidance means most children with standard tubes can swim at the surface without ear plugs — diving and forceful submersion are the exception. Our guide to swimming with ear tubes and our guide to ear infections and swimming cover both in detail.

🩹 What about a cast, stitches, or a skin rash?

Ask the doctor who put it on, and do not assume a waterproof cover settles it. Traditional plaster and standard fiberglass casts must stay dry and cannot be submerged; some orthopedists use a special waterproof cast liner that is designed to get wet.

Covers exist for the first kind, but they are not automatically approved for swimming, and a leak turns a dry cast into a skin problem underneath it. A cast also changes buoyancy and limits how a child uses that arm, so supervision matters more than usual even in shallow water — our guide to swimming with a cast walks through the options. Rashes and open wounds are simpler: keep them out until healed, both because pool water can irritate a wound and because some skin infections spread in shared water.

🔄 How soon after being sick can a child go back in the water?

For mild colds, once they feel well. For fever, once it has been gone a full day. For diarrhea, follow CDC guidance, which for certain illnesses means two weeks after it stops. Then ease back in rather than picking up exactly where you left off.

Start with a shorter, lower-intensity session, make sure your child is well hydrated, and dry their ears thoroughly afterwards; good post-swim care habits speed recovery and prevent the next problem. A missed week or two does not undo what a child has learned, though several missed months does start to show — which is the case our guide to year-round versus seasonal lessons makes. Missing one lesson is a minor setback. Spreading an illness or worsening an infection is not.

📅 What sick-day policy should you confirm before you need it?

Ask how makeups work for illness before you enroll, because the notice rule is what decides whether a sick day costs you a lesson. Common policies cap makeups at two to four per session, require them to be used within 30 to 60 days, and demand 24 to 48 hours of advance notice.

That notice rule is the one that bites here: a fever at six in the morning rarely gives anybody twenty-four hours’ warning, so ask specifically whether a same-day illness cancellation still earns a makeup and whether a doctor’s note changes the answer. Ask how long the credit lasts and whether a sibling can use it. Policies vary widely, so use the agreement you are actually given rather than a general rule — our guide to make-up lesson policies and the swim school policy fine-print checklist cover the wording to use. It is worth settling in month one, because sick days are the single most common reason a family misses a lesson.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I decide if my child is too sick for swim lessons?

Work through three questions in order. First, is there any diarrhea, vomiting, fever, pink eye, or a contagious skin or eye infection? If yes, the answer is no swimming, and diarrhea is the one rule that never bends. Second, is the illness one that water itself makes worse, such as swimmer’s ear, a ruptured eardrum, or an open wound? If yes, keep them out until it has healed. Third, if neither applies and it is a mild cold with no fever, ask how your child actually feels. If they are energetic and breathing comfortably, a gentle session is usually fine. If they are tired or struggling to breathe through congestion, rest is the better choice. When any answer is unclear, keep them out and call your pediatrician.

Is a runny nose a reason to skip swim class?

Usually not, on its own. A mild cold with no fever is generally fine if your child feels well enough and is breathing comfortably. The deciding factor is not the nose, it is the child. If they are energetic and eager to swim, a gentle session is usually fine. If they are worn out, or congested to the point that breathing is hard work, rest is the better call. Watch for the things that change the answer entirely: a fever, an ear that hurts, or a cough that makes breathing difficult all mean staying out regardless of how mild the cold looked yesterday.

Can a child swim with a fever if they seem to feel fine?

No. A fever is a clear stop sign whatever the child’s mood suggests. It signals that the body is fighting an infection and needs energy for recovery rather than exertion, and cool water can make a feverish child uncomfortable and may mask chills. The usual guidance is to wait until the fever has been gone for a full day and your child is back to normal before returning to the pool. Feeling fine in a warm living room is not the same as sustaining effort in a pool, so use the temperature rather than the mood.

Which symptom has the strictest stay-out rule?

Diarrhea, by a wide margin. It is the one rule no parent should bend. Swimming with diarrhea can spread germs that cause recreational water illnesses, and some of them, such as Cryptosporidium, can survive even in properly chlorinated water. The CDC advises that anyone with diarrhea should not enter the water, and for certain diarrheal illnesses recommends waiting two weeks after the diarrhea stops before swimming. A single accident from one sick child can sicken an entire pool of swimmers, so keeping your child out protects other families as much as your own.

Do kids with ear tubes have to stay out of the water?

In most cases, no. The American Academy of Otolaryngology updated its guidance, and most children with standard ear tubes can swim in pools and surface water without ear plugs. The exception is diving and forceful underwater submersion at depth, which can push water through the tubes into the middle ear. That is different from a typical middle ear infection with an intact eardrum, where the eardrum keeps water out and swimming generally does not make things worse, and different again from swimmer’s ear, which is an infection of the ear canal that water aggravates and which means staying out until it heals. Confirm your own child’s situation with their ENT or pediatrician.

Is it safe to swim with a waterproof cast cover?

It depends entirely on the cast, and the orthopedist decides, not the cover. Traditional plaster and standard fiberglass casts must stay dry and cannot be submerged at all. Some orthopedists use a special waterproof cast liner that is designed to get wet. Waterproof covers exist for the first kind, but they are not automatically approved for swimming and a leak turns a dry cast into a skin problem underneath. Ask your child’s doctor first and never assume a cast can go in the water without their approval. A cast also changes buoyancy and arm use, so supervision matters more than usual even in shallow water.

Will a swim school give a makeup lesson if my child is sick?

Often, but the rules decide whether the makeup is real. Common policies cap makeups at two to four per session, require them to be used within 30 to 60 days, and demand 24 to 48 hours of advance notice. That last rule is the one that bites on sick days, because a fever at 6 a.m. rarely gives you 24 hours’ warning. Ask before you enroll whether same-day illness cancellations still earn a makeup, whether a doctor’s note changes the answer, and how long the credit lasts. Policies vary widely between programs, so use the agreement you are actually given rather than a general rule.

How soon can a child return to swim lessons after a stomach bug?

Wait until they are fully recovered, and longer if diarrhea was involved. For a mild cold, your child can return once they feel well. For a fever, wait until it has been gone for a full day and they are back to normal. For diarrhea, follow CDC guidance, which for certain diarrheal illnesses means waiting two weeks after it stops. When they do go back, ease in with a shorter, lower-intensity session, keep them well hydrated, and dry their ears thoroughly afterwards. Missing one lesson is a minor setback; spreading an illness or worsening an infection is not.

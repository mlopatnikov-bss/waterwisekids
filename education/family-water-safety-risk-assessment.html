The Family Water Safety Risk Assessment: Score Your Home in 25 Questions

Most families are not unsafe around water. Most families are strong in two layers of protection, decent in a third, and completely blank in a fourth — and nobody notices, because no one ever scores it. This free printable scorecard rates your family across all five layers in 25 questions, then tells you which one to fix first.

📊 What is a family water safety risk assessment?

A family water safety risk assessment is a scored self-audit that rates your household across the five layers of drowning protection, so you can see which layer is weakest rather than whether you are broadly "being careful." It replaces the yes-or-no checklist with a 0–1–2 score: 0 for not in place, 1 for partly (sometimes, or only some children, or only at home), and 2 for fully (every child, every time, everywhere you swim). Five layers, five questions each, ten points per layer, fifty points total.

The scoring scale is the whole point. Water safety failures rarely come from families who do nothing — they come from families who do four things well and one thing not at all, and who feel safe because of the four. A yes-or-no checklist hides that shape. A scored one exposes it in about ten minutes at the kitchen table.

This assessment complements the full layers of protection water safety checklist: the checklist tells you what to do, and the scorecard tells you which part to do first.

🛡️ What are the five layers of drowning protection?

The five layers are barriers, supervision, swim skills and water competency, life jackets and equipment, and emergency readiness — and they exist because each layer covers the moments another layer fails. The framework is shared across the American Academy of Pediatrics, the CDC, and the National Drowning Prevention Alliance. Here is what each layer is protecting against, and what the assessment asks about it.

Layer 1 — Barriers and access control. This layer works when nobody is watching, which is exactly when young children reach water. It covers four-sided isolation fencing that separates the pool from the house, self-closing and self-latching gates, door and window alarms, and the indoor water most families forget: bathtubs, toilets, buckets, coolers, and pet bowls. Our pool fence and gate inspection checklist covers the annual test, and the room-by-room home water safety checklist covers everything indoors.

Layer 2 — Supervision. This layer fails through diffusion of responsibility, not neglect. Six adults around a pool is functionally zero adults if none of them has been named. The assessment asks whether one specific adult is assigned out loud, whether that adult does nothing else, whether children under five and non-swimmers stay within arm's reach, and whether gatherings have a rotation set before anyone gets wet. The Water Watcher card is the simplest fix for a low score here.

Layer 3 — Swim skills and water competency. This is the only layer that travels with the child. The assessment goes past "can they swim" to full water competency: entering water over their head, returning to the surface, floating or treading for a minute, turning around and orienting to safety, swimming 25 yards, and exiting without a ladder. It also asks whether skills are maintained year-round, because motor skills fade over a long off-season. See the water competency skills checklist for the full standard.

Layer 4 — Life jackets and equipment. The most common failure here is not absence but substitution: treating water wings and puddle jumpers as safety devices. The assessment asks about U.S. Coast Guard–approved jackets sized to current weight, jackets worn in open water and on boats, floaties correctly classified as toys, poolside reaching and throwing equipment, and compliant anti-entrapment drain covers. Our life jacket sizing guide and floaties and puddle jumpers safety checklist both address this layer.

Layer 5 — Emergency readiness. This is the most commonly blank layer in otherwise careful families, because nothing in daily life ever reveals that it is missing. The assessment asks about current CPR certification including infant and child CPR, whether caregivers can recognize the signs of drowning, whether a charged phone and a posted street address sit at the water, whether roles are assigned in advance, and whether the plan has ever actually been rehearsed out loud. Build yours with the water emergency action plan.

🔢 How the scoring works — and why your lowest layer matters most

Score each of the 25 questions 0, 1, or 2, total each layer out of 10, and read your lowest layer before you read your total. A family at 40 out of 50 with a zero in emergency readiness is more exposed than a family at 34 spread evenly, because layered protection is not additive — it is sequential. Each layer is there to catch what the previous one dropped. A layer scoring zero has nothing behind it.

The score bands on the printable read like this. 42–50: strong — your layers are working together, so maintain them, re-score each spring, re-check life jacket sizing as children grow, and renew CPR on schedule. 30–41: good with gaps — one or two layers are carrying the others, so close the lowest one this month. 18–29: significant risk — too much depends on adults never being distracted, so prioritize the layers that survive distraction. Below 18: act this week — start with lessons and barriers.

If two layers tie for lowest, break the tie toward the ones that protect a child without requiring an adult to be paying attention at that exact moment: barriers and swim skills. A fence works at 2 a.m. when nobody knows a child slipped out the back door. Swim skills work at the neighbor's pool, the hotel, and the lake, where your fence and your house rules simply do not exist.

Why families score themselves too generously

The most common scoring error is answering for your best day instead of your average one, which is why the middle score exists. A "1" is not a failing grade — it is the honest answer for "we do this at home but not at Grandma's," or "our oldest can swim but the younger two cannot," or "the alarm is installed but the battery died in March." Those partial states are precisely where children get hurt, and rounding them up to a 2 defeats the purpose of scoring at all.

Score for the water your family actually uses this year, not the pool in your backyard alone. Most families swim in five or six different bodies of water across a season: their own pool, a relative's, a neighbor's, a hotel, a lake or beach on vacation, and the bathtub at home. A family with excellent home barriers and no plan for the rental house has a Layer 1 score of 1, not 2. Our vacation water safety checklist exists for exactly that gap.

One more honest-scoring rule: score per child, and take the lowest. If one child has completed lessons and another has not, Layer 3 is scored on the child who has not. Protection is not an average across siblings.

📅 When to score and re-score

Score once now, then re-score every spring before pools open, and again after any change that shifts your risk. The end of summer is an unusually good time for the first scoring, because the season is fresh in memory — you can still recall the party where nobody was watching, the vacation rental with the unlatched gate, the cousin who could not swim as well as everyone assumed.

Re-score after a new baby, a move to a home with a pool or hot tub, a child aging into more independence around water, or a long off-season. Several layers expire quietly: life jackets stop fitting as children grow, CPR certification lapses on a two-year clock, and swim skills fade when they go unused from September to June. A family that honestly scored 45 in June can be a 37 by the following May without anyone doing a single thing wrong — which is the strongest argument for year-round swim skills and a dated scorecard on the fridge.

What to do about the layer you score lowest

Pick your lowest layer, write down three specific fixes with dates, and re-score in 90 days. The printable includes a short action block for exactly this, because an assessment that ends in a number changes nothing — the fix has to be named and dated.

If Layer 1 is lowest, the highest-value fix is usually the gate, not the fence: self-closing and self-latching hardware fails silently and is cheap to replace. If Layer 2 is lowest, print the Water Watcher card today and use it at the next family swim. If Layer 4 is lowest, take fifteen minutes to check every life jacket against each child's current weight and retire the water wings. If Layer 5 is lowest, book a CPR class this month and post your street address at the pool.

And if Layer 3 is lowest — the most common weak layer in families with pools, because a backyard pool creates confidence rather than competence — the fix is formal instruction. The American Academy of Pediatrics reports that swim lessons reduce drowning risk by as much as 88% for children ages 1–4. Quality, safety-first swim programs teach survival skills like back floating and rolling to breathe from as early as a few months old, year-round. For how to evaluate a program before you enroll, see choosing a swim school, and if cost is the barrier, our swim lesson scholarships page lists programs that help.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good score on the water safety risk assessment?

A score of 42 to 50 out of 50 indicates strong layered protection that mainly needs maintenance. A score of 30 to 41 means one or two layers are carrying the others and the weakest layer should be closed within the month. A score of 18 to 29 signals significant risk, with too much depending on adults never being distracted. Below 18 calls for action this week, starting with the two layers that work even when supervision lapses: swim lessons and physical barriers. In every band, the correct next step is set by the lowest layer subtotal, not the total.

Which water safety layer should I fix first?

Fix your lowest-scoring layer first, because layers are designed to compensate for each other's failures and a layer scoring zero has nothing behind it. If two layers tie for lowest, prioritize the ones that protect a child without requiring an adult to be paying attention at that exact moment: barriers and swim skills. A fence works at 2 a.m. when no one knows a child left the house, and swim skills work at a friend's pool, a hotel, or a lake where your fence and your rules do not apply.

How often should a family redo the water safety assessment?

Re-score at least once a year, ideally each spring before pools open, and again after any change that shifts risk: a new baby, a move to a home with a pool, a child aging into more independence, or a summer that ends with skills going unused. Life jacket sizing and CPR certification both expire quietly, and swim skills fade over a long off-season, so a family that scored 45 in June can score 37 by the following May without anyone doing anything wrong.

Do swim lessons really lower drowning risk?

Yes. The American Academy of Pediatrics reports that formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by as much as 88% for children ages 1 to 4, and recommends lessons as a layer of protection for most children starting around age 1. Lessons are the layer that travels with a child rather than staying home with the fence. Lessons do not make a child drown-proof and never replace supervision or barriers, but among the five layers they are the one that keeps working at a friend's pool, a hotel, or a lake.

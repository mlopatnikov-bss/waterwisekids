Indoor Pool Safety Checklist for Families

Every fall, family swimming moves indoors — to the hotel pool on a weekend trip, the gym or apartment pool down the hall, the community center, the year-round indoor swim lesson. Parents tend to relax a little once there's a roof overhead. That instinct is backwards. Indoor pools trade one set of hazards for another: air that can sting instead of sun that can burn, echo that hides a child in trouble instead of open space, and very often no lifeguard at all. This free printable checklist walks you through what to check before your child gets in.

Hard surfaces make decks slick and create echo that masks the quiet signs of drowning. Most hotel, gym, and apartment indoor pools have no lifeguard, so a designated adult Water Watcher is essential. 🏠 Why do indoor pools carry their own risks?

The single most common indoor-pool mistake is assuming the roof makes it safe. A pool inside a building removes weather, sun, and open water — the hazards parents can see. What remains is the hazard that actually causes drownings: water deeper than a child can stand in, and adults who are nearby but not truly watching. Add the fact that indoor pools are often small, tucked away, and unstaffed, and the risk profile shifts rather than shrinks. A hotel pool at 9pm with two families in it and no guard on duty is one of the least supervised environments a child will swim in all year.

Indoor pools also concentrate hazards that outdoor pools dilute. Chemical byproducts that would blow away in open air accumulate under a roof. Splashed water that would evaporate on a hot deck sits on smooth tile. Sound that would dissipate outside bounces off glass and hard walls until every voice becomes indistinguishable noise. None of this makes indoor swimming a bad idea — the year-round consistency an indoor pool makes possible is one of the best things a family can do for water safety. It just means the checklist changes.

🌬️ What does a strong chemical smell at an indoor pool tell you?

A well-run indoor pool barely smells like anything. The sharp chemical odor people call "the chlorine smell" is mostly chloramines — compounds that form when chlorine reacts with sweat, body oils, urine, and other contaminants swimmers carry in. Chloramines are heavier than air and settle in a layer right above the water surface, which is exactly where a swimmer's face is. When ventilation is inadequate or the water chemistry is off, they build up and cause stinging eyes, a scratchy throat, coughing, and a stuffy feeling. Counterintuitively, a strong smell means the pool needs more attention, not that it has been over-chlorinated.

Families reduce chloramines directly: everyone showers before getting in, takes bathroom breaks on a schedule, and nobody pees in the pool. Swim diapers for babies and toddlers are non-negotiable, and they need changing in the changing room, never on the deck. On the facility side, look for a natatorium that feels airy rather than heavy, where you don't notice the air at all after a few minutes. Our guide to pool chemistry basics for parents covers what proper chlorine and pH levels look like, and public pool hygiene explains what a clean facility should feel like on arrival.

Air quality matters most for kids with reactive airways. Children with asthma often do very well in the water — warm, humid air is generally kinder to airways than cold outdoor air — but a poorly ventilated pool can trigger symptoms. Keep the rescue inhaler in the swim bag on deck rather than locked away, tell the instructor about the diagnosis before the first lesson, and if a particular facility reliably makes your child cough, that's information worth acting on.

🚷 What should you check on the deck, depth, and drains?

Indoor decks are the slipperiest surface most children encounter all year. Smooth tile plus constant splash-over plus bare feet plus a child excited to get in is a predictable fall, and indoor pool decks put a hard edge within a few feet of almost every slip. The rule is the oldest one in aquatics and still the most violated: walking feet only, always. Reinforce it before you get to the pool rather than shouting it across an echoing room afterward.

Then do a two-minute walkthrough before your child gets in. Look at the water first: if you cannot clearly see the bottom drain at the deepest point, the pool should not be used — cloudy water means a swimmer in trouble on the bottom is invisible, and it signals a filtration or chemistry problem. Find the depth markings and note where the floor drops off, since many small indoor pools slope faster than they look. Confirm where the shallow end ends. Check that drain and suction covers on the floor and walls are intact and securely fastened; a loose, broken, or missing cover is an entrapment hazard serious enough to close a pool over. Finally, locate the rescue ring or reaching pole, the first aid kit, and the nearest phone. If a facility has none of those, that tells you what kind of operation you're swimming in.

👀 Who is the lifeguard when an indoor pool has none?

Most indoor pools attached to hotels, apartments, gyms, and rentals have no lifeguard on duty, which means the supervising adult is the lifeguard. That job cannot be shared vaguely among the adults present — when everyone is responsible, nobody is. Assign one adult as the designated Water Watcher, phone away, eyes on the water, and hand the role off out loud when you swap. Twenty-minute shifts keep attention sharp. Children who are not strong swimmers stay within arm's reach, close enough to touch, every second they're in.

Indoor acoustics make this harder than it sounds. Hard tile, glass, and low ceilings turn a handful of happy kids into a wall of noise, and the human signs of drowning are quiet ones. A drowning child cannot call out, cannot wave, and typically slips under in less than a minute — they go vertical in the water, head tilted back, arms pressing down as if climbing an invisible ladder, with no splashing at all. Knowing what drowning actually looks like matters double in a room where you will never hear it happen. Watch for stillness and silence, not for noise.

Supervision is one layer, not the whole plan. Layered protection means barriers, constant supervision, swim skills, life jackets when appropriate, and an adult who knows CPR — because any single layer can fail on any given day. In an unguarded indoor pool, several of those layers are already thin, which makes the remaining ones matter more.

🏊 Do swim skills fade without year-round practice?

The best reason to get comfortable with indoor pools is that they make year-round swimming possible, and swim skills fade fast without practice. A child who stops in September and starts again in June spends the first weeks of summer relearning what they already knew. Families who keep lessons going through the school year in a warm indoor pool arrive at summer ready instead of rusty — and formal swim lessons are one of the strongest documented layers of protection against childhood drowning.

There's a safety dividend beyond skill retention, too. A child who practices safe entries, back floating, and getting to the wall every week in January is practicing them constantly, not once a year. The habits become automatic. Frequently Asked Questions

Why does an indoor pool smell so strongly like chlorine?

That sharp smell is usually not chlorine itself. It comes from chloramines, which form when chlorine reacts with sweat, body oils, urine, and other things swimmers bring into the water. A well-run indoor pool with good ventilation and proper chemistry has very little odor. A strong chemical smell, stinging eyes, or a scratchy throat suggests chloramines are building up and the air handling or water chemistry needs attention. Everyone showering before they get in, taking regular bathroom breaks, and never peeing in the pool measurably reduces how many chloramines form in the first place.

Is indoor pool air safe for a child with asthma?

Many children with asthma swim comfortably and benefit from it, because the warm, humid air near the surface is generally easier on the airways than cold outdoor air. The variable is air quality, not the sport. Poorly ventilated indoor pools where chloramines accumulate can irritate airways and trigger symptoms. Choose facilities where the air smells clean and does not sting, keep your child's rescue inhaler in the swim bag on deck rather than in a locker, tell the instructor about the diagnosis before the first lesson, and talk with your pediatrician about an asthma action plan for swimming.

Do I still need to watch my child closely at an indoor pool?

Yes, and indoor pools arguably make close supervision harder rather than easier. Hard tile and glass create echo that turns every voice into noise, so the quiet signs of a child in trouble are easy to miss. Drowning is silent and fast, not the splashing and shouting people expect. Many hotel, apartment, gym, and community indoor pools have no lifeguard at all, which means you are the lifeguard. Assign one adult as the designated Water Watcher, keep young or weak swimmers within arm's reach, and hand the role off out loud when you take a break.

What should I check before letting my child into an indoor pool?

Do a two-minute walkthrough. Confirm you can see the bottom of the pool clearly at the deepest point, since cloudy water means the pool should not be used. Check the posted depth markings and where the no-diving areas are. Look for a rescue ring or reaching pole and a first aid kit, and note whether a lifeguard is on duty and where the nearest phone is. Check that drain covers on the floor and walls look intact and securely fastened. Notice whether the air smells clean, and whether the deck is slick or has standing water in the walkways.

Should we keep swimming indoors through the school year?

Yes. Swim skills fade with time out of the water, and the months between September and May are exactly when many families stop and lose ground. Indoor year-round lessons in a warm pool keep skills sharp so your child is not relearning the basics every June. Formal swim lessons are one of the strongest layers of protection against drowning, and consistency is what makes them stick. Water safety habits also transfer: the child who practices safe entries and back floating in January is far better prepared for an open pool in July.

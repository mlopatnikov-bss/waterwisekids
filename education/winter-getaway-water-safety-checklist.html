Winter Getaway Water Safety Checklist for Families (Free Printable)

Heading to Florida, the Caribbean or a warm resort this winter? It feels like summer, but children’s swim skills may have faded since August, the pools and beaches are new, lifeguard hours may be shorter and the adults are tired from travel. This checklist covers what to do before you go, what to pack, the first-hour arrival sweep, pool and beach rules, relatives’ homes and an emergency plan, with a free printable checklist.

Quick Answer: Before a winter trip somewhere warm, check your child’s skills and book a few refresher lessons if they haven’t swum since summer, ask your host about pools, fences, gates and nearby ponds or canals, and pack each child’s own Coast Guard-approved life jacket. In the first hour after you arrive, walk the property and check every gate, door and hot tub cover. Name a Water Watcher every time children swim, stay within arm’s reach of young or weak swimmers, swim only at guarded beaches and check flags and lifeguard hours daily, and teach the rip current plan: relax, swim along the shore, call or wave for help. 🌴 Why Winter Trips Need Their Own Plan

A December, February or spring-break trip to Florida, the Caribbean, Arizona or a warm resort feels like summer, but it isn’t the same as a summer weekend at your own pool. Many children have not swum since August, so skills they had in summer may have faded; our guide to swim skill retention explains why. The pools, beaches and homes are new to everyone, travel days leave children tired and adults distracted, and holiday crowds make it easy to assume someone else is watching. Lifeguard hours at some beaches and resort pools can also be limited or seasonal, so check what is staffed when you arrive. In Florida, the Florida Department of Health says drowning is the leading cause of unintentional death for children ages 1 to 4. None of this means skipping the pool; it means a short plan before you go and a quick check when you arrive.

📅 Two to Four Weeks Before You Go

Start with your child’s skills. Can they get in and back to the surface, roll to their back and float, turn around to the wall and climb out on their own? Our water competency skills checklist lists what to look for. If the answer is “not since summer”, a few refresher lessons before the trip help skills come back while the water is still familiar; our swim lesson winter break plan covers keeping lessons going over the holidays. Then ask questions about where you are staying: is there a pool, hot tub, pond, canal or lake on or next to the property? Is the pool fenced on all four sides with a self-closing, self-latching gate? Do doors to the pool area lock or have alarms? Is there a lifeguard at the resort pool, and when? Our vacation rental pool safety checklist has a longer list of questions for hosts.

🧳 Pack the Safety Gear You Won’t Find There

Rentals, condos and many resorts do not keep life jackets in children’s sizes. The American Academy of Pediatrics says life jackets should be U.S. Coast Guard-approved and fit properly, that children should always wear one in, on or near natural bodies of water such as lakes or rivers, and that children without strong swim skills should also wear one at a pool or water park. It also advises avoiding floaties, water wings and inflatable toys, which can deflate and do not prevent drowning. Pack each child’s own fitted life jacket (our life jacket sizing guide explains the fit check), bright swimsuits that are easy to spot, a Water Watcher card or tag, and a printed copy of the address where you are staying so anyone can give it to 911.

🔑 The First-Hour Arrival Sweep

The riskiest moment is often the first hour, when bags are coming in, doors are propped open and children run off to explore. Before you unpack, one adult takes the children while another walks the property. Find every pool, hot tub and water feature, plus ponds, canals, lakes, fountains and even buckets or coolers with melted ice. Check that pool gates close and latch on their own, that doors and windows to the pool area lock, and that hot tub covers are locked. The Florida Department of Health says home swimming pools are where most children are likely to drown, and many vacation homes and condos have them. If a gate does not latch or there is no fence, tell the host, and plan for a dedicated adult or a locked door at all times. Our hotel pool safety checklist covers resort and hotel pools.

🏊 Every Time Children Swim

Name one adult as the Water Watcher for each swim, with no phone, no book and no drinks, and hand off the role out loud. The AAP recommends staying within arm’s length with constant “touch supervision” for young children and weaker swimmers, and rotating the water watcher every 15 to 20 minutes. It also says that if you need to get out, take your child with you, even if lifeguards are present. Set the rules on day one: no going to the pool without a grown-up, walk on the deck, and feet first in water you don’t know. Our touch supervision guide and why lifeguards don’t replace supervision explain more, and our signs of drowning guide shows that drowning is usually quiet.

🌊 Beach Days: Flags, Lifeguards and Rip Currents

The National Weather Service advises swimming near a lifeguard, and the United States Lifesaving Association calculates the chance of a fatal drowning at a beach protected by its affiliated lifeguards at 1 in 18 million, based on ten years of reports. In winter, some beaches have shorter lifeguard hours or none, so check the posted hours and flags each day and swim only in guarded areas. Teach the rip current plan before you go: the National Weather Service says to relax, because rip currents don’t pull you under, not to swim straight back to shore, and to swim along the shoreline until out of the current’s pull; if you can’t reach shore, face the shore and call or wave for help. If someone else is caught, get a lifeguard, call 911 and throw something that floats rather than swimming out. Our rip current safety checklist and beach warning flags guide have more detail.

👵 Grandparents’ Condos and Relatives’ Homes

Many winter trips are visits to grandparents or relatives who live near water. The AAP reminds parents to keep water safety in mind at home, “where your friends, relatives and caregivers live and places you stay during family trips.” Homes that have not had small children for years may have gates left open, pool covers that look solid but are not, or a canal or retention pond behind the yard. Talk with your hosts before you arrive about who watches the children and when, and share our grandparent water safety checklist. Our guide to retention pond water safety covers the stormwater ponds common in many warm-weather neighborhoods.

🚑 Know Your Emergency Plan in a New Place

In an unfamiliar place, write down the address, the nearest emergency room and the lifeguard hours, and keep a phone charged by the pool. If a child is missing, check the water first. If you find a child in the water, get them out, shout for help, call 911 and follow the dispatcher’s instructions; our CPR basics for parents guide explains why breaths matter after drowning. Our printable has a fill-in box for this information so everyone in the group knows the plan.

🎽 Get Skills Ready Before the Trip

Weekly indoor lessons through the winter keep skills fresh so a February trip doesn’t start from scratch. The AAP describes lessons as one important layer of protection that does not make children drown-proof. You can browse swim lessons near you, read about winter swim lessons and keeping lessons going while you travel, or compare plans in our vacation water safety checklist. Families in New Jersey and Pennsylvania can see our pages on British Swim School in the Jersey Shore area and Northwest Philadelphia, which link to the official location pages; ask about a refresher before your trip. Swim lessons never replace close adult supervision, barriers, life jackets and a Water Watcher.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does a winter beach or pool trip need its own water safety plan?

Winter getaways combine several risks at once: children’s swim skills may have faded since summer, the pools, beaches and homes are unfamiliar, lifeguard coverage at some beaches and resort pools is limited or seasonal, and adults are often tired and distracted by travel and holiday plans. A short plan before you go and a property check when you arrive cover the gaps.

Should my child take swim lessons before a winter vacation?

A few refresher lessons in the weeks before a trip can help a child who has not swum since summer get back basic skills such as getting back to the surface, floating, turning to the wall and climbing out. The American Academy of Pediatrics says swim lessons are one layer of protection but do not make children drown-proof, so close supervision, barriers and life jackets are still needed on the trip.

What should I check when we arrive at a rental, condo or relative’s home?

Before you unpack, walk the property: find every pool, hot tub, pond, canal or lake, check that pool gates close and latch by themselves, see whether doors to the pool area lock or have alarms, look for unlocked hot tub covers and buckets or fountains, and agree which adult watches the children. The Florida Department of Health says home swimming pools are where most children are likely to drown.

Do we need to bring our own life jackets?

Often, yes. Rentals, condos and some resorts may not have life jackets in children’s sizes. The American Academy of Pediatrics says life jackets should be U.S. Coast Guard-approved and fit properly, that children should always wear them in, on or near natural bodies of water, and that children without strong swim skills should also wear them at a pool or water park. It advises avoiding floaties and water wings, which can deflate and do not prevent drowning.

What should my child do if caught in a rip current?

The National Weather Service says to relax, because rip currents don’t pull you under, not to swim straight back to shore, and to swim along the shoreline until out of the current’s pull. If you can’t reach shore, face the shore and call or wave for help. Parents should not swim out after a child; get a lifeguard, call 911 and throw something that floats if you can.

Are beaches safer when a lifeguard is on duty?

Yes. The National Weather Service advises swimming near a lifeguard, and the United States Lifesaving Association calculates the chance of a fatal drowning at a beach protected by its affiliated lifeguards at 1 in 18 million, based on ten years of reports. Check the lifeguard hours and flags each day, because coverage can change by season, and remember that a lifeguard never replaces your own eyes on your child.

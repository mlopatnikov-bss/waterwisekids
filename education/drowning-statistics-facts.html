Drowning Statistics & Facts: Understanding the Numbers Behind Water Safety

Drowning is preventable. Understanding the data—who is at risk, when and where drowning happens, and what the trends tell us—helps families make informed decisions about water safety.

Quick Answer: The United States averaged 4,083 unintentional drowning deaths and an estimated 8,111 emergency department visits for nonfatal drowning per year in 2012–2021. A separate CDC report counted more than 4,500 deaths in each year from 2020 through 2022. CDC identifies drowning as the leading cause of death for U.S. children ages 1–4. See CDC Drowning Facts and CDC Vital Signs.

The sections below break each of them down. For what to do with the numbers, see our drowning prevention guide.

Why Do Drowning Statistics Matter?

Understanding drowning data reveals patterns that help parents protect children through evidence-based decisions. Drowning statistics tell stories: which children are most vulnerable, when and where drowning happens, and what works to prevent it. Data from sources like the CDC Drowning Facts shows that supervision, barriers, swim skills, and CPR knowledge aren't just guidelines—they're grounded in analysis of real incidents, real risks, and real outcomes.

How Many People Drown Each Year in the U.S.?

More than 4,500 people died from drowning in the United States each year during 2020–2022 — about 12 deaths a day. That is roughly 500 more deaths a year than in 2019, and the first sustained increase after decades of decline, according to CDC Vital Signs (2024).

You will also see the figure about 4,000 a year quoted widely, including on CDC's own Drowning Facts page. Both numbers are correct — they cover different periods. The ~4,000 figure (precisely, 4,083) is the annual average across 2012–2021. The 4,500+ figure is the level reached in each year from 2020 through 2022, as reported in the CDC’s 2024 Vital Signs analysis. When you cite a drowning total, name the period with it.

How many children drown each year?

The CDC’s Vital Signs analysis recorded 461 unintentional drowning deaths at ages 1–4 and 230 at ages 5–14 in 2022. Among children ages 1–4, the death rate was about 28% higher in 2022 than in 2019.

How many nonfatal drownings happen each year?

About 8,100 emergency-department visits a year for nonfatal drowning (2012–2021 average). Drowning survivors can have a range of outcomes, including lasting brain injury. Fatal totals alone understate the harm.

What Is the Drowning Risk by Age Group?

The CDC’s 2024 Vital Signs report gives the following unintentional drowning death rates for 2022. These are deaths per 100,000 people in each age group.

  • Under 1 year: 1.1 per 100,000, or 39 deaths.
  • Ages 1–4: 3.1 per 100,000, or 461 deaths.
  • Ages 5–14: 0.6 per 100,000, or 230 deaths.
  • Ages 15–24: 1.1 per 100,000, or 488 deaths.

Where Does Most Childhood Drowning Happen?

In its drowning risk summary, CDC identifies different settings by age:

  • Infants under 1: About three-quarters of drownings occur in bathtubs.
  • Ages 1–4: Home swimming pools account for most drownings.
  • Ages 5–14: More than 40% occur in natural water and more than 35% in pools.
  • Ages 15 and older: Nearly half of fatal drownings occur in natural water.

How do drowning risks change by season?

A CDC surveillance study notes that most U.S. drowning deaths occur between May and August.

Keep supervision and barriers in place throughout the year, including near bathtubs, indoor pools, cold open water, and frozen ponds and lakes.

What Are the Demographic Disparities in Drowning Risk?

In CDC’s 2022 figures, the age-adjusted rate of fatal unintentional drowning was 2.0 per 100,000 among males and 0.6 among females. Rates also differed among racial and ethnic groups. The CDC reported 2.8 deaths per 100,000 among non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native people and 1.6 among non-Hispanic Black people in 2022.

Access to water safety education, swimming instruction, and supervised places to swim can differ by community. The CDC’s report describes those access differences and opportunities for prevention.

What Are Global Drowning Statistics?

The World Health Organization’s May 2026 fact sheet estimates around 300,000 drowning deaths worldwide each year. It reports that 92% occur in low- and middle-income countries and that children under 5 account for nearly one-quarter of drowning deaths worldwide.

What Prevention Methods Actually Work According to the Data?

The statistics reveal clear patterns about what actually prevents drowning. Evidence-based strategies from CDC research are proven effective:

Supervision Is Non-Negotiable

  • Keep a capable adult within arm’s reach of infants, toddlers, and weak swimmers. See the AAP drowning prevention policy.
  • Constant, attentive supervision significantly reduces risk
  • Supervision lapses of even seconds can be fatal for young children

Barriers Reduce Access to Unsupervised Water

  • The CDC cites an 83% lower risk with four-sided isolation fencing compared with three-sided property-line fencing.
  • Barriers delay access long enough for supervision to resume or for alerts to occur
  • Combination of barriers (fencing + gates + alarms) is more effective than any single measure

Swim Skills Matter, but Aren't Foolproof

  • Many drowning victims could swim—skills alone don't prevent all drowning
  • Use swim lessons alongside other layers of protection. The AAP recommends starting swim lessons after the first birthday and individualizing the timing for each child.
  • Water safety education (not just swimming technique) is important for older children and teens

CPR Saves Lives

  • CPR initiated within minutes dramatically improves survival rates and reduces permanent disability
  • Every parent and caregiver should be CPR-trained
  • Early initiation of CPR is one of the few interventions shown to improve outcomes after submersion

Life Jackets Are Proven Protective

  • Properly-fitting, USCG-approved life jackets reduce drowning risk in water environments
  • Compliance is low—only about 10% of boaters and water recreation participants wear life jackets
  • Life jacket use should be automatic for young children and non-swimmers in any water setting

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main drowning statistics in the U.S.?

The CDC reported an annual average of 4,083 unintentional drowning deaths and an estimated 8,111 emergency department visits for nonfatal drowning in 2012–2021. In a separate analysis, the CDC counted more than 4,500 deaths in each year from 2020 through 2022.

Which age group is at highest risk for drowning?

Children ages 1–4 had the highest unintentional drowning death rate of the age groups in the CDC’s 2022 analysis: 3.1 deaths per 100,000 people in that age group.

Where do most childhood drownings occur?

The CDC describes different patterns at different ages. Most infant drownings occur in bathtubs. Most drownings at ages 1–4 occur in home swimming pools. Natural water accounts for more than 40% of drownings at ages 5–14.

Why do males drown at higher rates than females?

CDC’s 2022 data show an age-adjusted fatal unintentional drowning rate of 2.0 per 100,000 for males and 0.6 for females. Exposure to water and other risk factors may contribute to the difference.

What prevention methods actually work to reduce drowning risk?

Use multiple layers of protection, including four-sided pool fencing, close and attentive adult supervision, swim lessons when developmentally appropriate, properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jackets, and caregiver CPR training.

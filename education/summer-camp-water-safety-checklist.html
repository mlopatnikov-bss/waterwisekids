Summer Camp Water Safety Checklist: Ask, Pack, and Practice Before Day One

A free one-page printable for parents — the questions to ask before you enroll, how to prep your child for the swim test, and what good supervision looks like.

Quick Summary: Most summer camps include swimming, and many run a swim test on day one. The risk rises because camps combine crowds, open water, new staff, and kids eager to prove themselves. This guide gives you the exact questions to ask before enrolling, how to prepare your child for the swim test, what to pack, and the supervision standards that separate a safe camp from a risky one. Why does camp change water risk?

Camp changes water risk by combining large groups, open water, unfamiliar staff, and social pressure — a mix that does not exist at a backyard pool. According to the CDC, drowning is the leading cause of unintentional injury death for children ages 1 to 4 and a leading cause for older children (see the full child drowning statistics dashboard for how risk shifts by age and setting). Camp adds a particular mix of risk factors that don't exist at a backyard pool: large groups of children, lifeguards watching many swimmers at once, open water like lakes and rivers, staff your child has just met, and the social pressure to keep up with friends.

None of that means camp is dangerous. Accredited, well-run camps are among the safest places a child can swim, precisely because they layer lifeguards, ratios, buddy systems, and swim tests. The difference between a safe camp and a risky one usually comes down to questions most parents never think to ask — and a little preparation before the season starts. That's what this guide and the accompanying printable checklist are built to do.

1. What should you ask before you enroll?

Before you enroll, ask whether certified lifeguards watch every water activity, what the lifeguard-to-swimmer ratio is, whether the camp is ACA-accredited, how the swim test works, and whether staff hold current CPR and first aid. The enrollment conversation is your best window into how a camp thinks about water. A camp that answers these questions clearly and confidently is showing you its safety culture.

Are certified lifeguards on duty for every water activity? Ask which body certified them — the American Red Cross, Ellis, or YMCA — and whether guards are dedicated solely to watching the water, not also teaching lessons or running games. A guard who is multitasking is not really guarding.

What is the lifeguard-to-swimmer ratio? The American Camp Association (ACA) sets supervision standards that vary by age and setting. Lower ratios for younger and weaker swimmers are a strong signal. If a camp can't tell you its ratio, that's an answer in itself.

Is the camp ACA-accredited? Accreditation reviews aquatic safety, staff screening, and emergency procedures against national standards. It's voluntary, so ask directly. For more on vetting programs generally, see our guide to choosing a swim school — many of the same instincts apply.

How does the swim test work, and how are kids grouped? Ask what the test involves and whether non-swimmers are kept in shallow, roped-off zones. The original summer camp water safety guide covers the questions to ask in more depth.

Are staff trained in CPR and first aid? Confirm certifications are current and that someone with these skills is present at every water activity. A child's brain begins suffering damage within four to six minutes without oxygen, and EMS often takes longer. Our CPR basics for parents explains why this layer matters.

2. How do you prepare your child for the swim test?

Prepare your child by building deep-water comfort, rehearsing treading water and back-floating, being honest about their level, and restarting lessons several weeks early if skills have faded. Many camps run a swim test on the first day to sort children into ability groups. Knowing what's coming removes the surprise — and the panic that can come with it.

Know what "passing" usually means. A typical test asks a child to swim a set distance without stopping (often the length of the pool), tread water for one to two minutes, and float on their back — usually in deep water. The exact bar varies by camp.

Practice deep-water comfort before camp. A child who only swims where they can touch the bottom may freeze the moment their feet leave the floor. This is best built gradually in lessons, not discovered on test day in front of new peers.

Rehearse treading water and back-floating. These rest-and-recover skills matter far more than speed. A child who can flip onto their back and breathe has the single most important self-rescue tool.

Be honest about your child's level. Don't coach a child to overstate their ability to get into a "cooler" group. Correct placement is far safer than deep water unprepared. If skills have faded over the winter — and they do fade — restart lessons several weeks early. Year-round lessons keep skills sharp; a pre-camp cram rarely works because water competence takes months, not days, to build. If you're weighing options, our piece on intensive vs. weekly swim lessons can help.

3. What should your child pack for camp water days?

Pack a bright-colored swimsuit, a Coast Guard-approved life jacket for a non-swimmer, water shoes, a towel, reef-safe sunscreen, goggles, a labeled water bottle, and a written note about any medical needs. What goes in the camp bag is a quiet safety decision. A few deliberate choices make your child easier to see and harder to lose track of.

A bright-colored swimsuit. Neon orange, pink, and yellow stay visible in the water; blues, greens, and whites disappear, especially against a pool bottom or murky lake. Our guide to bright swimsuit safety covers the color science.

A Coast Guard–approved life jacket for a non-swimmer. Don't assume the camp will have one that fits. Water wings and puddle jumpers are toys, not safety devices — see water wings vs. life jackets.

Water shoes, a towel, reef-safe sunscreen, goggles, and a labeled water bottle. Slippery decks and docks cause most camp water injuries that aren't drownings, and a comfortable, hydrated child is a calmer swimmer.

A written note to staff about medical needs. Seizure history, ear tubes, asthma, or recent illness all affect water activity. Put it in writing so it doesn't get lost in a busy first-day handoff.

4. What does good supervision look like?

Good supervision means dedicated lifeguards who only watch the water, counselors within arm's reach of younger and non-swimming kids, ability-based roped zones, and regular whistle-stop buddy checks. If you can observe a swim period — at a tour or open house — watch for these signs. They tell you more than any brochure.

Dedicated lifeguards who only watch the water. Guards shouldn't double as instructors, photographers, or game leaders during free swim. Counselors in the water with younger groups — within arm's reach for non-swimmers and kids under about eight. Roped-off zones by ability, with beginners kept out of deep water. Regular buddy checks, where everyone freezes and is counted on a whistle every ten to fifteen minutes. And a staff culture that understands drowning is silent — a drowning child is usually quiet and vertical, not splashing or shouting.

5. What should you ask about open water and off-site trips?

Ask about every off-site water trip in advance and confirm that life jackets are required for all non-strong swimmers in open water, since pool skills do not transfer reliably to lakes, rivers, and beaches. Many camps take kids to lakes, rivers, or beaches. Open water is far less forgiving than a pool: the bottom drops without warning, currents pull, and cold pockets surprise even strong swimmers. Ask about every off-site water trip in advance, and confirm life jackets are required for all non-strong swimmers in open water — pool skills don't transfer reliably. Confirm the camp checks weather and water conditions, and that lightning, high surf, or algae blooms cancel water time. If boating or paddling is on the schedule, Coast Guard–approved life jackets are required for kids and should fit each child. Our lake and ocean safety guide and open water checklist go deeper.

6. What should the emergency and communication plan cover?

The plan should cover how the camp reaches you in an emergency, a written Emergency Action Plan for the water (who calls 911, clears the water, and performs CPR), and clear water rules you have reviewed with your child. Before the session starts, confirm how the camp reaches you in an emergency and that they have current numbers, your child's conditions, and a backup contact. Ask whether a written Emergency Action Plan exists for the water — who calls 911, who clears the water, who performs CPR, and where the nearest AED is. Then talk to your child about camp water rules: listen to guards, stay with your buddy, never swim alone or sneak off, and tell an adult immediately if a friend is struggling. Finally, trust the test result — if your child is placed with beginners, support it. It means staff are paying attention.

How do swim lessons fit into camp water safety?

Swim lessons are a year-round foundation, not a pre-camp cram — water familiarity, deep-water comfort, and self-rescue take weeks to months to build before they pay off all summer. Here's the throughline: the work you do in spring pays off all summer. Formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk in children ages 1–4 by up to 88%, according to AAP research — but lessons are a year-round commitment, not a crash course the week before camp. Water familiarity, deep-water comfort, and self-rescue take weeks to months to build.

If your child isn't currently in lessons, the question isn't "can we squeeze one in before camp" — it's "when do we start?" Frequently Asked Questions

What questions should I ask a summer camp about water safety?

Ask whether certified lifeguards are on duty for every water activity, what the lifeguard-to-swimmer ratio is, whether the camp is ACA-accredited, how the swim test works and how kids are grouped by ability, whether staff hold current CPR and first-aid certification, what the buddy system and head-count routine looks like, and whether your child will swim in open water like a lake, river, or ocean. The answers reveal how seriously the camp treats aquatic safety.

What does a camp swim test usually involve?

Most camp swim tests ask a child to swim a set distance without stopping — often the length of the pool — tread water for one to two minutes, and float on their back, usually in deep water. Children are then grouped by ability, and non-swimmers are restricted to shallow, roped-off areas. The exact requirements vary, so ask the camp in advance and practice deep-water comfort, treading, and back-floating before test day.

How can I prepare my child for a summer camp swim test?

Practice deep-water comfort so your child doesn't freeze when their feet can't touch, rehearse treading water and back-floating because those rest-and-recover skills matter more than speed, and be honest about your child's level rather than coaching them to overstate it. If skills have faded over the off-season, restart swim lessons several weeks early. Water competence takes months to build, so year-round lessons beat a pre-camp cram.

Does passing a camp swim test mean my child is safe in the water?

No. A swim test is a snapshot of skill on one day, not a guarantee of safety. Passing it does not make a child drown-proof. Active supervision, certified lifeguards, appropriate ratios, roped-off zones by ability, life jackets for non-swimmers, and CPR-trained staff are all still required. Swim lessons reduce drowning risk in children ages 1 to 4 by up to 88% according to the AAP, but they are one layer in a multi-barrier approach, not a substitute for supervision.

What should my child pack for water days at summer camp?

Pack a bright-colored swimsuit in neon orange, pink, or yellow so lifeguards can track your child easily, a Coast Guard–approved life jacket if your child is a non-swimmer, water shoes for slippery decks and docks, a towel, reef-safe sunscreen, goggles, and a labeled water bottle. Send a written note to staff about any medical needs such as seizure history, ear tubes, asthma, or recent illness, since all of these affect water activity.

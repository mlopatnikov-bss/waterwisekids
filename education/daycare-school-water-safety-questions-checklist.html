Daycare & School Water Safety Questions Every Parent Should Ask

You child-proofed your own home. But most of your child's water exposure now happens somewhere you can't see it — a water table at daycare, a sprinkler day in the yard, a bus to a public pool with a permission slip you signed in thirty seconds. Enrollment season is the one moment you have real leverage to ask. This free printable checklist gives you the exact questions to ask your daycare, preschool, school, or aftercare program — and the answers that should worry you.

🔍 The water at daycare and school that parents overlook

Most parents picture a swimming pool when they think about water risk in group care — but the water that actually surrounds young children all day is far more ordinary. Water tables in the toddler room. Mop buckets in a hallway. A wading pool on a hot afternoon. Standing puddles after sprinkler play. Toilets and sinks in a bathroom a two-year-old can reach alone. Young children can drown in a very small amount of water, and it happens silently — there is no splashing, no shouting, nothing to pull a busy adult's attention across a noisy room. Our full guide to water safety at daycare and schools covers the regulations and standards behind all of this in depth.

None of this means water play should stop. Water tables are genuinely good for development, and sprinkler days are the best part of August. The problem is not the activity — it's that in many programs, water activities run on the same staffing as circle time, with no adult specifically assigned to watch the water. Knowing what drowning actually looks like makes the gap obvious: a caregiver who is also refilling cups, wiping hands, and answering the door is not watching.

❓ The five questions that matter most

If you only get two minutes with a director, ask these five. They're not confrontational, and a good program will answer all five immediately and in specifics:

  1. What water is on this property? — water tables, buckets, sprinklers, a wading pool, a swimming pool, a retention pond or creek on or near the grounds.
  2. Who is assigned to watch children during water play, and do they have any other duty at the same time? The right answer names a specific role with one job.
  3. What is the adult-to-child ratio during water activities specifically? Water play should tighten ratios, not match classroom ratios.
  4. Which staff hold current CPR and first-aid certification, and how many are on site at any given time? Ask when it was last renewed.
  5. What is the written plan if a child is found in water? If there isn't a written plan, there isn't a plan.

The quality of the answers matters more than the answers themselves. "Someone's always watching" is not an answer — it means no one person owns the job, which is exactly the condition under which supervision fails. The same principle underlies the water watcher system that quality programs and safety-conscious families both use: supervision works when it is assigned to one named person, undistracted, for a defined block of time.

🚌 Before you sign the swimming field trip permission slip

The permission slip is your last checkpoint, and it deserves more than a signature at the bottom. Ask where the group is going and whether it's a lifeguarded pool or open water; how many adults are supervising and how many of those are assigned to watch the water and nothing else; whether lifeguards will be on duty; whether children are swim-tested or wristbanded by ability before entering; whether Coast Guard-approved life jackets are provided for non-swimmers; and how children are counted, since a headcount at fixed intervals is the standard practice.

Open water trips — a lake, a river, a beach — deserve extra scrutiny. There are no walls to reach, the bottom drops away without warning, and currents can move a capable swimmer faster than an adult on shore can respond. A child who swims confidently in a pool has not automatically transferred those skills to open water. If a summer program is involved, our summer camp water safety checklist covers the camp-specific version of these questions, and the water emergency action plan template shows what a written response plan should actually contain.

🚩 Answers that should worry you

A few responses are worth treating as red flags rather than minor gaps. A program that can't name who supervises water play. No written emergency water plan. Water activities staffed at classroom ratios. Water tables or buckets left filled and unattended between activities. Uncertainty about which staff hold current CPR certification. A field trip permission slip with no detail about location, lifeguards, or life jackets. And — the clearest signal of all — defensiveness when a parent asks.

Most directors welcome these questions. Water safety is a standard quality programs hold themselves to, and being asked about it by a parent is usually a good day for them. If you'd like to go further than asking, many schools will host a free water safety presentation, and there are simple classroom water safety activities teachers can run. For a child with additional needs, especially any history of wandering, pair this checklist with the special needs water safety checklist so the program has a specific plan, not a general one.

🏊 The one layer of protection that goes with your child

Here's the uncomfortable truth behind every question on this list: you're auditing a layer of protection you don't control. You can ask excellent questions in August and still not know what supervision looks like on a chaotic Tuesday in March, or at a birthday party, or at a friend's house with a backyard pool. Supervision, barriers, and life jackets are all layers that belong to whoever is in charge at that moment — a framework laid out in the layers of protection checklist.

Swim skills are different. They're the only layer that travels with your child into every one of those rooms. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends swim lessons for most children starting around age one as part of that layered approach, and formal instruction measurably reduces drowning risk — while never making any child "drown-proof." If you're evaluating programs, the swim lesson enrollment checklist covers what to look for, and year-round, warm-water lessons keep those skills from fading over the school year. Every caregiver in your child's life, yourself included, should also have current CPR training.

💬 Frequently asked questions

What water safety questions should I ask my child's daycare?

Ask five things first: what water is on the property (water tables, buckets, sprinklers, toilets, a pool, a retention pond), who is assigned to watch children during any water play and whether that person has any other duty at the same time, what the adult-to-child ratio is during water activities specifically, whether staff hold current CPR and first-aid certification, and what the written plan is if a child is found in water. A good program answers these immediately and in specifics. Vague answers such as "someone is always watching" are the answer — they tell you no one person owns the job.

Are water tables and sprinklers actually a drowning risk for toddlers?

Yes. Young children can drown in very small amounts of water, and drowning is silent — there is no splashing or calling out to alert a busy adult. Water tables, mop buckets, wading pools, and standing water after sprinkler play are all real hazards in group care, especially for infants and toddlers who cannot lift their heads free. The risk is not that these activities happen; it's that they often happen without one adult assigned solely to watch the water. Ask specifically how water tables are supervised, emptied, and stored, and whether standing water is dumped immediately after play.

What should I ask before signing a swimming field trip permission slip?

Before you sign, ask where the group is going and whether it's a lifeguarded pool or open water such as a lake or beach, how many adults are supervising and how many are assigned to watch the water and nothing else, whether lifeguards will be on duty and how many, whether children are swim-tested or wristbanded by ability, whether US Coast Guard-approved life jackets are provided for non-swimmers, and how children are counted — a headcount at fixed intervals is the standard. Open water field trips deserve extra scrutiny because there are no walls, no clear bottom, and currents or drop-offs can surprise even capable swimmers.

What are the warning signs a school or daycare's water safety is inadequate?

Watch for a program that can't name who supervises water play, has no written emergency water plan, staffs water activities with the same ratio as classroom time, leaves water tables or buckets filled and unattended, can't confirm current CPR certification for staff on site, sends a swimming field trip permission slip with no detail about location, lifeguards, or life jackets, or reacts defensively when you ask. None of these questions is confrontational. A quality program is usually glad a parent asked, because it's the same standard they hold themselves to.

Does my child still need swim lessons if the daycare supervises well?

Yes. Supervision is one layer of protection, and it's a layer you can't control once your child is out of your care. Swim skills are the only layer that travels with your child — to daycare, to a birthday party, to a friend's pool, on a field trip. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends swim lessons for most children starting around age one as part of a layered approach, alongside supervision, barriers, and life jackets. Formal lessons reduce drowning risk, but no child is ever "drown-proof," so lessons supplement good supervision rather than replacing it.

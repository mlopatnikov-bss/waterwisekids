What to Do If You See Someone Drowning

Drowning kills children and adults silently, often without the dramatic signs movies show us. Knowing what real drowning looks like, how to recognize it, and the life-saving steps to take in those critical first moments can mean the difference between recovery and tragedy. Learn the step-by-step response, rescue techniques for bystanders, and what absolutely not to do in a water emergency.

Safety Summary: Call 911 for a water emergency. Begin CPR if unresponsive and not breathing normally; after drowning, include rescue breaths if able. Use age-appropriate compressions and follow the dispatcher and AED. Persistent or worsening breathing symptoms after rescue need prompt medical care.

How Do You Recognize Real Drowning?

Drowning is silent, and recognition takes knowledge. One of the most dangerous myths about drowning is what it looks like. Parents, lifeguards, and bystanders are often taught that a drowning person will wave, yell, thrash about, and call for help. In reality, this almost never happens. Research from the National Drowning Prevention Alliance (NDPA) and CDC shows that real drowning is quiet and often unrecognized. Our drowning statistics page shows how often drowning happens and in which settings.

Francesco Pia, a researcher who studied drowning survivors, coined the term "Instinctive Drowning Response." Unlike the panicked thrashing shown in movies, a person experiencing the instinctive drowning response is physiologically unable to call for help or remove themselves from danger. Their body takes over, and survival instinct takes precedence over voluntary action.

The Instinctive Drowning Response: What to Actually Look For

During the instinctive drowning response, a drowning person will:

  • Mouth at or below water level: The mouth alternates between being above and below the waterline. When the mouth is above water, it's typically open, not closed.
  • Head tilted back: The head is tilted back with mouth open. Eyes are glassy or closed. The face often shows no expression of distress.
  • Arms at or below shoulder level: Arms do not wave. Instead, they're held flat against the body or moved in a manner that actually keeps the person vertical, making it harder to swim.
  • Legs motionless: Children especially may not kick or move their legs. If they do move, it's usually just attempting to stay upright.
  • No splashing or noise: This is the critical point: there is no yelling, waving, or splashing. The person is silent.
  • Eyes looking straight ahead or unfocused: The drowning person is not looking around or making eye contact. They're focused entirely on the act of breathing.
  • Vertical body position: The body is often upright or nearly upright in the water, not horizontal as in swimming or floating.

This response typically lasts 20 to 60 seconds before the person begins to slip underwater. The critical window for recognition and intervention is just one minute.

Distressed Swimmer vs. Drowning Person

It's important to distinguish between someone who is distressed in water (a swimmer in trouble) and someone who is drowning:

A distressed swimmer can still call for help, wave, splash, and make noise. They're panicking, yes, but they can move and vocalize. A distressed swimmer can often self-rescue or accept help verbally. There's time to talk, to reassure, to guide them.

A drowning person is silent and immobilized by the instinctive drowning response. They cannot help themselves. They cannot yell or wave. They require immediate intervention. The window is seconds, not minutes.

Learning to spot these signs—the vertical body position, the silent mouth, the immobility—can be the difference between getting someone help before they go under and losing those critical seconds to a misidentified "distressed swimmer" who's actually drowning.

Why Does Every Second Count in a Drowning Emergency?

Drowning is time-critical. Recognize distress, summon help and begin a safe rescue and appropriate resuscitation immediately. There is no fixed number of seconds or minutes that predicts an individual person’s outcome; do not delay care or assume rescue is futile based on a website timeline.

What Are the Step-by-Step Rescue Actions?

Call for a lifeguard and emergency help. Reach with an object or throw a flotation aid from a stable, safe position. Do not lean so far that you can be pulled in. Tell the person to hold the aid and guide them toward safety if possible.

Do not enter dangerous water or attempt an in-water rescue beyond your training. A flotation device does not make an untrained rescue safe. Trained rescuers should follow their training and local emergency procedures.

After safe removal, check responsiveness and normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the dispatcher. A lay rescuer should not delay for a pulse check.

How Do You Rescue a Drowning Child?

Never enter unsafe water beyond your training. Call for a lifeguard and use reach-or-throw rescue methods from a safe position. A person who is alert and breathing normally should not receive chest compressions.

Call 911 for severe or labored breathing, blue or gray color, unresponsiveness, confusion with breathing distress, or a child who is difficult to wake. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the dispatcher. Anyone who needed CPR or rescue breaths after drowning needs emergency medical care.

A brief cough or sputter that resolves promptly, followed by completely normal breathing and behavior, can be observed by an attentive caregiver. Persistent or worsening cough, rapid or difficult breathing, unusual sleepiness or confusion require prompt medical care. If unsure whether your child is back to normal, contact a medical professional.

What CPR Steps Should Be Taken After a Rescue?

Call 911 and follow the dispatcher. Call for a lifeguard. Reach or throw a flotation aid from a safe position; do not enter dangerous water or attempt an in-water rescue beyond your training. Once safely removed, place the person on a firm, flat surface.

Check responsiveness and breathing

Tap and shout; for an infant, tap the bottom of the foot. Check for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, including only gasping, begin CPR. Lay rescuers should not delay for a pulse check.

Activate emergency help

Send someone to call 911 and get an AED. If alone with a mobile phone, call on speaker and start care while following the dispatcher. If alone with a child or infant and you must leave to reach a phone, give about 2 minutes of CPR before leaving to call.

Include rescue breaths after drowning

Breaths are especially important after drowning. The Red Cross drowning sequence starts with 2 rescue breaths, then cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths for a single rescuer. AHA guidance allows trained rescuers to begin with breaths or compressions; do not delay CPR to debate the sequence. If unable or unwilling to give breaths, start chest compressions and follow the dispatcher.

Give age-appropriate compressions

Compress at 100–120 per minute on the lower half of the breastbone, allowing full recoil and minimizing pauses. For a child from age 1 until puberty, use one or two hands; press at least one third of the chest depth, about 2 inches (5 cm). For an infant under 1 year, use the heel of one hand or two thumbs with hands encircling the chest; press at least one third of its depth, about 1.5 inches (4 cm). The 2025 AHA guidance no longer recommends two-finger infant compressions.

Give breaths and use an AED

For each breath, open the airway with a head tilt and chin lift. Seal over a child’s mouth while pinching the nose; cover an infant’s mouth and nose. Give each breath over about 1 second, only enough for visible chest rise. Continue 30 compressions and 2 breaths. Begin CPR with breaths before applying an AED after drowning. As soon as available, expose and dry the chest, attach pads and follow its prompts; use pediatric pads/attenuation if available. Do not delay CPR to find an AED. Nobody should touch the person during analysis or shock.

Continue and reassess

Continue until normal breathing or other clear signs of life return, trained help takes over, you are too exhausted to continue, or the scene becomes unsafe. Pause when the AED instructs, then promptly resume. If breathing normally but still unresponsive, maintain an open airway and follow the dispatcher; use a side-lying recovery position if appropriate and no suspected spine injury prevents it. Watch breathing continuously and restart CPR if normal breathing stops.

What Should You NOT Do in a Drowning Emergency?

Just as important as knowing what to do is knowing what not to do. Many well-intentioned rescue attempts make situations worse or put rescuers at risk.

Don't Jump in Without Flotation

Never jump into water to rescue someone unless you're trained, wearing a PFD, and have a rescue device. A panicking drowning person is unpredictable and extremely dangerous. They may grab you, pull you under, and cause you both to drown. Trained water rescuers know this and don't take the risk. Neither should you.

Don't Try to Grab a Panicking Swimmer With Bare Hands

If you must enter the water, never approach from the front or allow direct contact. Approach from behind or to the side. Have a rescue device between you and the person at all times. Never let them grab your arms, legs, or body directly. They will pull you down.

Don't Assume Someone Is Playing

If you see someone face-down in water, unmoving and silent, assume they're drowning. Do not assume they're playing or that they'll surface on their own. The seconds you delay could be the seconds that matter. Act immediately.

Don't Leave the Area to Get Help Alone

If you're with others, direct someone else to call 911. Do not leave a drowning person unmonitored. If you're alone, call 911 on speaker while staying with the person and attempting rescue if safe to do so. You can provide emergency responders with updates and maintain a presence in case the person surfaces.

Don't Give Up on CPR Quickly

There are documented cases of people being resuscitated after 15-20 minutes or more of submersion, especially in cold water. The cold water slows metabolism and can preserve brain function. Do not give up on CPR too early. Continue until emergency responders arrive and take over. Let the professionals make the call to stop resuscitation.

Don't Remove Water From the Lungs

Do not try to "pump out" water from a drowning person's lungs. Do not hold the person upside down. Do not strike the person on the back. These actions can cause the person to vomit, which can aspirate fluid into the lungs and cause additional harm. Get the person on their side in the recovery position to allow fluids to drain naturally.

Why Is Prevention Always Better Than Response?

Knowing what to do in a drowning emergency is crucial, but preventing drowning in the first place is always the better path. The best rescue is the one that never needs to happen.

The Layers of Protection Model

Drowning prevention experts use a "layers of protection" model rather than relying on any single strategy. These layers include:

  • Barriers: Fences, pool gates, safety covers, and enclosures that prevent unsupervised access to water.
  • Supervision: Constant, attentive watching. No multitasking, no phones, no distractions. Eyes always on the child near water.
  • Swim Skills: Teaching children to float, swim, and understand water safety increases their ability to self-rescue in an emergency.
  • Life Jackets: For non-swimmers and weak swimmers, proper-fitting life jackets provide flotation and protection.
  • CPR and First Aid Training: Everyone who supervises water play should know CPR and rescue techniques.
  • Rules and Education: Teaching children water safety rules, buddy systems, and when to ask for adult help.

No single layer works on its own. A barrier helps, but a curious child might find a way around it. Supervision helps, but accidents happen fast. Swim skills help, but panic can override them. Each layer strengthens the others, creating a comprehensive protection system.

The Role of Swim Skills

One of the most effective prevention strategies is teaching children to swim and float. A child who can float can breathe and rest in water. A child who can swim has more options and more control. A child who understands water has less fear and better decision-making. Read our article on teaching your child to float and explore our swim lessons program to find quality instruction in your area.

Adult Supervision and Constant Watchfulness

Constant supervision is the single most effective drowning prevention strategy. This means being within arm's reach of young children, eyes always on them, no distractions. At public pools, it means not assuming the lifeguard is watching your child—you're their primary protector. Read our complete guide to drowning prevention for more strategies.

What Water Safety Should You Teach Children?

Teach children to alert a lifeguard or trusted adult immediately when someone is struggling or unexpectedly quiet. Children should not enter the water to rescue another person. Practice calling for help, staying back from the edge and following adult safety directions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does real drowning actually look like?

Real drowning is silent. A drowning person exhibits the Instinctive Drowning Response: mouth at waterline, head tilted back, arms at sides (not waving), legs motionless, no splashing or yelling, glassy unfocused eyes, and vertical body position. This typically lasts 20-60 seconds before slipping underwater.

How do you rescue someone who is drowning?

Step 1: Call 911. Step 2: Reach or throw—extend a pole, rope, or throw a flotation device. Step 3: Row—use a boat if available. Step 4: Go—enter water only if trained and equipped; untrained rescuers often drown themselves. Once out of water, place on back and begin CPR if unresponsive and not breathing.

Should untrained people attempt water rescue?

No. Untrained rescuers often panic the victim or become victims themselves. Use the reach-throw-row-go sequence: reach with a pole or rope, throw flotation, use a boat, or call 911 for trained responders. Always prioritize your own safety—a second drowning victim complicates rescue efforts.

What should you do after pulling someone from the water?

Place the person on their back, check responsiveness and breathing, call 911 immediately, and begin CPR if unresponsive and not breathing normally. Remove wet clothing to prevent hypothermia. Never assume someone is 'just fine'—secondary drowning can occur hours after submersion.

What is secondary drowning and when can it occur?

No. “Secondary,” “dry,” “delayed” and “near” drowning are not accepted medical diagnoses. Drowning means breathing impairment caused by immersion or submersion in liquid, and its outcome can be fatal or nonfatal. Breathing problems after a real drowning incident require medical attention. Call 911 for severe or labored breathing, blue or gray color, unresponsiveness, confusion with breathing distress, or a child who is difficult to wake. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the dispatcher. Anyone who needed CPR or rescue breaths after drowning needs emergency medical care.

Pool Chemical Safety Card

Every other page on this site is about the water. This card is about the container. Pool chemical injuries do not happen to a child who is swimming — they happen on the deck, in the shed and in the garage, and more than half of them happen at somebody’s home. The short version: water is the trigger, not the solvent, and the danger is the inventory rather than any single product. Four moments, one shelf audit, ten minutes.

🚨 Before Anything Else — Two Numbers on the Wall

  • 911 — if somebody is struggling to breathe, collapsed, unresponsive, or has chemical in their eyes and cannot open them. Get everyone upwind and outside first; do not go back in for the container.
  • Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222 — free, confidential, staffed twenty-four hours a day by nurses, pharmacists and doctors, with interpretation available. This is the number for a swallow, a splash, a lungful of fumes, or a “is this bad?” you are not sure about. Most calls are handled on the phone.
  • Write both on this card and put the card where the chemicals are. A number saved in one adult’s phone is not a plan for the adult who is standing there.
  • Keep the product label. Whoever you call will ask what the product was. The label, not your memory of the bucket, is the answer — which is one more reason nothing ever gets decanted into an unlabelled container.

📦 Moment 1 — The Shelf: What Is Stored Next to What

  • Acid and chlorine are stored apart — separate places, not neighbouring shelves. This is the single most important line on the card. Together they make chlorine gas, and a leaking jug does not need anyone’s help to put them together.
  • Different chlorine products are stored apart from each other too. Tablets and granular shock are not interchangeable and must never share a container, a shelf spill or a scoop — including old and fresh stock of what looks like the same product.
  • Everything is dry. Check the roof, the floor, the hose coiled overhead, the melt line where snow gets in. A few drops of water into a dry sanitiser is an ignition source, not a dilution.
  • Cool, ventilated, out of direct sunlight. Not a sealed metal deck box baking in July. Not beside the mower, the fuel can or the barbecue gas.
  • Everything is closed, in its original labelled container. No ice-cream tubs, no jam jars, no faded buckets whose label peeled off two seasons ago.
  • Liquids are stored low. A jug at eye level that splits is a face and eye injury; the same jug on the floor is a mop.
  • Locked, and out of reach of children and pets. A latch a four-year-old can work is not a lock, and a shelf a curious child can climb to is not out of reach.
The rule that makes this cluster different from everything else on this site. Everywhere else, adding a layer makes a family safer — another fence, another alarm, another trained adult. Here it is the reverse: buying one more product and setting it on the same shelf creates a hazard that neither product had on its own. The inventory is the risk. Fewer products, further apart, is the safer shed.

💨 Moment 2 — The Lid: Opening the Container

  • This is the moment that sends the most people to an emergency room. Not a spill, not a swallow — breathing in fumes or dust while opening a container. Treat the lid as the dangerous part of the job.
  • Open it outdoors, in moving air, with your face away and upwind. Never in a closed shed, a garage with the door down, or a pump room.
  • Safety goggles and gloves on before the lid comes off — goggles, not sunglasses, and not your swim goggles. Add a mask where the label calls for one. Eyes are the single most commonly injured part.
  • One container open at a time, and closed again before the next one opens. Two open lids on one bench is how two products meet without anybody deciding to mix them.
  • Read the whole label before each use, not once when you bought it. The label carries the incompatibility list, the protective equipment, the dose and the wait time. Products change; so does what is on the shelf next to them.
  • No children on the deck while any container is open. Not “playing over there”. Inside, or with another adult, for the whole job.

🥣 Moment 3 — The Scoop: Measuring and Adding

  • Chemical goes into water. Water never goes into chemical. Backwards, the reaction can be violent and even explosive. This holds every time the label tells you to pre-dissolve something.
  • Only pre-dissolve when the label says to. Otherwise the product goes into the pool the way the label directs, and a bucket never enters the picture.
  • Never mix two pool chemicals together, in a bucket or anywhere else — not with each other, not with a household cleaner, not with anything.
  • One scoop per product, labelled, dried and stored with that product. A shared scoop carries residue from the last job into the next one, which is mixing by a slower route.
  • Measure. Do not eyeball. Over-dosing is the usual cause of the skin and eye complaints that get blamed on “too much chlorine” later in the week.
  • Add one product, then wait and let the pump circulate before the next. Two doses close together meet in concentrated form in the plumbing rather than in a pool’s worth of water.
  • Pump running, every time. Chemicals fed into still water sit concentrated wherever they land and arrive in the pool all at once when the water moves again.
Why the dry products are the surprising ones. Almost everything else you own is safe to get wet. The dry sanitisers on your shelf are designed to react the instant they meet water — that is the whole point of them — which is why a damp shed, a wet scoop or a splash back into an open bucket is the hazard and not the housekeeping detail it looks like. The safest place for pool chemicals is the driest one, which is almost never the place closest to the pool.

🏊 Moment 4 — The Re-Entry: When Kids Get Back In

  • Nobody is in the water while you dose, and nobody gets in until the label’s stated wait has passed. Adding chemicals just before a swimmer entered the water is one of the three commonest ways people end up injured.
  • Set a timer rather than trusting a guess. The label gives the wait; the timer is what makes the wait real when a child is asking.
  • Test the water before the first child gets in, not after somebody complains. Stinging eyes and itchy skin are a reading you took too late.
  • Tell the other adults the pool is closed and why. Most re-entry injuries involve somebody who did not know a dose had just gone in — a partner, a grandparent, a neighbour’s child.
  • A strong chemical smell is a reason to stay out, not proof the water is clean. The sharp smell is the by-product, not the disinfectant, and a well-run pool barely smells at all.
  • Rinse-shower before swimming, and take bathroom breaks or check diapers every hour. Less sweat, sunscreen and urine in the water means less of the by-product that stings eyes and burns through your sanitiser.

🗑️ The Leftovers — Containers, Spills and Old Stock

  • Never reuse a pool chemical container for anything. Not for another chemical, not for water, not for fuel, not for storage. Follow the label for disposal.
  • Anything unlabelled goes to your local hazardous materials agency, not in the household bin and not down a drain. Call and ask; it is a routine question for them.
  • A bulging, damp, crusted, hot or oddly smelling container is an evacuate-and-call, not a tidy-up. Get people out and upwind, then phone.
  • Deal with a spill immediately, and never by sweeping one product into another. Clean it the way the label directs, with the shed open and children well away.
  • Do the whole audit at pool opening and again at closing — the two weeks of the year when most families move, open and buy the most chemicals, and two thirds of these injuries land between late May and early September.

A Locked Shed Protects the Child Who Is Not in the Water

Everything on this card guards the child who never gets wet — the one who found an open bucket or walked through a cloud of fumes. It does nothing at all for the child who is already in the pool. That child is protected by a fence, a watching adult, and a taught sequence: reach the surface, roll onto the back, float and breathe, turn, and get to an edge.

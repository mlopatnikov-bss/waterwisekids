Water Activity Swim-Skill Checklist

Tubing, paddleboarding, kayaking, snorkeling, jet skis and floating aqua parks each assume a child can already swim — and almost none of them check. The rental counter takes a card and a waiver. This sheet runs the check that nobody else does: the five water competency skills as a scored test, what each of the six activities actually assumes, and a verdict you can write down. Fill it in at a pool before the trip, not at the dock during it.

📝 Start Here — How to Use This Checklist

  • Do Section 1 in a pool, in water over your child’s head. Not in the shallow end, and not from memory. The whole point is to find out what your child can actually do rather than what you assume they can do.
  • The five skills count only in a row and unprompted. A child who can do each one separately, with an instructor saying what comes next, does not yet have the thing these activities assume.
  • A life jacket is required on all six activities regardless of the score. Nothing in Section 1 ever unlocks going without one. Section 4 is the fit test.
  • “Not yet” does not cancel the trip. It makes the activity smaller, slower, closer to shore and closer to an adult. Section 6 turns the score into that decision.
Open water is harder than the pool you tested in. Cold drains strength, current moves the target, depth hides the bottom, and distance over water is badly underestimated by adults, let alone children. Treat a pool pass as the minimum, not the margin.

🏊 1. The Skill Floor — Test All Five, In Order

In water over the head, unpromptedYesNot yetDate tested
1. Enters water over the head and returns to the surface______________________
2. Floats or treads water for one full minute______________________
3. Turns in a full circle and finds a way out______________________
4. Swims about 25 yards without stopping______________________
5. Exits the water without using a ladder______________________
All five, in a row, in one attempt______________________
Why one minute, and why 25 yards. A minute is roughly the window in which somebody notices, turns the boat around and comes back. Twenty-five yards is a proxy for closing a realistic gap to a board, a hull or a shoreline. Neither number is arbitrary and neither is a competition.
Skill 5 is the one families skip. Climbing out over an edge with no ladder is exactly what getting back onto a paddleboard or into a kayak is. If your child cannot do it at the pool wall, they cannot do it on a wet inflatable board in a breeze.

🛶 2. The Six Activities — What Each One Assumes

  • Towable tubing — commonly age 6+, confident swimmer. Assumes the rider will come off and surface calmly. Needs a firm grip and reliable response to hand signals. Check the tube’s printed age, weight and rider-capacity ratings. Keep speeds low for children — many experts suggest roughly 5–15 mph depending on age, conditions and comfort. A driver and a separate spotter, always.
  • Paddleboarding — ride-along from 3–5, kneeling from 5–7, independent 8–12. Assumes the rider falls in and climbs back on. Competent swimming before going beyond wading depth. The U.S. Coast Guard treats a paddleboard as a vessel outside a swimming area, so life jacket rules apply.
  • Kayaking and canoeing — tandem only 4–6, solo on calm water 7–9. The clearest published threshold of the six: able to swim 25 meters unassisted before soloing. Mild Class I current with instruction at 10–13. Assumes a capsize is recoverable.
  • Snorkeling — often around age 5. The gentlest entry point. Assumes a flooded mask is an annoyance, not an emergency: comfortable face-in, follows simple directions, will not panic when water gets in. Younger children in a flotation vest, held by an adult, briefly.
  • Jet skis — passenger vs operator are different questions. A passenger must be big enough to sit securely, reach and grip the handholds, and follow instructions calmly. Most states set a minimum age to operate — commonly between 14 and 16 — plus a boater-education certificate. Rules vary widely; check your state’s boating law agency.
  • Floating water parks — the one that usually checks. Reputable operators commonly require a minimum age (often around 6 or 7), a minimum height and a mandatory swim test. Life jackets normally required of every guest regardless of ability. Deep water, constant falls, slippery inflatables.
Red flag at a floating water park: no published age or height minimum, no swim test, and no lifeguards actively watching the water — as opposed to staff at the entrance taking payment. A well-run operator welcomes these questions.

Is there a minimum age or height for this activity?____________

Are life jackets provided, and in my child’s weight range?____________
Is anyone watching the water who is not also renting or driving?____________
For towed sports: is there a dedicated spotter, or just a driver?____________
What is the weather and wind policy, and who calls it?____________
Expect “no” to most of these, and do not read it as a scandal. A rental business is built to verify payment and record a waiver, not to assess a seven-year-old at a dock in July. Knowing the check will not happen is the reason you run it yourself.

🦺 4. The Life Jacket Fit Test — Every Session

  • U.S. Coast Guard approved, and foam — not inflatable. Inflatable life jackets are not approved for children under 16. Children need inherently buoyant foam that works passively, with nothing to activate.
  • Sized to your child’s current weight range. Not the weight they are growing into. Check the label against the child, today.
  • Every buckle clipped, every strap snug. A jacket worn open is a jacket not worn.
  • The lift test. Grab both shoulder straps and lift gently. If the jacket slides up past the child’s chin, it is too big — find a smaller one before anyone gets on the water.
  • Worn the whole time, not carried. Including on the dock, in the shallows, and on the ride back.
Water wings and puddle jumpers are not life jackets. They are not substitutes on any of these six activities, and the difference matters far more in open water than in a backyard pool.
What the jacket does and does not do. It buys time — the minutes between a fall and a rescue, which is the resource that runs out first. It does not teach a child to stay calm, orient toward the boat and get there. That stays a skill. Both layers, every time.

🌊 5. Rehearse These Four in a Pool First

  • Flood the mask on purpose. Standing in shallow water: breathe through the snorkel face-out, then face-in, then clear the snorkel with a sharp exhale and clear the mask by lifting its bottom edge. Never let open water be the first place a child uses a snorkel.
  • Fall off and climb back on. Remounting a paddleboard from deep water is water-competency skill 5 in disguise. Practise where the child can rest.
  • Capsize deliberately. Warm water 3–4 feet deep, no current, life jacket on, and the child chooses when to tip. Surface and orient → back float → find the boat and grab the hull → raise a hand and call out → kick the boat to shore rather than climbing back in. Never rehearse a capsize in cold water — cold water shock can incapacitate a child in under a minute.
  • Go under, come up, stay calm. The most transferable drill of the four and the one that pays off on a tube. A child who has practised surfacing calmly after an unexpected dunking handles a tumble at speed far better.
Rehearsed in a pool, each of these is a game. Met first in open water, each is a panic — and panic, not the water, is what turns an ordinary fall into an emergency.

✅ 6. The Verdict — Write It Down

Child’s name: __________________
Age: ______
Skills passed (out of 5): ______
All five in a row? ______
Activity planned: __________________
Date tested: ____________
Verdict — Go / Go smaller / Not yet: __________________
Re-test on: ____________
  • All five in a row → GO. Life jacket on, adult watching who is not also driving, spotter for anything towed, and the pool drills rehearsed.
  • Three or four → GO SMALLER. Tandem rather than solo. Calm, shallow, close to shore. No speed and nothing towed. An adult within arm’s reach the whole time.
  • Two or fewer → NOT YET for anything independent. Snorkel in a vest held by an adult, or ride along in a canoe. Nothing towed, nothing solo, nothing that puts distance between the child and a grown-up who can reach them.
The rule that scales across all six: the further a child is from the skill floor, the shorter the distance they should ever be from an adult who can get to them.
No swim skill, life jacket, method or amount of practice makes a child drown-proof. Skills change what a child can do; they do not change the supervision that child needs. Swim skills are one layer of protection alongside a properly fitted life jacket worn the whole time, constant undistracted supervision by an adult who is not also driving, a designated spotter for anything towed, and an adult trained in CPR.

The Checklist Tells You Where Your Child Is. Lessons Move Them.

The five skills in Section 1 are exactly what a decent learn-to-swim program teaches, in order, as its core curriculum — which is why the gap between “not yet” and “go” is usually one season long. Participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a substantially reduced drowning risk for young children, and drowning remains a leading cause of death for children ages 1–4. A child who starts this autumn is often a different child on the water by next July.

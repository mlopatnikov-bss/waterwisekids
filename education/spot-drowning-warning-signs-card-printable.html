How to Spot Drowning — Silent Warning Signs Card

Real drowning is silent and fast — no splashing, no yelling. These are the visible signs to watch for, and what to do the moment you see them.

The Silent Warning Signs (Watch, Don't Listen)

  • Head low in the water, mouth at water level. The face sinks and reappears at the surface as they fight for air.
  • Head tilted back with the mouth open, reaching upward to breathe.
  • Eyes glassy and empty, unable to focus — or eyes closed. A vacant, panicked stare.
  • Hair over the forehead or eyes, not pushed away.
  • Body vertical with no supporting kick, not making progress in any direction.
  • Arms pressing down at the sides, as if trying to climb an invisible ladder — they cannot wave or reach for a rope.
  • Silent. No splashing, no yelling. A swimmer who suddenly goes quiet may be in the most trouble.

Distress vs. Drowning (Catch It Early)

  • Aquatic distress (earlier stage): can still call out, wave, and grab a thrown float or pole — they can help in their own rescue. This is your best window to act calmly.
  • Active drowning (silent stage): cannot speak, wave, or reach for a line — only presses down on the water. Lasts about 20–60 seconds before going under.
  • Don't wait for obvious struggle. A swimmer who is tiring, drifting to deep water, bobbing, or going quiet is already telling you something. Move early.

The Moment You Spot It — What to Do

  • Do the "Are you OK?" check. Call the child's name. A thumbs-up, an answer, or eye contact = likely fine. A blank stare or no response = seconds count, act now.
  • Reach or throw from safety. Extend a pole, towel, or your arm; if they're too far, throw anything that floats. Don't swim out unless trained and holding flotation.
  • Get them out and call 911. Point to one person: "You — call 911 now," so it doesn't fall through the cracks.
  • Start CPR if unresponsive and you're trained. Drowning is a breathing emergency first — rescue breaths matter.
  • Get a medical check. Any child who was submerged should be evaluated by a professional, even if they seem fine afterward.
  • Teach kids the rule: "If they're quiet and still, get help — don't go in." A child's job is to find an adult, never to attempt a rescue.

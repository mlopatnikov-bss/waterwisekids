Beach Flag Color Card

The flag over the lifeguard stand is the only live report you will get on today’s water, and it can change hour to hour. Read it on arrival, read it again after lunch, and let the colour — not the waves — decide what your family does.

🚩 Read the Flag — What Each Colour Means

  • Green flag — Low hazard. Calm conditions. Swim as normal — but low hazard is not no hazard. Supervision and life jackets do not come off.
  • Yellow flag — Medium hazard. Moderate surf and/or currents. Young and weak swimmers stay in shallow water, within arm’s reach. A life-jacket day.
  • Red flag — High hazard. Strong surf and/or dangerous currents. Swimming is not recommended. Keep children out of the water entirely.
  • Double red flags — Water closed. The water is closed to the public. Do not enter. In parts of Florida this is enforcement, not advice — entering can bring a citation.
  • Purple flag — Dangerous marine life. Jellyfish, stingrays or other hazardous sea life. Flies alongside a hazard colour, not instead of it — read both flags.
  • Orange windsock — Offshore wind. Wind is blowing away from shore. No inflatables, no floats, no rafts — they leave the beach faster than an adult can swim.
  • Red over yellow — Patrolled swim zone. The lifeguards are actively watching the water between these two flags. This is the safest water on the beach — swim between the flags.
  • Black & white quartered — Surfcraft zone. Surfboards, kayaks and watercraft only. Swimmers stay out of this area entirely.
Green is the one families misread. Low hazard is not no hazard. The flag describes the surf and the current — it says nothing about whether your child can float, or whether the adult watching has looked up in the last two minutes.

⏱️ The 60-Second Arrival Check — Before Anyone Touches the Water

  • Find the flag and say the meaning out loud. Everyone hears it, including the children. If you cannot see a flag, that is information too — see the box below.
  • Ask the lifeguard what it means here, today. Thirty seconds at the tower gets you what no flag can encode: where the rip is running, which stretch they are watching, and what the purple flag is actually about.
  • Set up within sight of the lifeguard stand. Close enough that a guard can see your group, and you can see the flag without walking.
  • Pick a landmark as the meeting point and show every child. A tower number, a lifeguard stand, a beach entrance — never an umbrella or a towel, which move.
  • Life jackets on weak swimmers before they reach the sand. Coast Guard–approved and correctly fitted. Arm floats and inflatable rings are toys, not safety equipment.
  • Name the water watcher out loud and set the shift. One adult, no phone, no book, no second job. Hand the role off out loud when the shift ends.
  • Check the flag again after every break. A green morning becomes a red afternoon more often than families expect.

⛳ The Empty Flagpole — and Other Hard Stops

  • No flag flying means no information, not safe water. Most beaches fly flags only while lifeguards are on duty. A bare pole at 7 p.m. tells you the guards went home, not that the rip current did.
  • Two red flags means do not enter, full stop. In parts of Florida this is enforceable, not advisory — entering can bring a citation.
  • The system is not national. Florida runs a uniform statewide program built on four colours. Other states, counties and countries vary. Never assume your home beach’s chart applies on holiday.
  • A blue flag abroad is not a hazard signal. In Europe it is an environmental award for the beach and says nothing about today’s surf.
  • Purple flies with another colour, never instead of it. Read both. Purple under green means calm surf and stinging marine life at the same time.
  • Offshore wind cancels every inflatable. Orange windsock, or flags streaming out to sea: floats and rafts leave the beach faster than an adult can swim after them.
If a rip current takes you: do not fight it and do not swim straight for shore. Stay calm, float, and swim parallel to the beach until you are out of the pull — then angle back in. Teach this to every child before the trip, not on the day.

🧒 Make One Child the Flag Checker

  • Give the job a name. The flag checker finds the flag on arrival, says the colour and the meaning out loud, and reports any change during the day.
  • Agree the rule before you leave the house. Green and yellow: we swim but stay close. Red: sand and ankle-deep wash only. Double red: no water at all.
  • Let the flag make the decision. Because the colour decides, no adult has to be the one who says no when the waves look fun.
  • Swim near a lifeguard, always. The United States Lifesaving Association puts the chance of drowning at a beach protected by USLA-affiliated lifeguards at roughly 1 in 18 million.

The Flag Reads the Water. Swimming Ability Reads Your Child.

Flags, lifeguards and life jackets are layers you leave behind when you leave the beach — swimming ability is the layer that travels to every shoreline your child will ever stand on. Formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1–4.

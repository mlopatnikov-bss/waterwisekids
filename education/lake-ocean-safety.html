Lake & Ocean Safety: What Pool Swimmers Don't Know

A strong pool swimmer can struggle in the ocean. Understand rip currents, waves, cold water, and the hidden dangers of natural water bodies.

Quick Answer: Natural water is far less predictable than a pool — with currents, waves, cold water, drop-offs, and no lifeguards. A strong pool swimmer can still drown in a lake or ocean. Keep children in shallow water, require Coast Guard-approved life jackets, supervise constantly with a designated Water Watcher, learn to escape rip currents by swimming parallel to shore, and always obey beach warning flags.
Critical Reality
Children who are competent swimmers in pools can drown in natural water. The conditions are completely different. Never assume pool skills transfer directly to lakes and oceans.

Why is natural water different from pools?

Natural water is more dangerous than a pool because it has unpredictable currents and waves, hidden underwater hazards, changing temperatures, poor visibility, and usually no lifeguards. A pool is a controlled, predictable environment. Natural water bodies present hazards that pools don't have:

  • Unpredictable currents and waves: Water moves in multiple directions simultaneously
  • Invisible underwater hazards: Rocks, drop-offs, debris, and aquatic life
  • Changing temperature: Cold water affects muscle function and judgment — a year-round risk on the Great Lakes, where families should build skills early (browse swim lessons in Michigan)
  • Limited visibility: You can't see what's below the surface
  • No lifeguards or rescue equipment: Help may not be immediately available — know what to do in a drowning emergency
  • Weather changes: Conditions can deteriorate quickly

What are the main ocean and lake hazards?

The main natural-water hazards are rip currents, waves and undertow, cold water shock, sudden drop-offs, limited visibility, and aquatic life.

Rip Currents (Ocean)
A rip current is a narrow, powerful current flowing away from shore. Even strong swimmers can be pulled offshore and panic. If caught in a rip: Don't fight it directly. Swim parallel to shore (left or right) until you escape the current, then swim back to shore at an angle.
Waves and Undertow (Ocean)
Waves knock people down and pull them under. Undertow (water returning to sea after waves break) can drag children offshore. Larger waves are dangerous. Never turn your back on the ocean—waves can surprise you.
Cold Water
Cold water (below 60°F) causes cold water shock—involuntary gasping and loss of breathing control. Hypothermia develops quickly in children. Limit time in very cold water and use appropriate clothing or wetsuits.
Drop-offs and Uneven Bottoms (Lakes)
Lake and river bottoms can have sudden changes in depth. A child wading in shallow water may suddenly step into deep water. Never assume you know the depth.
Limited Visibility
Murky water means you can't see struggling children. You don't know what's on the bottom. Always assume visibility is limited and supervise accordingly.
Aquatic Life
Lakes and oceans have fish, stingrays, jellyfish, and in some regions, sharks. Understand the local wildlife and take appropriate precautions. Most aquatic animals avoid humans, but respect their space.

Why are life jackets essential in natural water?

Life jackets are essential in natural water because currents, waves, and cold can overwhelm even strong swimmers; a U.S. Coast Guard-approved PFD provides reliable backup protection. For any child in natural water, a U.S. Coast Guard-approved personal flotation device (PFD) is strongly recommended:

  • Non-negotiable for weak swimmers: Any child who isn't a strong, confident swimmer should wear a PFD in natural water
  • Highly recommended for all children: Even strong swimmers can panic in unfamiliar water
  • Boating: Life jackets are essential and often legally required
  • Beaches and lake docks: Keep life jackets accessible
  • Fit matters: The jacket must be the correct size and all straps must be fastened

What are the key beach and ocean safety rules?

Key beach safety rules: check conditions and lifeguard coverage before you go, keep children in shallow water, supervise constantly, use life jackets, never turn your back on the ocean, and know how to escape a rip current. If taking children to the beach:

Before You Go

  • Check weather and water conditions (temperature, waves, currents)
  • Identify the lifeguard stations and whether guards are on duty
  • Learn about hazards specific to that beach (strong currents, marine life, rocks)
  • Check if swimming is allowed in that area

At the Beach

  • Stay in shallow water only. Keep children in water where their feet touch bottom
  • Supervise constantly. One adult watching only the water, no distractions
  • Use life jackets. Non-swimmers and weak swimmers must wear PFDs
  • Stay away from piers, jetties, and rocks. These create dangerous currents and hazards
  • Never turn your back on the ocean. Waves can surprise you
  • Watch for changing conditions. Weather and waves can change quickly
  • Understand rip currents. Know how to escape if caught in one
  • Use the buddy system. Children should never be alone, even in shallow water

What are the key lake and river safety rules?

For lakes and rivers, learn the depth profile and currents before swimming, start in shallow water, require life jackets, supervise closely because of murky visibility, use the buddy system, and stay away from dams and moving water.

Before Going to a Lake or River

  • Know the depth profile of where you'll be swimming
  • Check for currents (rivers always have currents)
  • Identify hazards (rocks, drop-offs, debris)
  • Check for water quality warnings or closures
  • Know if the area is monitored by lifeguards

At the Lake or River

  • Wear life jackets. Essential for rivers and unfamiliar lakes
  • Start in shallow water only. Never assume depth
  • Supervise extremely carefully. Limited visibility means you need even more vigilance
  • Use the buddy system always. Nobody swims alone
  • Teach water awareness. Point out currents, drop-offs, and hazards
  • Stay away from dams, locks, and moving water. Extremely dangerous
  • Don't assume "natural water" is safe. Many lakes and rivers are more dangerous than you'd expect

What are the most important lake safety tips?

The seven lake safety tips that prevent the most drownings are: put young and weak swimmers in a Coast Guard–approved life jacket, never let anyone swim alone, wade in feet-first instead of diving, name a dedicated water watcher, stay inside a marked swim area, get out at the first rumble of thunder, and treat alcohol and open water as incompatible.

Lakes fail people differently than pools do. The water is opaque, so a struggling child is invisible from a few feet away; the bottom drops off without warning; the temperature below the surface stays cold enough to trigger cold-water shock even in August; and there is rarely a lifeguard. The tips below are ordered by how much risk each one removes. Campsites add remoteness to every one of those problems — see water safety for family camping trips.

  1. Life jackets on the weak swimmers, every time. A properly fitted, Coast Guard–approved life jacket is the single highest-impact thing you can do at a lake. Foam water wings and inflatable toys are not flotation devices — see why pool noodles and floats fail in open water.
  2. Never swim alone, and never let a child swim unsupervised. Assign one adult as the designated water watcher with no phone, no book and no conversation, and rotate every 15–20 minutes.
  3. Enter feet-first, always. Lake bottoms hide rocks, stumps, sandbars and sudden drop-offs. Never dive head-first into water you have not personally waded through.
  4. Stay inside the marked swim area. Buoyed areas are placed where the bottom is known, boat traffic is excluded and depth changes gradually.
  5. Respect the cold under the surface. Lakes stratify: the top may be 80°F while the layer a few feet down is in the 50s. That gap is enough to cause an involuntary gasp and rapid loss of muscle control.
  6. Get out at the first thunder. Lightning is a leading cause of open-water fatalities. Leave the water at the first rumble and wait 30 minutes after the last one.
  7. No alcohol for anyone supervising or swimming. Alcohol is a factor in a large share of adult and adolescent open-water drownings.

Before a lake day, run through the same competency questions we use in the water competency skills checklist — can your child enter water over their head, turn, float for a minute, and get themselves back to the edge? Open water is where those five skills stop being theoretical. Our child drowning statistics dashboard breaks down how natural-water deaths differ from pool deaths by age. How dangerous is cold water?

Cold water below 60°F can trigger involuntary gasping (cold shock) that causes drowning within seconds, and children develop hypothermia faster than adults. Cold water (below 60°F) presents unique dangers:

  • Cold shock response: When first entering very cold water, uncontrollable gasping can cause drowning within seconds
  • Hypothermia: Core body temperature drops, affecting consciousness and coordination
  • Children lose heat faster: Due to smaller body size and less fat, children develop hypothermia more quickly
  • Limit time in very cold water: Don't stay in water below 50°F for extended periods
  • Use wetsuits or rashguards: Extra insulation helps maintain body temperature
  • Get out and warm up: Watch for shivering, slurred speech, and confusion

How do you stay safe boating and doing water sports?

Life jackets are non-negotiable for boating. For a comprehensive guide to taking kids on boats, see our boating safety with children article. Key rules:

  • All children must wear Coast Guard-approved PFDs at all times. Not optional.
  • Fit the jacket properly (all straps fastened, snug but not restricting breathing)
  • Supervise constantly—even children in life jackets can get into trouble
  • For water skiing, wakeboarding, and jet skiing: Maximum supervision + PFD
  • Know the boating rules and etiquette for your area

How do you read beach warning flags and conditions?

Flag colours are the fastest read on conditions you cannot judge from the sand. Our full guide to beach warning flags and what every colour means covers the purple and double-red flags most families never learn.

Beach warning flags signal hazard levels: green means calm, yellow means moderate hazards, red means high hazard for strong swimmers only, and double red means the water is closed. Beaches and public water areas post warning flags and signs:

  • Green flag: Safe conditions, calm water
  • Yellow flag: Moderate hazards (moderate waves, moderate current). Caution advised.
  • Red flag: High hazard (strong currents, rough water, dangerous conditions). Strong swimmers only; children should not enter.
  • Double red flags: Water is CLOSED. Do not enter.

Always respect warning flags and signs. They're based on real hazard assessment.

How do you teach children about natural water?

Teach children that natural water needs its own rules: point out hazards as you see them, explain why life jackets matter, model cautious behavior, and expand boundaries only as skills grow. Help your child understand that natural water is different:

  • "The ocean/lake is different from the pool. We need different safety rules here."
  • Point out hazards as you see them: "See that current pulling the water toward shore? That's powerful."
  • Explain why rules matter: "We wear life jackets in natural water because the conditions are unpredictable."
  • Model respect for water: Show that you're cautious and aware
  • Practice in shallow water first, then gradually expand boundaries as skills improve. As they mature, teens need additional guidance about peer pressure and independent water activities

Can pool floats be used safely in lakes and oceans?

No. Pool floats — inflatable rings, loungers, unicorns, noodles, armbands and puddle jumpers — should not be used as flotation in a lake, river or ocean. They are designed for still, contained, shallow water with an edge you can always reach, and every one of those assumptions fails in open water.

Three specific failure modes, all of them common:

  • Wind carries them out. An inflatable with a child on it is a sail. An offshore breeze that feels pleasant on the beach moves a float away from shore faster than most adults can swim, and the child cannot paddle back against it. This is the single most frequent open-water inflatable rescue.
  • They separate from the child. A pool float is something a child sits on or holds, not something strapped to them. One wave, one wake from a passing boat, one moment of imbalance, and the child is in deep water and the float is three feet away and moving.
  • They are not life jackets and are not tested as such. Inflatable toys carry no U.S. Coast Guard approval and are not designed to turn an unconscious wearer face-up or to keep a tiring child's airway clear. The word “float” in the product name is doing a lot of unearned work.

The replacement is straightforward: a properly fitted, U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket, worn in and near open water regardless of swimming ability. If you want a toy in the water too, that is fine — the life jacket is what is keeping the child up, and the toy is a toy. Our life jacket sizing guide covers the fit check that most parents skip, and are puddle jumpers safe? explains why the most popular pool device is a particularly poor open-water choice.

Do lakes have undercurrents and undertow?

Yes — lakes have currents, and the danger is that they are invisible on a surface that looks completely calm. The words parents use for this get tangled, so it helps to separate them:

  • Undertow is the seaward pull of water returning down a beach slope after a wave breaks. It tugs at your legs, it is strongest in the surf zone, and it is generally short-range — unpleasant and destabilizing for a small child rather than the thing that carries swimmers out to sea.
  • Rip currents are the thing that carries swimmers out. Narrow, fast channels of water flowing away from shore, they occur in oceans and in large lakes — the Great Lakes generate them routinely, and they kill people there every year. Swim parallel to shore to escape one, never against it.
  • Structural currents are lake-specific and badly underestimated. Water moving past piers, breakwalls, jetties, dam outflows and river mouths creates strong, persistent pull in exactly the places children like to jump in. On the Great Lakes these are a leading drowning setting.

Add the two hazards unique to lakes and rivers that behave like currents to the person caught in them: cold-water layers, where a warm surface hides water cold enough to cause involuntary gasping a few feet down, and river current under a calm surface, which is why a slow-looking creek can pin a child against a submerged branch.

The practical rules follow from that. Swim only at designated, ideally guarded beaches; check the local flag or forecast before entering — the National Weather Service issues rip current risk statements for the Great Lakes as well as the coasts; keep children away from piers, breakwalls and river mouths entirely; and put a life jacket on any child who is not a strong swimmer, because none of these currents announce themselves.

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes natural water different and more dangerous than pools?

Natural water (lakes, rivers, oceans) lacks lifeguards, has variable depth and currents, contains cold water that can cause shock, features unpredictable waves and tides, and may have underwater hazards. Visibility is poor, and rescue response is slower. These factors make constant supervision, life jackets, and advanced skills essential.

What are the main hazards in lakes and rivers?

Lake and river hazards include: sudden depth changes, strong currents and undertows, cold water temperatures, boat traffic, underwater obstacles, murky visibility, and lack of lifeguard protection. Currents can be especially dangerous, moving children away from shore faster than they can swim.

How can I recognize ocean hazards like rip currents?

Rip currents appear as discolored water channels moving away from shore, gaps in wave patterns, or areas of turbulent, choppy water. Beach warning flags and lifeguard postings indicate hazards. If caught in a rip current, swim parallel to shore until you escape the current, then return to shore.

Should my child wear a life jacket in natural water?

Yes, especially for non-confident swimmers. Life jackets are strongly recommended in natural water even for children who can swim, as currents, waves, and cold water can overwhelm even good swimmers. A properly fitted Type III jacket provides essential backup protection.

What should I teach children about natural water safety?

Teach children to: always have adult supervision, wear life jackets in moving water, never turn their back on waves, check water conditions before entering, understand current and tide patterns, buddy up with another person, and report warning flags to you. Emphasize that natural water is unpredictable and requires constant vigilance.

Can pool floats be used safely in lakes and oceans?

No. Inflatable rings, loungers, noodles and armbands are designed for still, contained, shallow water. In open water an offshore breeze turns a float into a sail and carries a child out faster than an adult can swim, a single wave or boat wake separates the child from the float, and none of these toys carry U.S. Coast Guard approval or are designed to keep an airway clear. Use a properly fitted Coast Guard-approved life jacket instead.

Do lakes have undercurrents?

Yes. Lakes have rip currents, which occur routinely in the Great Lakes and cause deaths there every year, plus strong structural currents around piers, breakwalls, jetties, dam outflows and river mouths. Undertow specifically means the short-range seaward pull after a wave breaks. Lakes also hide cold-water layers a few feet below a warm surface. None of these are visible from shore, which is why designated guarded beaches and life jackets matter more in open water than in pools.

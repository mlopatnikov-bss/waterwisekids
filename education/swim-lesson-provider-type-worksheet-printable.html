Swim Lesson Provider Type Worksheet

Before you can compare three swim programs you have to decide which three, and that means choosing between six quite different products — not six prices for the same one. The kind of provider does not predict how well your child will be taught. It does predict whether lessons run in February, how warm the water is, how long the wait is, and what happens when your child misses a Tuesday. Fill this in for three providers, one phone call each.

📝 Start Here — How to Use This Worksheet

  • Pick three providers of at least two different types. Comparing three community centers tells you which YMCA is nearest. Comparing a community center, an independent school and a host-pool program tells you what your town actually offers.
  • Ask the supply questions before the money questions. A price you cannot buy is not a price. Find out whether there is a place, and when the program runs, before you compare what it costs.
  • The model is not the vetting. Nothing on this sheet tells you whether a program is safe or well taught. Section 6 does that, and it has to be asked of every provider regardless of type.
  • One sitting, three calls, about twenty minutes. Doing it in one go is the point — a comparison spread over a month becomes a comparison of which conversation you remember best.
The single most useful thing to know before you start. The variation between two YMCAs, or between two private swim schools, is wider than the average difference between the categories. The label on the door predicts access and logistics reliably, and predicts teaching quality hardly at all.

🏛️ 1. The Six Models — One Line Each

  • Nonprofit community center (YMCA, JCC). Usually indoor and year-round, published level system, most likely to offer sliding-scale assistance. Watch for: membership attached to the fee, larger classes, registration windows.
  • Municipal parks and recreation. City or county pool, often outdoor and seasonal, usually the least expensive lessons in town. Watch for: an eight- to ten-week season, and a registration morning that fills in an hour.
  • Independent swim school. One owner, one or two sites, frequently a purpose-built warm pool and small ratios. Watch for: waitlists, and whether the owner is still on the deck.
  • National franchise. Branded curriculum and standardised pool, run by a local owner. Consistency and transferable levels are real benefits. Watch for: quality that follows the local owner, not the brand.
  • ISR or survival-swim instructor. Short one-to-one sessions several days a week for several weeks, aimed at self-rescue rather than strokes. A different product, not a cheaper one. Watch for: the calendar and cost are front-loaded.
  • Private instructor at a host pool. An individual or a program renting time at a health club, hotel, apartment or school pool. Often the only year-round option in a town with no swim school. Watch for: verify the instructor yourself — there may be no organisation behind them.
A seventh variant: a club or HOA program behaves much like a municipal one with a membership gate in front of it. Score it in the municipal row and note the gate.

📏 3. The Six Structural Facts

Temperature matters most for the youngest children. A pool built for adults swimming lengths is a different temperature from a pool built for a four-year-old standing still, and a cold child spends the lesson shivering rather than learning. Ask for a number; an adjective is not an answer.
Seasonal staffing is not a criticism, it is a prediction. A summer program staffed by college instructors is doing exactly what it is built to do. It does mean your child’s teacher is more likely to change, which matters more for a nervous four-year-old than for a confident eight-year-old.

⌛ 4. Supply and Calendar — Ask Before Price

The questions that decide whether this is realABC
Is there a place available now? (y/n)__________________
If not, how long is the wait?__________________
How does registration open? (rolling / window / date)__________________
Next date we could actually start__________________
Lessons we would have 12 months from now__________________
Drive time, door to door, at lesson time__________________
The second-to-last row is the whole comparison. A low price per lesson on a program that runs eight weeks and has a six-month waitlist delivers no lessons this year. A higher price you can start next Tuesday delivers forty. There is no price at which the first is better value.
Ask every provider about financial assistance — including the expensive ones. Community centers commonly run sliding-scale programs, many municipal programs hold reduced or free places, and independent schools and franchises sometimes keep scholarship spots they never advertise. It is one question and almost nobody asks it.

The last row is the only cost figure worth comparing. It folds season length, waitlist and make-up policy into one number, which is exactly what a monthly price hides.

🛡️ 6. The Six Safety Questions — Ask Every Model

  • What is the actual ratio in my child’s class, on my child’s day? Not the ratio on the website, which is often the beginner level only or the maximum rather than the typical.
  • What certification do the instructors hold, issued by whom, and who checks it is current? “Certified instructors” is not a certification.
  • Is someone watching the water who is not also teaching? A teacher covering two groups is not supervision, whatever the sign on the wall says.
  • How long has my child’s instructor worked here? Turnover is invisible on every website and it is what a young child feels first.
  • What does a child have to demonstrate to move up a level? A specific answer is a sign of a real curriculum; a vague one is a sign of a subscription.
  • What is the written policy for missed lessons and cancellation? Ask for it in writing, not in the summary a receptionist gives you.
How the questions land is itself information. None of these are secrets and a well-run program of any type hears them every week. Plain answers are a good sign. Irritation, vagueness or a redirect to the website is a different kind of answer, and it is the same in a community center as in a franchise.

Five or six, book a visit this week. Three or four, visit and watch a class before committing. Two or fewer usually means the model is wrong for your family rather than the program being bad — go back to section 1 and add a type you have not called.
Do not let the comparison delay the lessons. The worst outcome of this worksheet is a child who is not in the water while it is being filled in. Book something reasonable, keep asking the questions, and move if the answers do not hold up.

⭐ 8. The Lines That Do Not Change With the Model

  • No program makes a child drown-proof. Not at any age, any price, or in any model. Lessons change what your child can do; they do not change the supervision your child needs.
  • Lessons are one layer alongside the others. Four-sided pool fencing, constant undistracted supervision within arm’s reach of a young or non-swimming child, properly fitted life jackets in open water, and an adult trained in CPR.
  • Attendance is what produces a swimmer. Distance, timing and cost decide attendance. A good-enough program your family will actually get to beats a better one you will start skipping in November.
  • Ask about assistance everywhere. Every model has some form of help available and it is almost never volunteered.
Why the calendar is the safety question. Drowning remains a leading cause of death for young children, and participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a substantially reduced risk. That association is built on lessons a child actually attends — which is why season length, waitlist and drive time belong on a safety worksheet at all.

The Model Narrows the List. A Visit Settles It.

Once two or three providers have survived this worksheet, the next step is to see a class from the deck and ask the six safety questions out loud. Participation in formal swim lessons is associated with a substantially reduced drowning risk for young children, and drowning remains a leading cause of death for children ages 1–4 — so the fastest route to a safer child is a decent program your family will actually attend, starting soon.

Night Swim Safety Checklist

Darkness takes away the two things an adult normally relies on at a pool — a body visible under the surface and a change in the sound of splashing. This one-page checklist replaces both with a routine: check the light, name the watcher, count everyone in, and count everyone out.

🌙 Before Anyone Gets In — Settle These Six First

  • Turn the lights on and look. Underwater lights, deck lights, and any path lighting on and working. Stand at the deep end: can you see the bottom and the main drain from the deck? If not, the swim does not happen.
  • Name the Water Watcher out loud. One sober adult, no phone, no cooking, no host duties. Set the shift length before it starts — 15 or 20 minutes is realistic.
  • Count everyone in. Say the number out loud and write it on this sheet. You cannot count heads in the dark from memory.
  • Put rescue equipment in the light. Reaching pole and ring buoy at the poolside, both inside the lit area, both reachable without stepping into shadow.
  • Charge a phone and leave it on the deck. With the Water Watcher’s backup, not the Water Watcher. It is for calling 911, not for scrolling.
  • Check the gate, the latch, and the alarm. The barrier matters more after dark, not less — a child who slips out at 10 p.m. is missed later than one who slips out at 2 p.m.

💡 Lighting Check — Can You Actually See the Bottom?

  • The main drain is visible from the deck. This is the single best test of whether a pool is lit for swimming. If the drain disappears, so does a swimmer on the bottom.
  • No dark corners. Walk the full perimeter. Steps, benches, the deep-end corners, and under any overhang are where a still swimmer goes unseen longest.
  • No glare across the surface. A light aimed flat across the water turns the surface into a mirror. Aim lights down into the water or shield them.
  • Deck and path are lit too. Most night-swim injuries are slips and trips on a wet deck, not drownings. Light the route to the door and to the bathroom.
  • A backup light source is on the deck. A charged flashlight or lantern within reach. Bulbs and breakers fail at the worst possible time.
  • Underwater lights are on a GFCI and have been inspected. Damaged pool light fixtures and unbonded metal are the classic electric shock drowning setup. If a light flickers, buzzes, or has cracked housing, keep everyone out until an electrician clears it.
  • Water is clear, not cloudy. Cloudy water plus darkness is worse than either alone. If the water was hazy this afternoon, do not swim in it tonight.

👁️ Supervision Rules That Change After Dark

  • Water Watcher has no second job. Not grilling, not hosting, not putting a younger sibling to bed. One job.
  • Weak swimmers get an adult in the water. Touch supervision means within arm’s reach. From a deck chair in the dark, arm’s reach is a fiction.
  • Everyone swims with a buddy. Buddies check on each other by name, out loud, at every headcount.
  • Headcount every 15 minutes, out loud, by name. Silence is the sound of drowning. A verbal headcount is the only reliable way to notice it after dark.
  • Bright or light-colored swimsuits, and glow sticks on non-swimmers. Dark blue and black suits vanish against a pool bottom even in daylight. At night they are invisible.
  • No alcohol for any supervising adult. The CDC reports alcohol is involved in up to 70% of deaths associated with water recreation among adolescents and adults. If the adults are drinking, the pool is closed.
  • No diving, no rough play, no breath-holding games. Depth is harder to judge in artificial light, and hyperventilation games kill strong swimmers.
  • Teens swim under the same rules as everyone else. Unsupervised night swimming by teens — often with alcohol and dares — is a distinct risk pattern, not a minor one.

🔒 When Swimming Ends — the Shutdown Routine

  • Call everyone out and count heads out loud. Compare to the number you wrote down at the start. Do not accept “everyone’s out” from anyone.
  • Walk the shallow end and the deep end before the lights go off. Look at the bottom, not at the surface. Do this while the pool is still lit.
  • Take every float, noodle, and toy out of the water. A toy floating in a pool is a documented lure for a toddler who wakes up later.
  • Latch the gate and test it. Self-closing, self-latching, latch above a child’s reach. Push on it to confirm.
  • Arm the alarm or secure the cover. Door alarms, gate alarms, surface alarms, safety cover — whichever layers you have, engage them.
  • Lock the house door that leads to the pool. Most young children who drown got to the water through the house, not over the fence.
  • Do a final headcount of children inside the house. The half hour after the adults believe swimming is over is the highest-risk window of the whole evening.

🚫 Call It Off If Any of These Are True

  • The lights are out, dim, or damaged. No exceptions and no flashlights-as-a-substitute.
  • Thunder has been heard in the last 30 minutes. Wait 30 minutes from the last thunder before anyone gets back in.
  • Any supervising adult has been drinking. Decide this in daylight so nobody has to argue about it at 9 p.m.
  • Nobody will take the Water Watcher shift. If no one volunteers, that is your answer.
  • A non-swimmer is present and no adult will get in with them. Life jackets help, but they are not supervision.
  • The water is cloudy or the drain is not visible. You cannot supervise what you cannot see.
  • Everyone is tired. Fatigue is the quiet cause of most late-evening incidents — in the swimmers and in the watcher.

🔢 Tonight’s Headcount — Write It Down

Swimmers in: ______
Swimmers out: ______
Numbers match? Y / N
Say both numbers out loud. A written count at the start and a spoken count at the end is the only part of this list that catches a missing child in the dark. Everything else is prevention; this is detection.

The Best Light in the Pool Is a Child Who Can Swim

Lighting, fences, and headcounts are layers — swimming ability is the layer that travels with your child to every pool they will ever visit. Formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1–4.

What Private Equity Ownership Means for Your Child's Swim Lessons

When you walk into a swim school, you assume you're picking a small business. Increasingly, you're enrolling at a 200-location franchise of a private-equity portfolio company. Here's what that ownership structure actually changes about your child's lessons.

Quick Answer: Most large swim school chains are now backed by private equity (Youth Enrichment Brands, Morgan Stanley Capital Partners, Level 5 Capital, and others). PE ownership tends to drive curriculum standardization, technology investment, and faster expansion — but also pricing pressure, instructor pay caps, and franchise consolidation. Quality at any specific location still depends primarily on the local franchisee and instructors, not the corporate parent.

🏢 How Has Private Equity Rolled Up the Swim School Industry?

Private equity firms have spent the last decade acquiring swim school chains because parents spend reliably on children’s activities and the fragmented industry was ripe for roll-up. Over the last decade, private equity capital has flowed aggressively into youth enrichment categories — tutoring, music lessons, gymnastics, martial arts, and swim. The thesis is consistent: parents in dual-income households spend reliably on children's activities, the demand is recession-resilient, and a fragmented industry of single-location operators creates a rollup opportunity. Buy ten or twenty independent schools, standardize them under one brand, layer on shared back-office systems and marketing, and the combined entity is worth more than the sum of its parts.

📊 Context: By industry estimates, more than 60% of named swim school franchise locations in the United States are now operated under PE-backed parent companies. The remaining 40% are independent local schools, YMCA / municipal programs, or community-based nonprofits.

Swim schools fit this thesis especially well. Capital requirements are high (a custom-built warm-water indoor pool can cost millions), demand is structurally underserved (drowning is a leading cause of death for children under five), and parents are willing to commit to 12+ months of recurring tuition. Once a private-equity-backed platform reaches scale, it becomes the buyer of choice for independent operators looking to retire or exit.

By 2026, the largest names in the category are predominantly PE-backed. Youth Enrichment Brands, which acquired the swim-school franchisor Streamline Brands in 2022, operates SafeSplash, SwimLabs, Swimtastic, Saf-T-Swim, Miller Swim School and Sharkie’s Academy across hundreds of locations — and Youth Enrichment Brands is itself a portfolio company of the private equity firm Roark Capital, which lists it among its current investments. Streamline itself has since rebranded to SafeSplash, and the former AquaKids, The Swim Revolution and Fish Tails locations were folded into that brand. Morgan Stanley Capital Partners completed an investment in Emler Swim School in October 2022, and Emler now anchors Emler Swim & Gym, a multi-brand platform that reports 20 brands, 71 swim schools and 14 gymnastics centres across 19 states — so several consumer-facing swim brands you would not recognise as Emler share the same owner. Goldfish Swim School and Big Blue Swim School are both backed by Level 5 Capital. Hubbard Family Swim School remains privately held but operates with similar professional management. The handful of major chains without PE involvement are increasingly the exception, not the rule.

The chain usually has more links than the sign outside

Take the largest swim-school family in our directory as a worked example, reading from the pool deck upward. Your child’s class is taught at a SafeSplash or SwimLabs location, which is typically owned by a local franchisee — often a family that bought one or two territories. The brand itself belongs to Youth Enrichment Brands, a franchisor platform. And Youth Enrichment Brands belongs to Roark Capital, an Atlanta-based private equity firm whose portfolio is mostly franchise and multi-unit businesses.

Two things are worth noticing about that chain. The first is that the private equity firm is four steps away from the instructor teaching your child, which is why ownership rarely changes what a single lesson feels like — the franchisee still hires the staff and runs the schedule. The second is that Youth Enrichment Brands is not a swim company. On Roark’s own portfolio listing it is described as a youth activities company serving more than a million children a year, and its named brands include School of Rock, US Sports Camps, i9 Sports and U.S. Baseball Academy alongside SafeSplash. Swim is one line of a youth-enrichment business, not the whole business.

How to read a chain like this: none of these four layers is a red or green flag on its own. A franchisee can be excellent under a PE-owned brand and mediocre under an independent one. What the chain tells you is where decisions get made — curriculum and pricing frameworks usually come from the franchisor, while staffing, make-up policy, and how a complaint gets handled almost always stay local.

🔄 What Actually Changes After a Private Equity Acquisition?

After a private equity acquisition, three things change most predictably: curriculum standardization, more frequent tuition increases, and centralized back-office operations. Three things tend to change predictably after a private equity firm acquires a swim school chain. First is curriculum standardization. PE owners view variable curricula across locations as operational risk and brand inconsistency. They typically invest in centralizing the lesson plan, the level progression, and the instructor training materials so a parent can move between locations and expect a similar experience. This is good for consistency and bad for the kind of local instructor judgment that experienced teachers may have been bringing.

Second is pricing discipline. PE-backed chains conduct regular pricing studies, raise tuition more aggressively than independent owners typically would, and add ancillary fees (registration, equipment, assessment) to grow per-customer revenue. Lessons that cost $100/month at an independent school often migrate to $130–$160/month within two to three years of acquisition. The increase is usually modest in any single year — often 6–10% — but it compounds.

Third is technology investment. PE owners want operational data: enrollment trends, instructor productivity, customer churn, marketing channel performance. They invest in scheduling apps, parent-facing progress trackers, and CRM systems. Parents at acquired schools often see new app launches, automated reminders, and progress emails within the first year — features that are genuinely useful, but designed primarily to reduce churn and lift lifetime value rather than to teach swimming better.

🧑‍🏫 How Does PE Ownership Affect Instructors (and Your Child)?

PE ownership raises the floor on instructor training but can increase turnover â and the instructor in the water is the single most important variable in your child’s lessons. The single most important quality variable in a swim lesson is the instructor in the water. PE ownership influences instructor quality in mixed ways. Standardized training programs raise the floor — even a brand-new instructor at a PE-backed chain has a documented training pathway and a consistent curriculum to deliver. This is genuinely valuable for parents who would otherwise be enrolling at a school with no formal training process.

But cost discipline at the corporate level translates into wage discipline at the pool deck. Many PE-backed chains pay instructors $14–$18 per hour in markets where local cost of living would justify $20–$25. Higher turnover follows: industry estimates suggest annual instructor turnover at major chains regularly exceeds 50%. From a parent's perspective, this means your child may have several different teachers across a year of lessons, with each transition costing some progress.

Independent and family-owned schools (and well-managed franchise locations) often pay above corporate minimums to retain experienced instructors, because the local owner sees instructor quality as the core of the business rather than as a cost line item. When evaluating any swim school, ask directly: what is the average tenure of your instructors? A school where most teachers have been there 3+ years is structurally different from one where most are first-year hires.

🔍 How Can You Spot a Truly Independent Swim School?

The clearest signal of an independent swim school is a single-location brand name, locally made decisions, and an owner you can actually meet. Several signals indicate that a swim school is genuinely independent rather than a PE-backed franchise. The clearest is the brand name — if the school operates under a single-location name ("Riverside Swim Academy," "Coastal Aquatics," "Mrs. Elena's Swim School") rather than a recognizable national brand, it's much more likely to be independent. National brand names with hundreds of locations (Goldfish, Aqua-Tots, Big Blue, SafeSplash, Swimtastic, SwimLabs) are predominantly franchise operations under corporate parents; Emler’s schools are corporate-owned rather than franchised.

A second signal is the website footer. Independent schools typically don't have legal language about "each location independently owned and operated." Franchise networks almost always do. The footer of a website often contains the disclosure that reveals corporate ownership structure even when the school's marketing presents as local.

A third signal is the founder's presence. At independent schools, the founder or owner is usually visible on the About page, accessible by email, often present at the pool. At PE-backed franchises, leadership is corporate and abstract; the local franchisee may or may not be visible. Neither is inherently better — a well-managed franchise can deliver excellent lessons — but parents who specifically value local ownership should look for the founder presence.

Finally, ask directly during your tour: "Who owns this school? Are you part of a national franchise?" The answer tells you what you're buying. There is no shame in either answer; what matters is that you make an informed choice.

❓ What Questions Should You Ask Any Swim School Before Enrolling?

Ask about instructor tenure, recent tuition increases, real class ratios, and who makes decisions — these questions surface quality regardless of ownership. Whether you're enrolling at a PE-backed franchise or a local independent, these questions surface what actually matters for your child. What is the average tenure of instructors at this location? What was the most recent tuition increase, and how often do prices change? Are class sizes capped, and what is the actual ratio in my child's level? Who is the owner of this specific location, and how reachable are they if I have a concern?

Ownership structure aside, the stakes of choosing well are high. The CDC reports that drowning is the #1 cause of unintentional death for children ages 1–4, and the American Academy of Pediatrics cites research showing that formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by 88% for that age group. Whichever school you choose, look for alignment with the American Red Cross water safety framework and the NDPA layered-protection approach.

Ask also about curriculum flexibility: does the corporate curriculum allow my child's instructor to adapt to their pace, or are advancement criteria rigid? Standardization helps consistency but can frustrate parents whose child is ready to move ahead, or struggles in a specific area. The school's answer reveals whether the local team has authority to make judgment calls — a meaningful indicator of how the corporate-versus-local balance plays out at the pool deck.

Finally, ask what happens when an instructor leaves. The typical response at a high-quality school is a transition plan: you're notified, the new instructor is briefed on your child's progress, and there's continuity in the lesson plan. The typical response at a poorly managed school is silence followed by a new face on the deck. The answer here is one of the strongest predictors of overall service quality.

💡 Is Private Equity Ownership Bad for Swim Lessons?

No — private equity ownership is not categorically bad for swim lessons, but it changes the trade-offs parents should evaluate. PE ownership is not categorically bad for parents. It has expanded swim lesson capacity in many markets, raised the floor on instructor training, brought useful technology to a category that historically used paper logbooks, and created standardized environments where parents can predict the experience. Many families have excellent multi-year relationships with PE-backed schools and excellent outcomes for their children.

What PE ownership does change is the incentive structure. Corporate priorities — same-store revenue growth, customer lifetime value, EBITDA margins — sit alongside the instructional mission, and sometimes in tension with it. Parents who understand this dynamic can make better decisions: they can choose franchises with strong local management, push back on price increases that aren't matched by quality investment, and recognize when a school is optimizing for retention metrics rather than for their child's actual progress.

The most informed parents treat the corporate-versus-independent question as one input into a larger decision. The instructor in the pool with your child this week matters more than any quarterly business plan in a corporate office. Choose for that — and stay informed about who actually owns the school where you're spending $100+ per month.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which major swim school chains are owned by private equity?

Several of the largest swim school franchise networks are PE-backed: Youth Enrichment Brands, which acquired the swim-school franchisor Streamline Brands in 2022, owns SafeSplash, SwimLabs, Swimtastic, Saf-T-Swim, Miller Swim School and Sharkie’s Academy, and Youth Enrichment Brands is itself a portfolio company of the private equity firm Roark Capital. Morgan Stanley Capital Partners completed an investment in Emler Swim School in October 2022, and Emler now anchors Emler Swim & Gym, a multi-brand platform that reports 20 brands, 71 swim schools and 14 gymnastics centres across 19 states — so several consumer-facing swim brands you would not recognise as Emler share the same owner. Goldfish Swim School and Big Blue Swim School are backed by Level 5 Capital. Hubbard Family Swim School operates with professional management while remaining privately held. Many regional chains are also PE-backed; ask any school directly about its ownership structure.

Does private equity ownership mean my child gets worse swim lessons?

Not necessarily. PE-backed chains often have stronger instructor training programs, more consistent curricula across locations, better technology, and more predictable operations than poorly managed independent schools. The tradeoffs are higher pricing, higher instructor turnover from cost discipline, and less local flexibility. Quality at any specific location still depends primarily on the local franchisee, the instructors they hire and retain, and the culture they build — not the corporate parent.

How can I tell if a swim school is independently owned vs. a franchise?

Look for these signals: (1) a single-location brand name rather than a recognizable national chain; (2) website footer language without 'each location independently owned and operated' franchise disclosures; (3) a visible founder or owner profile on the About page; (4) direct accessibility of the owner by email or phone. Or simply ask during your tour: 'Who owns this school? Are you part of a national franchise?' The answer is straightforward and reveals the ownership structure.

Why do PE-backed swim schools raise prices more than independent schools?

Private equity firms expect investment returns and routinely conduct pricing studies to identify what the market will bear. PE-backed chains typically raise tuition 6–10% per year — modest in any single year but meaningful over time. Independent owners often hold prices steady longer because they have closer relationships with families, lower investor return expectations, and aren't measured on quarterly revenue growth. This is one of the structural differences parents should expect when comparing options.

What questions should I ask a swim school about its instructors and ownership?

Ask: What is the average tenure of instructors at this location? What was the most recent tuition increase? Are class size caps strictly enforced? Who owns this specific location, and how reachable is the owner? What happens when my child's instructor leaves — what's the transition process? Does the corporate curriculum allow flexibility for my child's specific pace? These answers reveal whether the school prioritizes instructional quality or operational metrics, and whether local management has the authority to serve your child's individual needs.

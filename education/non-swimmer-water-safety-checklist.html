The Non-Swimmer Water Safety Checklist: A Free Printable for Families

A child who can’t swim yet is the child who most needs a plan. This free, printable Non-Swimmer Water Safety Checklist gives you the exact layers of protection — constant supervision, home barriers, the right life jacket, clear water rules, and the path to swim lessons — that keep a non-swimmer safe until their skills catch up.

🛟 Why does a non-swimmer need a different set of rules?

A child who can’t swim has no way to save themselves in the water, so every safety layer has to come from the adults and the environment around them — not from the child. A confident swimmer who slips underwater can float, turn, and reach the wall. A non-swimmer cannot. That single difference is why the rules for a child who hasn’t learned to swim yet are stricter, not looser — the protection has to be built entirely around them until their own skill catches up.

This matters because drowning is the leading cause of unintentional injury death for children ages 1 to 4 in the United States, according to the CDC’s Drowning Facts, and a leading cause for older children too. Drowning is also fast and silent — it rarely looks like the splashing and shouting people expect, which is why learning the real signs of drowning is one of the most useful things any parent of a non-swimmer can do. For the national data on how drowning risk shifts by age and setting, see our child drowning statistics dashboard.

The reassuring part: drowning among non-swimmers is highly preventable. It comes down to stacking a handful of layers so that if one fails, another still stands — the same layered approach the five layers of protection and the American Red Cross water safety guidance both recommend. This checklist walks you through each one.

👁️ What is the single most important layer for a non-swimmer?

Constant, undistracted supervision within arm’s reach — often called touch supervision — is the most important layer for any non-swimmer, because it is the only one that responds in real time. A barrier can be left open and a life jacket can be forgotten, but a focused adult standing close is what closes the gap in the seconds that matter. For a non-swimmer, that means touch supervision: close enough to reach out and grab your child at all times, not watching from a lounge chair.

When several adults are around — a pool party, a barbecue, a vacation rental — name one designated Water Watcher whose only job is watching the kids in the water, with no phone, no book, and no conversation, handed off in clear shifts. “Everyone’s watching” almost always means no one is. Our printable Water Watcher card makes that hand-off official so the responsibility never falls through the cracks.

🏡 How do I make my home safe for a child who can’t swim?

Put physical barriers between your non-swimmer and any water at home — a four-sided isolation fence around a pool, self-closing and self-latching gates, and alarms on doors that lead to water. Most young children who drown were not expected to be in the water at all; they slipped away during a gap in supervision. Barriers buy back those seconds. If you have a pool, the gold standard is a four-sided isolation fence at least four feet high that separates the pool from the house, with a self-closing, self-latching gate that opens outward. Our pool fence and gate inspection checklist shows how to test yours.

Water is everywhere else in a home, too. Empty and flip buckets, wading pools, and coolers; keep bathroom and laundry doors closed; and never leave a non-swimmer alone near a bathtub, even for a moment. If your child’s a toddler, our toddler home water safety checklist covers the room-by-room hazards that are easy to miss.

🦺 What kind of life jacket should a non-swimmer wear?

A non-swimmer should wear a properly fitted, U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket — never water wings, puddle jumpers, or inflatable toys, which are not safety devices. When a non-swimmer genuinely needs to be in or near open water, on a boat, or at a busy pool, a Coast Guard-approved life jacket is the only flotation you should trust. Check the label, match it to your child’s weight, and test the fit: it shouldn’t ride up over the chin or ears when you lift at the shoulders.

Skip the puddle jumpers and water wings. As our guide to floaties and puddle jumpers explains, they hold a child upright in a chin-up position — the opposite of a real swimming float — can slip off or deflate, and lull everyone into a false sense of security. A life jacket keeps a non-swimmer’s head above water; it does not replace the watching adult beside them. For choosing and sizing one, see our life jacket guide.

📋 What water rules should I teach a non-swimmer?

Teach a non-swimmer a few firm, non-negotiable rules: ask permission before going near water, always swim with an adult, stay in the shallow area, and no running or pushing near the pool. Rules give a child who can’t yet rescue themselves a script to follow. Keep them short and absolute: never go near water without a grown-up, always ask first, and stay where you can stand or where the adult says. Practice them out loud before every trip so they become automatic.

Older non-swimmers can learn one more habit that saves lives: if a friend is struggling in the water, reach or throw, don’t go. Well-meaning would-be rescuers who jump in are a common drowning story. Hand them something that floats and get an adult, rather than jumping in after them.

🏅 What finally removes the “non-swimmer” label?

Swim lessons are the only layer that removes the risk at its source — formal lessons can reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1 to 4, and swimming skill is the one protection that travels everywhere your child goes. Every other layer on this list buys time: a fence, a life jacket, a watching adult. Lessons change the equation, because a child who can float, turn, and reach the wall is no longer defenseless in the water.

According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, formal swim lessons reduce drowning risk by up to 88% for children ages 1 to 4. Quality, safety-first programs teach survival skills — back floating, rolling over to breathe, and reaching the side — long before pretty strokes, because those are exactly the skills a non-swimmer needs first. Frequently Asked Questions

How do I keep a child who can't swim safe around water?

Keep a non-swimmer safe by stacking several layers of protection so no single mistake becomes a tragedy. Provide constant, phone-free touch supervision within arm's reach any time your child is in or near water. Put barriers between your child and the water at home, such as a four-sided isolation fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate and door alarms. Use a Coast Guard-approved life jacket rather than water wings or puddle jumpers. Teach and enforce clear water rules. And enroll your child in formal swim lessons, which the American Academy of Pediatrics reports can reduce drowning risk by up to 88 percent for children ages 1 to 4. No layer is enough on its own, but together they keep a child safe until swimming skill catches up.

Are puddle jumpers and water wings safe for non-swimmers?

No. Puddle jumpers, water wings, and other inflatable toys are not safety devices. They hold a child upright in a vertical, chin-up position, which is the opposite of the horizontal float a swimmer needs, and they can teach a dangerous habit that works against real swimming. They can also slip off or deflate without warning, and they give both children and parents a false sense of security that leads to less supervision. For a non-swimmer who genuinely needs flotation, use a Coast Guard-approved life jacket that fits snugly and never as a substitute for a watching adult.

At what age does a child stop being a non-swimmer?

There is no age that automatically makes a child water-safe. A child stops being a non-swimmer when they can reliably perform water-competency skills on their own, such as stepping or falling into water over their head and returning to the surface, floating or treading for a period of time, turning around, and swimming to an exit and getting out. Many young children can begin building these skills through lessons well before they master them, and even a strong young swimmer still needs supervision. Treat swimming ability as a skill your child is building, not a box that gets checked at a certain birthday.

How do I keep my non-swimmer safe at a pool party or on vacation?

At a crowded pool party, on vacation, or anywhere with lots of adults around, assign one designated Water Watcher whose only job is watching the children in the water, with no phone and no conversation, handed off in shifts. Do not assume that because many adults are present someone is watching, because that is exactly when supervision falls through the cracks. Keep your non-swimmer in a properly fitted Coast Guard-approved life jacket, stay within arm's reach in the water, and know where the nearest phone and exit are. Point out the lifeguard if there is one, but never rely on a lifeguard in place of your own eyes.

When should a non-swimmer start swim lessons?

Most children are ready to begin swim lessons around age 1, and the American Academy of Pediatrics now supports lessons for many children starting at age 1 as a layer of drowning protection. The best time to start is now, whatever your child's age, because swimming skill is the only safety layer that travels with your child everywhere they go. A quality, safety-first program teaches survival skills first, such as floating on the back, rolling over to breathe, and reaching the wall, which are exactly the skills that protect a non-swimmer in an unexpected fall into water.

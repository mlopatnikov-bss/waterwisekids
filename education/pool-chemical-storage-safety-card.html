Where Do You Keep the Chlorine? Pool Chemical Safety in Four Moments

Almost everything we publish is about the water. This page is about the container — because a pool chemical injury does not happen to a child who is swimming. It happens on the deck, in the shed, and in the garage, and more than half of these injuries happen at somebody’s home. Four moments account for nearly all of them, and each one has a rule you can act on this weekend. Comes with a free printable shelf audit card.

Quick Summary: Pool chemical injuries cluster in four moments. The shelf: acid and chlorine stored apart, everything dry, closed, labelled, liquids low, locked — and note that a dry shed matters more than a nearby one, because dry sanitisers are built to react with water. The lid: the commonest injury is breathing fumes or dust while opening a container, so open outdoors, upwind, goggles and gloves on, one container at a time. The scoop: chemical goes into water, never water into chemical; never mix two products; one scoop per product. The re-entry: nobody in the pool while dosing, and nobody back in until the label’s wait has passed — adding chemicals just before somebody got in is one of the three commonest injury circumstances. Keep Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 where the chemicals are.

🏠 Why this is a home problem, not a pool company problem

Ask a family with a backyard pool where the chemicals are and you will usually get a vague gesture towards a shed. Ask what is on the shelf next to them and the answer gets vaguer. That gap is the subject of this page, and the numbers say it is not a small one.

CDC analysis of national emergency department data found an estimated 13,508 U.S. emergency department visits for pool chemical injuries across 2015 to 2017. More than a third of the patients — 36.4 percent — were under 18. At least 56.3 percent of the injuries happened at a residence rather than at a public pool. And roughly two thirds landed inside the summer swim season, between Memorial Day weekend and Labor Day. Nearly 94 percent of people were treated and released, and no deaths were documented, so this is not a category of catastrophe. It is a category of avoidable, painful, extremely common household injury that happens to be concentrated on children in the same twelve weeks their families are thinking hardest about the water.

The reason it deserves its own page is that everything else we publish about backyard pools works by adding a layer — another fence, another alarm, another trained adult, another taught skill. Chemical safety is the one place on this whole site where that instinct is backwards. Buying one more product and setting it down on the same shelf creates a hazard that neither product had on its own. Here, the inventory is the risk. Fewer products, stored further apart, is the safer shed.

If you want the underlying chemistry rather than the handling rules, our guide to pool chemistry basics for parents covers what each product is actually doing in the water, and pool chemical safety explained takes the whole subject from the swimmer’s side.

📦 Moment 1 — the shelf: what is stored next to what

Start here, because this is the one that can hurt somebody while nobody is in the room.

The CDC’s storage guidance is short and worth following line by line: separate incompatible chemicals, and acid and chlorine are the example it gives by name. Lock chemicals up to protect people and animals. Keep chemicals dry and do not mix different chemicals, including different types of chlorine product. Keep them cool, in a well-ventilated area away from direct sunlight. Keep them closed in their original, labelled containers. Store liquid chemicals low to prevent accidental contact.

Two of those deserve expanding, because they are the ones families get wrong for sensible-sounding reasons.

Separate places, not separate shelves. Acid and chlorine together produce chlorine gas, and a jug does not need anybody’s help to make that happen — it only has to leak, or tip, or split in the heat. A shelf above another shelf is a drip away from being the same shelf. If you have one cupboard, one of the two products needs to live somewhere else entirely.

Dry beats close. This is the genuinely counterintuitive one. Almost every other product in a garage is fine to get damp. The dry sanitisers on a pool shelf are engineered to react the instant they meet water — that is the entire point of them — so a leaking roof, a snow-melt line under a door, a hose coiled on the shelf above or a scoop put back wet is not untidiness, it is an ignition path. Which means the safest place for pool chemicals is the driest place you have, and that is almost never the place closest to the pool. Most families store them by the water because they think of them as a pool thing. Storage is a garage-and-shed question, and the pool has nothing to do with it.

While you are in there: keep chemicals away from fuel, the mower and barbecue gas, and make sure the lock is a real lock. A latch a four-year-old can work is not one, and a shelf a determined child can climb to is not out of reach. Our room-by-room home water safety checklist walks the same sweep through the rest of the house.

💨 Moment 2 — the lid: opening the container

If you take one thing from this page, take this: the dangerous part of the job is opening the container.

Injury report narratives point most often at people inhaling chemical fumes or dust, particularly while opening containers. Poisoning was the most common diagnosis in the CDC data, and about 90 percent of those poisonings came from breathing something in rather than swallowing it. The eye was the affected body part in about a third of all cases. Nobody expects that. Everybody expects the spill.

So slow the lid down. Open it outdoors, in moving air, standing upwind with your face turned away — never in a closed shed, a garage with the door down, or a pump room. Put safety goggles and gloves on before the lid moves, not after; goggles rather than sunglasses, and a mask if the label calls for one. Open one container at a time and close it again before the next one opens, because two open lids on one bench is how two products meet without anyone deciding to mix them.

And read the whole label before each use, not once when you bought it. The label is where the incompatibility list lives, along with the protective equipment, the dose and the wait time. The CDC puts label-reading at the front of both its storage and its handling guidance for exactly that reason. Products get reformulated, and what is sitting next to them on the shelf changes too.

Children go indoors for the whole job. Not “playing over there” — indoors, or with another adult. The second recurring circumstance in the injury narratives is pool chemicals that were simply not secured away from children.

🥣 Moment 3 — the scoop: measuring and adding

There is one rule here that people reverse, and reversing it is how a routine job becomes a violent one.

Chemical goes into water. Water never goes into chemical. The CDC states it plainly: only pre-dissolve a pool chemical when the product label directs it, and when the label does direct it, add the pool chemical to the water — never water to the pool chemical, because a violent and potentially explosive reaction can occur. If the label does not tell you to pre-dissolve, no bucket should be involved at all.

Around that sits the never-mix rule, which is broader than most people apply it. Never mix chlorine products with acid. Never mix different pool chemicals with each other or with any other substance — including two chlorine products that look similar, and including old and fresh stock of what is nominally the same thing. Mixing also happens by routes nobody would call mixing: a shared scoop carrying residue from the last job into the next one, a spill of one product swept into another, a bucket rinsed but not dried.

So: one scoop per product, labelled, dried, and stored with that product. Measure rather than eyeball — over-dosing is the usual cause of the stinging eyes and itchy skin that get blamed vaguely on “too much chlorine” later in the week. Add one product, then let the pump circulate before you add the next, so two doses meet in a pool’s worth of water rather than concentrated in the plumbing. And have the pump running, always: chemicals fed into still water sit wherever they land and then arrive all at once when the water moves.

For what the readings should look like once the dosing is done, our pool water quality checklist has the numbers to test for and the ones to ask about at a public pool.

🏊 Moment 4 — the re-entry: when the kids get back in

The third recurring circumstance in the injury narratives is the one parents are most likely to cause by accident: pool chemicals added to the water just before the injured person got in.

Nobody is in the water while you dose. Nobody gets back in until the wait on the label has passed, with the pump running. The wait varies by product, dose and pool volume, so the label is the authority and a kitchen timer is what makes the wait real when a child is standing there asking. Test the water before the first child gets in, rather than after somebody complains.

Then tell the other adults. Most re-entry injuries involve somebody who did not know a dose had just gone in — a partner, a grandparent, a neighbour’s child who let themselves through the gate. A sign on the gate for an afternoon costs nothing.

One more thing worth un-learning here. A strong chemical smell is a reason to stay out, not proof the water is clean. That sharp poolside smell mostly comes from chloramines, which form when chlorine combines with sweat, urine, sunscreen and dirt carried in by swimmers. They irritate eyes and airways and they eat the chlorine that should be killing germs. A well-run pool barely smells at all. Part of the fix belongs to the swimmers: a rinse shower before getting in, and bathroom breaks or diaper checks every hour, put far less of that material in the water to begin with — the same habits our guide to preventing recreational water illness asks for, and the ones public pool hygiene covers for pools you do not own. If the water has gone cloudy as well as smelly, cloudy water is a safety signal explains why that is a get-out rather than a cosmetic problem.

🗑️ The leftovers — containers, spills and the bucket nobody can identify

Every pool shed eventually acquires a container whose label has gone. It is the single most common finding when families run this audit, and it has a specific answer.

Never reuse a pool chemical container for anything — not another chemical, not water, not fuel, not storage — and follow the label for disposal. For anything unlabelled, the CDC points you at your local or state hazardous materials agency for the proper disposal procedure. It is a routine question for them and it is not a thing to use up or pour away. A container that is bulging, damp, crusted, warm or smelling wrong is an evacuate-and-call rather than a tidy-up: people out and upwind first, then the phone.

Respond to spills immediately, the way the label directs, with the door open and children well away. And never clean one product up by sweeping it into another.

Run the whole audit twice a year, at pool opening and at pool closing. Those two weekends are when most families move, open, buy and dose the most chemicals, and they bracket the season in which two thirds of these injuries occur. If you are setting a pool up for the first time, the shelf belongs in the commissioning list alongside the fence and the drain covers — our new pool owner water safety checklist puts them together, and what homeowners should know about pool liability explains why insurers ask about storage at all. Spas and hot tubs run on the same chemistry in a tenth of the water, which makes dosing errors proportionally bigger; the hot tub and spa safety checklist covers those.

🚨 If something goes wrong: two numbers

Decide this before you need it, and write both numbers on the card where the chemicals are. A number saved in one adult’s phone is not a plan for the adult who is standing there.

Call 911 if somebody is struggling to breathe, collapsed, unresponsive, or has chemical in their eyes and cannot open them. Get everyone out and upwind into fresh air first, and do not go back in for the container.

Call Poison Help on 1-800-222-1222 for everything else — a swallow, a splash, a lungful of fumes, or a “is this bad?” you are not sure about. It is free and confidential, staffed twenty-four hours a day by nurses, pharmacists and doctors, with interpretation available in many languages, and it connects you to your regional poison center. Most calls are resolved on the phone without a trip to hospital. Keep the product label to hand, because the first question will be what the product was.

Keeping those numbers with the rest of your emergency kit makes them easier to find in a hurry — our poolside emergency kit checklist has the full list of what lives at the water’s edge.

🏊 The honest limit of everything on this page

Worth saying plainly, because it would be easy to let this page feel like it is protecting your child in the pool.

It is not. Every rule here guards the child who is not in the water — the one who found an open bucket, climbed to a shelf, or walked through a cloud of dust off a lid. A locked shed does nothing at all for a child who is already in the pool. That child is protected by three things and none of them are on a shelf: a four-sided fence with a self-latching gate, an adult who is actually watching, and what the child can do when they end up in water they did not plan on — get to the surface, roll onto the back, float and breathe, turn, and reach an edge.

That is the layer that travels to the neighbour’s pool and the hotel and the lake, where your shed and your lock do not exist. Frequently Asked Questions

Where should pool chemicals be stored at home?

Somewhere cool, dry, well ventilated, out of direct sunlight, locked, and out of reach of children and pets — and with acid stored in a different place from chlorine products, not simply on a different shelf. Keep everything closed in its original labelled container, store liquids low so a split jug is a mop rather than a face injury, and keep chemicals away from fuel, the mower and the barbecue gas. The counterintuitive part is dryness: the dry sanitisers on your shelf are designed to react the moment they meet water, so a damp shed, a leaking roof or a wet scoop is a hazard rather than untidiness. That usually means the safest spot is not the one closest to the pool.

Can you mix different pool chemicals together?

No. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is unambiguous: never mix chlorine products with acid, because that can create toxic gases, and never mix different pool chemicals with each other or with any other substance. That includes two different types of chlorine product, and it includes old and fresh stock of what looks like the same product. Mixing also happens by slower routes people do not think of as mixing: a shared scoop that carries residue from the last job, two open containers on one bench, or sweeping a spill of one product into another. One container open at a time, one scoop per product, and nothing ever combined in a bucket.

Do you add chemicals to water or water to chemicals?

Chemical goes into water. Water never goes into chemical. Adding water to a concentrated pool chemical can cause a violent, potentially explosive reaction, which is why the direction matters more than it sounds. The CDC also says to pre-dissolve a pool chemical only when the product label directs it — otherwise the product goes into the pool the way the label says and a bucket never enters the picture at all. If the label does call for pre-dissolving, fill the container with water first, then add the chemical to it, slowly, outdoors, with goggles and gloves on and your face out of the rising vapour.

How long after adding chlorine can kids swim?

Follow the wait time on the product label, because it varies by product, dose and pool volume, and set a timer rather than trusting a guess. This matters more than most families realise: one of the three commonest circumstances in injury reports is a pool chemical being added to the water just before the person got in. Nobody should be in the pool while you are dosing, and nobody gets back in until the label’s wait has passed with the pump running. Tell the other adults the pool is closed and why, because most re-entry injuries involve somebody who did not know a dose had just gone in. Test the water before the first child gets in, not after somebody complains their eyes sting.

How common are pool chemical injuries?

More common than most pool owners expect, and they fall disproportionately on children. CDC analysis of national emergency department data found an estimated 13,508 U.S. emergency department visits for pool chemical injuries across 2015 to 2017, with 36.4 percent of patients under 18. At least 56.3 percent of injuries happened at a residence, and roughly two thirds occurred during the summer swim season between Memorial Day weekend and Labor Day. The most common diagnosis was poisoning, and about 90 percent of those poisonings came from inhalation rather than swallowing. Nearly 94 percent of patients were treated and released, and no deaths were documented in that period.

What is the most dangerous moment when handling pool chemicals?

Opening the container. Injury report narratives point most often at people inhaling chemical fumes or dust, particularly while opening containers — ahead of spills and well ahead of swallowing. The other two recurring circumstances are chemicals not being secured away from children, and chemicals being added to the water just before somebody got in. So the lid is the part of the job to slow down for: open it outdoors in moving air, upwind with your face turned away, with safety goggles and gloves already on, one container at a time, and with children indoors for the whole job.

Does a strong chlorine smell mean a pool is over-chlorinated?

Usually the opposite. The sharp smell people call “chlorine” mostly comes from chloramines, which form when chlorine combines with sweat, urine, sunscreen and dirt brought into the water. They are a by-product, they irritate eyes and airways, and they consume the chlorine that would otherwise be killing germs. A well-run pool barely smells at all. A strong smell is a reason to stay out and ask about the readings rather than reassurance that the water is clean, and the fix is partly on swimmers: a rinse shower before getting in, and bathroom breaks or diaper checks every hour, put far less of that material into the water.

What do you do if a child swallows or breathes in a pool chemical?

Get everyone out of the area and upwind into fresh air first, and do not go back in for the container. If the child is struggling to breathe, collapsed, unresponsive, or has chemical in their eyes and cannot open them, call 911. Otherwise call Poison Help on 1-800-222-1222 — free, confidential, staffed around the clock by nurses, pharmacists and doctors, with interpretation available in many languages. Have the product label with you when you call, because the first question will be what the product was. Most poisoning calls are resolved on the phone without a trip to hospital.

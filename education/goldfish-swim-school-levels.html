Goldfish Swim School Levels Explained: Mini, Junior, Glider and Pro

Your child came home as a “Junior 2” and you have no idea whether that is good. Here is every Goldfish Swim School level in order, the age and ratio attached to each one, the exact skills a child must show to move up, and how the three-star system actually works.

Quick Answer: Goldfish Swim School has 12 numbered levels in four named tiers — Mini 1–4 (4–35 months, parent in the water for Mini 1–2), Junior 1–3 (ages 3–4), Glider 1–3 (roughly ages 4/5–12) and Pro 1–2 (ages 4–12) — plus Swim Force, a swim-team-style program for ages 5–12. Children advance whenever they earn three stars on every skill in their current level, not at the end of a session, because lessons run year-round and perpetually.

What are the Goldfish Swim School levels, in order?

Goldfish Swim School levels run Mini 1, Mini 2, Mini 3, Mini 4, then Junior 1, Junior 2, Junior 3, then Glider 1, Glider 2, Glider 3, then Pro 1 and Pro 2 — twelve numbered steps grouped into four named tiers, with the Swim Force swim-team program sitting alongside the top of the ladder. How does a child move up a level at Goldfish?

Goldfish grades every individual skill in a level on a three-star scale, and a child moves up only when every skill in that level has three stars — which can happen in any week of the year, because lessons are perpetual rather than session-based. The scale is simple and worth memorizing, because it is the language every progress report uses:

  • One star — the child has been shown the skill and can do it with the instructor's help.
  • Two stars — the child can do it with very little help.
  • Three stars — the child does it independently and consistently, without prompting.

This is the single most useful thing to understand about the Goldfish ladder, and it is where most parent frustration comes from. In a session-based program — a six-week course at a community pool, for instance — your child is evaluated at the end and either passes or repeats. At Goldfish there is no end. Enrollment is continuous and year-round, so the only event that moves a child is the third star landing on the last outstanding skill.

The practical consequence: two children who started the same week can be two levels apart within a year, and neither of them is behind. Consistency drives it more than talent does. Children who miss weeks lose muscle memory on half-formed skills and re-earn stars they had already banked. If you want the mechanics of why progress stalls and what actually restarts it, we cover that in why swim lesson progress plateaus.

What happens in the Mini levels (4–35 months)?

Mini is the baby and toddler tier, ages 4 to 35 months, and it splits in half: a parent is in the water for Mini 1 and Mini 2 at a 6:1 ratio, and the child goes in alone for Mini 3 and Mini 4 at a much tighter 3:1 ratio. That handoff is the real milestone of the Mini tier, and it usually lands somewhere around age two.

Mini 1 (4–15 months) is not skill acquisition in any meaningful sense. It is acclimation: getting a baby comfortable with water on the face, with being moved through the water, with the noise and echo of a pool room. Nothing here is graded on distance. Mini 2 (16–35 months) introduces the first measurable skills — a three-foot Superman glide and a three-foot pulling-paddle swim with the face in the water.

Mini 3 and Mini 4 both cover 24–35 months, and the difference between them is independence. Mini 3 stretches the same glides and paddles to five feet and adds a safety wall hold. Mini 4 is the safety level: a five-second Sea Otter back float and two unassisted rollover safety skills. A child who has three-starred Mini 4 can, in controlled conditions, roll onto their back and breathe. That is a genuine competency — and it is also where a lot of parents quietly relax too much. It is not the same as being safe unsupervised. If your toddler is in this tier, our guide to rollover and survival float skills for toddlers explains exactly what a five-second float does and does not buy you.

Junior or Glider — which one does a 4-year-old belong in?

Junior is for 3- and 4-year-olds, Glider is for school-age children, and 4-year-olds legitimately appear in both — Goldfish lists Glider 2 and Glider 3 as starting at age 4 while Glider 1 is listed at 5–12. This overlap is the most confusing part of the chart, and it trips up parents transferring in from another program.

There is also a live inconsistency in Goldfish's own published material, worth knowing about before you argue with a front desk. The site navigation lists Glider as “4–12 years.” The Glider program page headline says “5–12-Year-Olds” and describes the tier that way, while the individual level cards on that same page list Glider 1 at 5–12 and Glider 2 and Glider 3 at 4–12. All of these are published by Goldfish. None is a typo you should read too much into.

The resolution is the one that applies at every good swim school: placement is by demonstrated skill, not by birthday. A 4-year-old who already swims a pool width with rollover breathing will be placed in Glider 2 regardless of which age range a web page shows. A nervous 6-year-old with no experience will start in a Glider 1 class that explicitly requires no prior aquatic experience — which is why Glider 1 and Junior 3 teach almost identical skills at different ages. Goldfish runs a placement evaluation for exactly this reason, and so does every school worth choosing. Our swim lesson level placement guide walks through how to read a placement decision that does not match your expectations.

What is the difference between Glider and Pro?

Glider takes a child from no experience to a pool width of side-breathing freestyle; Pro starts where Glider 3 ends and is defined by hard entry requirements rather than by teaching goals. Pro is the only tier where Goldfish publishes requirements instead of skills taught, which tells you what the tier is for: it is a gate, not a beginner's on-ramp.

Pro 1 will not take a child who cannot already swim 25 feet of simple freestyle with side breaths, 25 feet of simple backstroke, 25 feet of streamline kick on front and back, and tread water for 45 seconds unaided. Pro 2 raises every one of those: two minutes of continuous freestyle with side breaths, 50 feet of backstroke, breaststroke kick and dolphin kick for 25 feet each, and 60 seconds of treading.

Notice what those numbers actually describe. A child who has cleared Pro 2 can tread water for a minute and swim continuously for two. In a crowded pool or open water, that combination is the difference between a scare and an emergency — and it is roughly the threshold most aquatics educators use for meaningful water competence. What it is not is a guarantee: fatigue in cold or choppy water arrives far faster than in a 90-degree teaching pool, which we cover in how fast children tire in the water.

Beyond Pro sits Swim Force, for ages 5–12: swim-team-style training with endurance sets and race skills rather than lesson-style instruction. It is a parallel track, not a thirteenth rung — children generally join it once stroke mechanics are solid rather than after clearing a specific level.

How do Goldfish levels compare to the YMCA and Red Cross?

Goldfish's 12 levels are proprietary and do not map cleanly onto the YMCA's Stage 1–6 framework or the Red Cross Learn-to-Swim levels, but the underlying skill sequence is close enough that a good instructor can place a transferring child in one evaluation. The rough correspondences:

  • Mini 1–2 ≈ YMCA Swim Starters (Stage A/B) — parent-and-child water adjustment.
  • Mini 3–4 and Junior 1–2 ≈ YMCA Stage 1–2 (Water Acclimation, Water Movement); Red Cross Level 1–2.
  • Junior 3 and Glider 1–2 ≈ YMCA Stage 3–4 (Water Stamina, Stroke Introduction); Red Cross Level 3.
  • Glider 3 and Pro 1 ≈ YMCA Stage 5 (Stroke Development); Red Cross Level 4.
  • Pro 2 and Swim Force ≈ YMCA Stage 6 (Stroke Mechanics); Red Cross Level 5–6.

Treat those as translation starting points, not conversions. No official crosswalk exists between any two of these systems, because each organization writes its own curriculum. The one thing that does travel between schools is a written record of what your child can physically do — 25 feet of freestyle with side breaths means the same thing everywhere, while “Junior 2” means nothing outside a Goldfish building.

If you are switching programs, bring the progress report and ask the new school to place by skill. What should you ask before enrolling?

Ask which level your child was placed in and why, which specific skills are still outstanding, and what the current wait is for a class at that level and time. The last one matters more than parents expect — busy locations run waitlists for weekday-evening and weekend slots, and a level placement you cannot actually attend is not a placement.

Four more worth asking anywhere, not just at Goldfish:

  • “Can I see the star sheet?” A program that grades skill by skill should be able to show you which skills sit at one, two and three stars. If nobody can produce it, the system is not being used.
  • “What is the make-up policy?” In perpetual year-round programs you pay continuously; missed weeks are lost skill practice, and policies vary by location.
  • “Who is teaching, and what are they certified in?” Instructor certification varies widely across the industry — see what ASCA certification levels actually mean.
  • “Is there a sibling or family rate?” Multi-child pricing is common and rarely advertised; we cover the usual structures in swim school sibling discounts.

One last framing point, because it gets lost in level charts. A level is a curriculum label, not a safety status. The CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics both frame swim lessons as one layer among several — alongside four-sided pool fencing, close and undistracted supervision, life jackets in open water, and an adult who knows CPR. Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Goldfish Swim School levels in order?

In order: Mini 1, Mini 2, Mini 3, Mini 4 (ages 4–35 months), Junior 1, Junior 2, Junior 3 (ages 3–4), Glider 1, Glider 2, Glider 3 (ages 4–12), then Pro 1 and Pro 2 (ages 4–12). Swim Force, the swim-team-style program for ages 5–12, sits alongside the top of the ladder rather than inside it.

How does a child move up a level at Goldfish Swim School?

Goldfish uses a 3-star system for each skill in a level. One star means the child can do the skill with help, two stars means with little help, three stars means independently and consistently. Once a child has three stars on every skill in their level, they move up — whenever that happens, not at the end of a session.

What age is Glider at Goldfish Swim School?

Goldfish lists Glider as 4–12 years in its site navigation, while the Glider program page describes it as 5–12 years and lists Glider 1 at 5–12 with Glider 2 and Glider 3 at 4–12. In practice placement is by skill, not birthday, so a strong 4-year-old can sit in Glider and a nervous 6-year-old can start in Junior.

What is the difference between Glider and Pro at Goldfish?

Glider is beginner-to-intermediate: unassisted freestyle across a pool width, side breathing, back glide, treading up to 45 seconds. Pro is intermediate-to-advanced and starts where Glider 3 ends — Pro 1 requires 25 feet of freestyle with side breaths, 25 feet of backstroke and 45 seconds of treading before a child can enroll.

How long does each Goldfish level take?

There is no fixed length. Goldfish runs perpetual year-round lessons rather than fixed sessions, so children advance when they earn three stars on every skill. Most families should expect several months per level, and plateaus are normal — a child working on a complex skill can sit in one level for a long stretch without anything being wrong.
